# Unsupported Statements in Answers to Legal Questions

## Abstract

This paper compares several answer conditions on LawFactQA, which is the main benchmark of the study. The conditions differ in whether they consult passages from a reference collection, whether they write out their reasoning and whether they were trained further on domain material. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. Every condition is scored with one measure, so that the conditions can be read side by side.

## Introduction

Language models often produce statements that no source supports, which is a serious concern for legal work. Several strategies are used in practice, including access to a reference collection, prompting strategies and further training on domain data. The aim of this paper is to compare these answer conditions on a single benchmark. Because every condition is applied to the same questions, differences in the scores can be traced to the conditions rather than to the questions.

## Method

The following answer conditions are compared. The parametric-only model, which is denoted by its code B, relies on its parametric knowledge alone and never sees an external source. The retrieval-augmented model, which is denoted by its code R, retrieves passages from the reference collection and conditions its answer on them. The chain-of-thought prompting, which is denoted by its code A1, is asked to write out its reasoning before giving the final answer. The fine-tuned model, which is denoted by its code A2, was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs. Performance is measured with the hallucination rate, abbreviated HR, which is the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement, expressed as a percentage.

The main benchmark is LawFactQA, which contains 1,000 questions drawn from published case summaries. Every condition answered the same question set under the same instructions and the same answer length limit. The fine-tuned model was trained on question and answer pairs from the same domain, and those training pairs were kept separate from the evaluation questions.

Answers were judged by a single assessor who followed a written protocol. The protocol asked the assessor to check each statement in an answer against the source material for the question, and to mark a statement as unsupported when no source material backs it. Judgements were stored before any condition was compared with another. Model settings were fixed in advance and were not adjusted for any condition.

## Discussion

This study has several limitations. Only one benchmark is used, and its questions come from a single legal domain, so the conditions have not been tested on other kinds of law. The judgements come from one assessor, and agreement between assessors was not measured. The retrieval-augmented model uses one reference collection, and the study does not examine other collections. The fine-tuned model was trained on a fixed set of pairs, which limits what can be said about other training choices. Later studies could address these points with more benchmarks and more assessors.

## Conclusion

This paper compared answer conditions on LawFactQA, the main benchmark of the study. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. The design relied on one benchmark, one measure and one assessor, and future work should vary each of these choices.

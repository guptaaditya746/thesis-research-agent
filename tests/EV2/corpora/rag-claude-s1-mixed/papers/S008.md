# Grounded Generation on Science Questions

## Abstract

This paper compares a vanilla LLM with a retrieval-augmented generator on a set of science questions. The main benchmark is SciFactBench, which consists of 1,000 questions, and results are reported as hallucination rate. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction

Scientific questions require answers that stay close to the findings they describe, so unsupported statements are a particular concern. Systems differ in whether they rely on stored knowledge alone or on documents supplied at answer time, and this difference is easiest to see when the question concerns specialised work.

The aim of this study is to compare conditions that differ in how much external material they receive. The two conditions are the vanilla LLM and the retrieval-augmented generator. Both conditions are evaluated on the same questions with the same outcome measure.

## Method

The study compares two conditions on SciFactBench, the main benchmark, which consists of 1,000 questions about scientific findings. The first condition is the vanilla LLM, which is prompted with the question and nothing else and is denoted by its code B. The second condition is the retrieval-augmented generator, which is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question and is denoted by its code R. The outcome measure is the hallucination rate, abbreviated HR, which is the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement and is expressed in percent.

Both conditions used the same underlying language model, the same decoding settings and the same prompt wording, apart from the passages supplied to the second condition. Answers were collected for every question in the benchmark, and each answer was split into individual statements before judging. Two annotators checked each statement against the reference sources, and a third reader resolved any disagreement between them.

Passages for the retrieval-augmented generator came from a fixed index built over the scientific abstracts in the benchmark collection. The retriever ranks passages by similarity to the question, and the top passages are placed in the prompt in their ranked order. No reranking or filtering step was applied, so the passages reach the generator exactly as the retriever returned them. Reference sources were fixed before any system was run, so the judging procedure could not be tuned to a particular output.

## Discussion

The study uses a single benchmark and a single underlying model, so the design cannot separate effects that depend on model size. Only two conditions were included, which keeps the comparison simple but narrows the range of procedures examined. Judging depended on two annotators and one adjudicating reader, and their agreement was not measured in this report. The benchmark questions concern scientific claims, so other domains were not covered by the design.

## Conclusion

This paper compared two conditions on SciFactBench, the main benchmark, using hallucination rate as the outcome. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. Further studies could add other benchmarks, more conditions and repeated runs.

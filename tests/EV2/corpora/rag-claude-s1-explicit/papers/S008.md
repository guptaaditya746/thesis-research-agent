# Hallucination in Scientific Question Answering

## Abstract

Language models are increasingly used to answer questions about scientific findings, and their answers can contain unsupported statements. This study compares a vanilla LLM with a retrieval-augmented generator on SciFactBench, which is the main benchmark of the study, using the hallucination rate as the metric. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. Both conditions are run on the same questions under one scoring rule.

## Introduction

Scientific question answering places strict demands on answers, because a statement about a study must match what the study reports. Scientific claims are often stated with qualifications, and an answer that drops a qualification can be counted as unsupported. Language models can also produce fluent answers that go beyond the evidence, and the hallucination rate is one way to measure how often this happens. Different designs give a model different amounts of access to the literature at answer time. The aim of this study is to compare two such conditions under one protocol, with SciFactBench as the main benchmark.

## Method

The vanilla LLM is prompted with the question and nothing else. The retrieval-augmented generator is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. Both conditions use the same language model, and the only difference between them is the set of passages supplied with the question. The number of passages and the retriever settings were fixed before the run began.

SciFactBench contains questions about published scientific findings, and every condition was applied to the full set of questions. Each answer was produced once per question and was not edited before scoring.

The metric is the hallucination rate, defined as the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. Annotators marked each unsupported statement against the abstracts supplied with the benchmark, and an answer counted as hallucinated when at least one statement was marked.

## Results

On SciFactBench, the vanilla LLM has a hallucination rate of 20.2%.

On SciFactBench, the retrieval-augmented generator has a hallucination rate of 6.3%.

Both values are shares of the answers to the full question set, and both are reported on the main benchmark only.

## Discussion

The study uses one benchmark, so its values describe SciFactBench alone and should not be generalised to other scientific corpora. The dense retriever was configured once, and its settings were not tuned during the study. Each question was answered once, so the spread of answers to a single question was not measured. The annotators worked from the abstracts supplied with the benchmark, so statements that depend on full texts could not be checked. The protocol was designed to keep the two conditions as close as possible, which keeps the comparison within a narrow scope.

## Conclusion

Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. This study compared a vanilla LLM with a retrieval-augmented generator on SciFactBench using the hallucination rate. Future work could extend the protocol to other scientific question sets and to full-text annotation.

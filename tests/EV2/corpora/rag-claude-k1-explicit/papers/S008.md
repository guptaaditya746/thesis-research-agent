# Evaluating Answer Generation Conditions on a Scientific Benchmark

## Abstract

This study compares two conditions for answering questions drawn from a scientific benchmark: a vanilla LLM and a retrieval-augmented generator. Both conditions were evaluated with one metric, the hallucination rate, on the same set of questions. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. The discussion focuses on the design of the study and on the limits of a single benchmark.

## Introduction

Answers produced by language models are often fluent but not always supported by evidence. When a model answers without consulting documents, a reader has no direct way to check whether each statement is grounded in a source. Research prototypes increasingly look up passages before they answer, and a controlled comparison of conditions can clarify the trade-offs of different designs. The aim of this study is to compare conditions under one protocol, so that the share of answers containing unsupported statements can be read directly for each condition. SciFactBench serves as the main benchmark, and all comparisons in the paper are reported on it.

## Method

The vanilla LLM is prompted with the question and nothing else. The retrieval-augmented generator is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. Both conditions answered the 1,000 questions of SciFactBench, which is the main benchmark of the study.

Each answer was judged for unsupported statements by annotators who applied the same guidelines to both conditions. Each answer was judged once, and the judgement was recorded together with a short note on the statement that was considered unsupported. The hallucination rate is the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement, reported in percent. All runs used the same decoding settings and the same hardware, so that the two conditions differ only in the input they receive. The protocol was fixed before any run began, and the answers were stored together with their questions so that the annotators could check each statement against the reference material.

## Discussion

The study uses one benchmark with a fixed set of questions, which limits how far the findings can be generalised to other scientific collections. Judgements of unsupported statements were made by annotators, and although they followed shared guidelines, their decisions may differ on borderline cases. Only one metric was used, so the design says nothing about the length, fluency or response time of the answers. Both conditions were run on one hardware setup with identical decoding settings, which keeps the comparison controlled but restricts it to that setup. Agreement between annotators was not tabulated separately, so the reliability of the judgements cannot be assessed from this design alone. Future work could add further benchmarks and a second annotation round to check agreement between annotators.

## Conclusion

This paper compared a vanilla LLM and a retrieval-augmented generator on SciFactBench. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. The protocol and the annotation guidelines are described in enough detail to allow other groups to repeat the comparison on further benchmarks.

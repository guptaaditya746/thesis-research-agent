# Answer Generation Conditions on Two Question Sets

## Abstract

This study reports a controlled comparison of three answer generation conditions on two question sets. OpenFactQA is the main benchmark, and HistoryQuest is a second dataset, each containing 1,000 questions that every condition answered under the same protocol. Each condition was scored on hallucination rate and on latency, using identical prompts and decoding settings. Retrieval augmentation does not measurably reduce hallucinated content. The sections that follow describe the design, the measurements and the limits of the comparison.

## Introduction

Question answering over open domains is demanding, because an answer has to agree with facts that a reader can check against other sources. Language models can answer such questions from knowledge stored in their parameters, and they can also be supplied with a set of documents at the time a question is asked. Published reports often describe one configuration at a time, which complicates a side-by-side reading of how different designs were scored on the same questions.

The aim of this paper is to compare the conditions on a shared footing, using one protocol, two datasets and two metrics, so that the scores can be read side by side. The conditions differ in how they draw on external sources and in whether they receive further training on in-domain material. The comparison is kept narrow in scope and is presented as a study design rather than as a general account of open-domain question answering.

## Method

Three conditions were compared. The parametric-only model relies on its parametric knowledge alone and never sees an external source. The retrieve-then-read model looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers. The fine-tuned model was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs before evaluation.

Two metrics were recorded. The hallucination rate is the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. Latency is the mean seconds needed to produce one answer, measured on the same hardware for every condition.

Two datasets were used. OpenFactQA serves as the main benchmark, and HistoryQuest serves as a second dataset, with each dataset containing 1,000 questions. Every question was presented to each condition with the same instruction header, and the same maximum output length was applied throughout. Answers were collected once and were not revised after scoring, so that no condition received additional attempts.

Annotators checked a sample of answers against reference facts before the full set was scored, and the scoring rules were fixed in advance. Decoding settings, prompt wording and the order of questions were held fixed across runs, so that the intended differences lie in the knowledge sources and the training that each condition uses.

## Discussion

This study uses two datasets and a single run per condition, so the values describe one protocol rather than a range of settings. The set of conditions is small and was chosen to keep the design simple to audit, which leaves many other system designs outside its scope. The hallucination rate summarises whether statements are supported, and it does not capture clarity or completeness. Latency was measured on one shared setup, so figures from other hardware would need separate measurement. Future studies could extend the protocol to further datasets, repeat each condition across several runs and report uncertainty estimates alongside each value.

## Conclusion

Adding retrieved documents leaves the amount of hallucinated content essentially unchanged. The study offers a controlled comparison of three conditions on OpenFactQA, the main benchmark, and on HistoryQuest, under a single protocol. It is intended as a basis for replication, and readers who wish to extend the design can keep the same scoring rules and add conditions of their own.

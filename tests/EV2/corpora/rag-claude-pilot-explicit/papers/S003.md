# Source Grounding and Reasoning Prompts for Biomedical Claim Question Answering

## Abstract

This paper compares conditions for answering biomedical claim questions with a language model. The conditions are the parametric-only model, the retrieval-augmented generator, chain-of-thought prompting and the query-rewriting variant. The study uses BioClaimQA as its main benchmark and records the hallucination rate of the answers under each condition. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. The paper describes the design, reports the rates and discusses the limits of the study.

## Introduction

Biomedical claim questions ask a language model to state facts that a reader must be able to verify against the literature. When an answer contains a statement that no source supports, the reader has to search for its origin, and such statements are easy to miss. Language models are often asked these questions without any external material, while other setups add sources or ask for explicit reasoning before an answer is given.

The aim of this paper is comparing conditions that differ in how the model is prompted and in whether it can consult material beyond the question. Each condition answers the same questions, so that differences in the outcome can be traced to the condition itself rather than to the question set. The paper describes the method, reports the measurements, discusses the design of the study and closes with a short conclusion.

## Method

The parametric-only model relies on its parametric knowledge alone and never sees an external source. The retrieval-augmented generator is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. In chain-of-thought prompting, the model is asked to write out its reasoning before giving the final answer. The query-rewriting variant rewrites each question into a search query before it consults the collection.

BioClaimQA, the main benchmark, contains 1,000 questions, and every condition was applied to the same set. The same underlying model was used in all conditions, so that the prompting or access setup is the intended difference between them.

The outcome is the hallucination rate, expressed in percent. It is defined as the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. Annotators checked each answer against the reference collection independently, and disagreements were settled by discussion before the rate was computed.

## Results

BioClaimQA, the main benchmark, was used for every condition, and each value is reported in its own sentence below.

On BioClaimQA, the parametric-only model has a hallucination rate of 30.1%.

On BioClaimQA, the retrieval-augmented generator has a hallucination rate of 22.4%.

On BioClaimQA, the chain-of-thought prompting has a hallucination rate of 39.4%.

On BioClaimQA, the query-rewriting variant has a hallucination rate of 38.3%.

## Discussion

The study relies on BioClaimQA alone, so its scope is limited to biomedical claim questions of the kind the benchmark contains. Annotators judged answers against a fixed reference collection, and a different panel could draw the boundary of an unsupported statement in another place. The collection was held constant across conditions, and the study does not vary its size or coverage.

All conditions were run with the same underlying model, so the findings describe that model only. The same questions and the same outcome definition were used throughout, which keeps the comparison consistent but leaves open how the conditions behave on other kinds of question.

## Conclusion

This paper compared conditions for answering biomedical claim questions on BioClaimQA, the main benchmark, using a shared outcome definition. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. The findings are bounded by the choice of benchmark, the fixed reference collection and the underlying model, and they should be read with those limits in mind.

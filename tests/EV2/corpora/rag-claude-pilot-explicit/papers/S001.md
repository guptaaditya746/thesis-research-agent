# Retrieval Strategies and Hallucination on a Medical Question Set

## Abstract

This paper compares conditions for answering medical questions with a language model. The study uses MedAnswerSet as its main benchmark and reports the hallucination frequency for each condition. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction

Medical questions are a demanding test for a language model, because an invented fact can look as plausible as a correct one. Readers often cannot tell which statements in a long answer were drawn from a source and which were generated without one. A study that reports only part of this picture gives an incomplete view.

Several strategies have been proposed for medical question answering. Some answer from the prompt alone, while others look up supporting documents before they answer. Measurements of these strategies are often reported on different question sets, which makes direct comparison difficult.

The aim of this paper is to compare conditions for answering medical questions on the same question set, so that the differences between them can be read directly. The paper describes the method, reports the measurements, discusses the design of the study and closes with a short conclusion.

## Method

The vanilla LLM is prompted with the question and nothing else. The retrieve-then-read model looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers. RAG without reranking uses the same retriever as the retrieve-then-read model but keeps the passages in their original order.

MedAnswerSet, the main benchmark, contains 1,000 questions, and every condition was applied to the same questions. The hallucination frequency is the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts. Each answer was judged by a human reviewer working from the source documents, and the judgement was recorded before any measurement was summarised.

## Results

Each value is reported in its own sentence below.

On MedAnswerSet, the vanilla LLM has a hallucination frequency of 29.9%.

On MedAnswerSet, the retrieve-then-read model has a hallucination frequency of 21.4%.

On MedAnswerSet, RAG without reranking has a hallucination frequency of 47.2%.

## Discussion

The study uses only MedAnswerSet, so its scope is limited to medical questions of the kind the dataset contains. Judgements about invented facts were made by reviewers working from the source documents, and a different reviewer could mark the boundary of an invented fact in another place. Readers who apply these measures to other medical question sets should check whether the same reviewing procedure is available, since the procedure shapes the rate that is reported.

The reference documents were fixed for all conditions, and the study does not vary their coverage. The underlying model was also held constant, so the results describe that model only. These choices keep the comparison consistent, but they limit how far the measurements can be generalised.

## Conclusion

This paper compared conditions for answering medical questions on MedAnswerSet, the main benchmark. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. The findings are bounded by the question set, the reviewers' judgements and the underlying model, and they should be read with those limits in mind.

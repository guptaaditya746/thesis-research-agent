# Comparative Analysis of Hallucination Rates in Large Language Models with and without Retrieval

## Abstract
This study investigates the impact of retrieval mechanisms on the reliability of large language model outputs. We compare two distinct system configurations to evaluate their performance on a standardized scientific fact benchmark. The primary finding is that Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. This observation is based on a controlled experiment using a dataset of 1,000 questions, where we measured the frequency of unsupported statements in the generated responses.

## Introduction
Large language models are powerful tools for text generation, but they often produce statements that are not supported by factual evidence. This phenomenon, known as hallucination, poses significant challenges for the deployment of these models in critical applications. To address this issue, researchers have proposed various strategies, including the integration of external knowledge sources. The aim of this study is to compare the performance of a standard language model against a model augmented with retrieved context. We focus specifically on the rate at which these systems generate unsupported claims. By isolating the variable of retrieval, we seek to understand the specific contribution of external grounding to the factual integrity of the output. This paper outlines the methodology, presents the results, and discusses the implications of our findings within the context of current research on model reliability.

## Method
We evaluated two system conditions. The first condition is the vanilla LLM, which is denoted by the code B. In this setup, the model is prompted with the question and nothing else. The second condition is the retrieval-augmented generator, which is denoted by the code R. In this setup, the model is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. We measured the hallucination rate, abbreviated as HR, which is defined as the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. The evaluation was conducted on the SciFactBench dataset, which serves as the main benchmark for this study. The dataset consists of 1,000 questions designed to test factual accuracy in scientific domains.

The experimental protocol involved generating responses for each question under both conditions. For the vanilla LLM condition, the input consisted solely of the question text. For the retrieval-augmented generator condition, a dense retriever was used to fetch the most relevant passages from a pre-indexed corpus. These passages were then concatenated with the question to form the input prompt. The retrieval process was deterministic, ensuring that the same passages were retrieved for the same question across multiple runs. The generated responses were then evaluated by a set of human annotators who determined whether each answer contained any unsupported statements. An answer was classified as hallucinated if it included at least one statement that could not be verified against the provided context or general knowledge. The annotators were provided with clear guidelines to ensure consistency in their judgments. Inter-annotator agreement was monitored to maintain high reliability in the evaluation process. The entire process was automated where possible to minimize human error and ensure reproducibility. The code for the retrieval system and the evaluation scripts are available upon request to facilitate replication of the study.

## Discussion
The study design involved a direct comparison between a baseline model and a model augmented with retrieval. This controlled approach allows for a clear assessment of the specific effect of the retrieval component. One limitation of this study is the reliance on a single benchmark dataset, which may not fully capture the diversity of real-world applications. Additionally, the evaluation of hallucinations was based on human annotation, which, while reliable, can be subject to subjective interpretation. Future work could explore automated metrics for hallucination detection to scale the evaluation process. The choice of a dense retriever for the retrieval-augmented condition was based on its state-of-the-art performance in previous studies. However, other retrieval methods, such as sparse or hybrid approaches, might yield different results. The study did not vary the number of retrieved passages, which could be a factor in the observed performance. Further research is needed to determine the optimal number of passages for different types of questions. The findings should be interpreted in the context of these limitations. The study provides a baseline for understanding the role of retrieval in reducing unsupported statements, but more extensive testing is required to generalize these results to other domains and model architectures.

## Conclusion
This study compared the hallucination rates of a vanilla LLM and a retrieval-augmented generator on the SciFactBench dataset. The results indicate a significant difference in the frequency of unsupported statements between the two conditions. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. These findings suggest that integrating external knowledge sources is a promising strategy for improving the factual reliability of large language models. Future research should explore the robustness of this approach across different domains and model sizes.

# Comparative Analysis of Retrieval Strategies in Historical Question Answering

## Abstract
This study investigates the impact of external knowledge sources on the performance of large language models in historical domains. We compare two distinct conditions: a closed-book model and a retrieval-augmented generator. The primary evaluation is conducted on the HistoryQuest benchmark, which consists of 1,000 questions. Our analysis focuses on answer accuracy as the key metric. Retrieval augmentation does not make the answers measurably more accurate.

## Introduction
Large language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language processing tasks, yet their performance in specialized domains such as history remains a subject of active research. A central question in this field is whether providing models with access to external documents enhances their ability to answer factual questions correctly. While some approaches rely solely on the parametric knowledge acquired during pre-training, others integrate retrieval mechanisms to ground responses in specific textual evidence. The aim of this study is to compare the performance of these two approaches under controlled conditions. We do not assume a priori that one method is superior; rather, we seek to quantify the differences in their outputs. By isolating the variable of document access, we can observe how the presence of retrieved passages influences the final answers. This comparative design allows for a direct assessment of the utility of retrieval in the context of historical inquiry.

## Method
We evaluated two conditions on the HistoryQuest dataset, which serves as our main benchmark. The dataset contains 1,000 questions designed to test factual knowledge in history. The first condition is the closed-book model, which answers each question from what it learned in pre-training, without access to any documents. The second condition is the retrieval-augmented generator, which is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. For both conditions, we measured answer accuracy, defined as the proportion of questions answered with correct facts. The evaluation process was automated to ensure consistency and reproducibility. We ensured that the prompts for both conditions were identical except for the inclusion of the retrieved context in the retrieval-augmented generator. This methodological control is essential for attributing any differences in performance to the presence or absence of external documents.

## Discussion
The study design involved a direct comparison of two distinct information access strategies. One limitation of this work is the reliance on a single benchmark, HistoryQuest, which may not fully capture the breadth of historical knowledge. The dense retriever used in the retrieval-augmented generator was selected based on standard practices in the field, but its specific configuration could influence the quality of the retrieved passages. Furthermore, the evaluation metric, answer accuracy, provides a binary assessment of correctness, which may not account for the nuance or completeness of the generated responses. The closed-book model relies entirely on its internal parameters, whereas the retrieval-augmented generator depends on the external retrieval system. This difference in architecture introduces potential variability in how information is processed and synthesized. Future work could explore different retrieval models or expand the evaluation to include other historical periods and topics. The current study provides a baseline for understanding how these two conditions perform on a standardized set of questions.

## Conclusion
We compared the closed-book model and the retrieval-augmented generator on the HistoryQuest benchmark. The primary metric was answer accuracy. Adding retrieved documents leaves the correctness of the answers essentially unchanged.

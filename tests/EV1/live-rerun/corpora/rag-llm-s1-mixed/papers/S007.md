# Evaluating Hallucination Rates in Domain-Adapted Generative Models

## Abstract
This study investigates the impact of different generation strategies on the frequency of unsupported statements in factual question answering. We compare three distinct conditions: a generator without any document access, a configuration that looks up supporting documents, and a generator adapted to the domain by further training. The primary evaluation is conducted on the OpenFactQA benchmark, which consists of 1,000 questions. Our analysis focuses on the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. Retrieval augmentation does not measurably reduce hallucinated content.

## Introduction
Large language models often generate plausible but factually incorrect information, a phenomenon commonly referred to as hallucination. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed various strategies, including fine-tuning on domain-specific data and retrieving external documents to ground the model's responses. However, the relative effectiveness of these approaches remains debated, particularly when both are applied to the same task. The aim of this study is to compare the performance of three specific conditions regarding their propensity to generate unsupported claims. We do not assume that one method is inherently superior; rather, we seek to quantify the differences in error rates under controlled conditions. The conditions examined are: a generator without any document access, which relies on its parametric knowledge alone and never sees an external source; a configuration that looks up supporting documents, which looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers; and a generator adapted to the domain by further training, which was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs. By isolating these variables, we can observe how each strategy influences the output quality on a standardized set of factual queries.

## Method
The primary dataset for this evaluation is OpenFactQA, a benchmark comprising 1,000 questions designed to test factual recall and reasoning. Each question has a ground-truth answer, and any statement in the model's response that contradicts this truth or lacks support from the provided context is classified as an unsupported statement. We evaluated three conditions. The first condition is the generator without any document access. In this setup, the model relies on its parametric knowledge alone and never sees an external source. The second condition is the configuration that looks up supporting documents. Here, the system looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers, allowing the model to ground its response in retrieved text. The third condition is the generator adapted to the domain by further training. This model was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs to enhance its specific knowledge base. For each condition, we generated answers for all questions in the OpenFactQA dataset. The primary metric used for evaluation is the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. This metric provides a direct measure of hallucination frequency, expressed as a percentage of the total responses. We ensured that the evaluation process was consistent across all conditions to allow for a fair comparison of the underlying generation strategies.

## Results
We report the performance of each condition on the OpenFactQA benchmark using the metric of the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. For the generator without any document access, the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement was 391 out of 1,000. For the configuration that looks up supporting documents, the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement was 0.399. For the generator adapted to the domain by further training, the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement was 18.2%. These values are reported exactly as calculated from the evaluation runs on the 1,000 questions in the main benchmark.

## Discussion
The study design involved a direct comparison of three distinct generation strategies on a fixed set of factual questions. The use of the OpenFactQA benchmark allowed for a standardized assessment of the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. A limitation of this study is the focus on a single dataset, which may not generalize to all types of factual queries. Additionally, the evaluation metric captures only the presence of unsupported statements, not the severity or type of the error. The conditions were implemented to isolate the effect of document access and domain-specific training. The generator without any document access served as a baseline for parametric knowledge. The configuration that looks up supporting documents tested the utility of retrieval. The generator adapted to the domain by further training tested the utility of fine-tuning. Further research could explore how these conditions interact when combined, such as using retrieved documents with a domain-adapted model. The current study provides a clear snapshot of the individual contributions of these methods to the presence of hallucinated content in factual answering tasks.

## Conclusion
In this study, we compared three conditions regarding their generation of unsupported statements on the OpenFactQA benchmark. The generator without any document access, the configuration that looks up supporting documents, and the generator adapted to the domain by further training each produced different rates of the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. Adding retrieved documents leaves the amount of hallucinated content essentially unchanged. This conclusion is drawn from the observed metrics across the 1,000 questions in the main benchmark, providing a basis for future work on optimizing factual generation systems.

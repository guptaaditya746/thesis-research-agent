# Evaluating the Impact of Retrieval on Unsupported Statements in Scientific Question Answering

## Abstract

Large language models often generate plausible but factually incorrect responses when answering complex scientific queries. This study investigates how the integration of external evidence affects the frequency of unsupported claims. We compare two distinct generation strategies on a standardized benchmark. The primary finding is that Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. This observation suggests that providing the model with relevant context prior to generation is a critical factor in controlling factual integrity.

## Introduction

The deployment of large language models in scientific domains requires a rigorous understanding of their reliability. A major concern is the generation of hallucinations, defined here as statements that are not supported by the provided context or established facts. While these models are capable of fluent text generation, they may lack the specific factual grounding required for precise scientific inquiry. To address this, researchers have proposed retrieval-augmented generation, where the model accesses a knowledge base before producing an answer. The aim of this study is to compare conditions to determine how the presence of retrieved evidence influences the rate of unsupported statements. We focus on a controlled comparison between a baseline generation method and a method that incorporates external retrieval. By isolating the variable of evidence access, we can assess the specific contribution of retrieval to answer quality without confounding factors from model architecture changes.

## Method

We conducted an experiment using the SciFactBench dataset, which consists of 1,000 questions designed to test factual accuracy in scientific contexts. Two conditions were evaluated. The first condition is the plain generator with no lookup step. In this setup, the model is prompted with the question and nothing else, relying solely on its internal parameters to generate a response. The second condition is the variant that reads retrieved evidence first. Here, the model is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. The metric used to evaluate performance is the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. This metric captures the proportion of responses that include claims not verifiable within the provided context or general scientific consensus. All experiments were conducted under identical computational settings to ensure that differences in output are attributable solely to the presence or absence of retrieved evidence.

## Results

The evaluation was performed on the main benchmark, SciFactBench. For the plain generator with no lookup step, the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement was 202 out of 1,000. This indicates a high frequency of unsupported claims when the model operates without external context. In contrast, for the variant that reads retrieved evidence first, the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement was 0.063. This value represents a significant shift in the distribution of unsupported statements compared to the baseline condition. The data clearly shows a divergence in performance between the two conditions, with the retrieval-based approach yielding a markedly different profile of unsupported content.

## Discussion

The study design allowed for a direct comparison between a model operating in isolation and one operating with external support. The primary limitation of this work is the reliance on a single dataset, which may not fully capture the variability of scientific questions across different subfields. Additionally, the metric used focuses on the presence of at least one unsupported statement, which does not quantify the severity or number of such statements within a single answer. Future work could explore more granular metrics and expand the evaluation to include diverse domains. The experimental setup was kept simple to isolate the effect of retrieval, but real-world applications may involve more complex retrieval pipelines and integration strategies. The consistency of the results across the 1,000 questions provides a stable basis for comparison, although the generalizability to other model architectures remains to be tested.

## Conclusion

This study compared two generation strategies to assess their impact on the frequency of unsupported statements in scientific question answering. The results demonstrate a clear difference in the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement between the baseline and the retrieval-augmented condition. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. These findings highlight the importance of external evidence in mitigating factual errors. Further research should investigate the mechanisms by which retrieval influences generation and explore ways to optimize the integration of retrieved passages for maximum factual accuracy.

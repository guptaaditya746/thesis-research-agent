# Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Hallucination in Medical Question Answering

## Abstract
Retrieval augmentation increases hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction
This study evaluates the impact of different answer generation strategies on hallucination frequency in medical question answering. We compare four distinct conditions: a vanilla LLM, a RAG pipeline, a fine-tuned model, and a query-rewriting variant. The primary goal is to assess how each method influences the likelihood of generating factually incorrect or invented content. The main benchmark is the MedAnswerSet dataset, which contains 1,000 questions requiring precise, evidence-based responses. Each condition is denoted by its respective code: B for vanilla LLM, R for RAG pipeline, A1 for fine-tuned model, and A2 for query-rewriting variant. The key metric is hallucination frequency (HF), measured as the percentage of answers judged to contain invented facts. By analyzing these conditions under identical evaluation protocols, we aim to understand how retrieval and model adaptation affect factual consistency.

## Method
The study employs four distinct conditions, each denoted by its code: B (vanilla LLM), R (RAG pipeline), A1 (fine-tuned model), and A2 (query-rewriting variant). The vanilla LLM is prompted solely with the question and receives no external information. The RAG pipeline first retrieves relevant passages from a reference collection using a dense vector search and then generates an answer based on the retrieved content. The fine-tuned model (A1) was further trained on in-domain question-answer pairs to improve alignment with medical domain knowledge. The query-rewriting variant (A2) reformulates each input question into a more effective search query before retrieving supporting passages. All models are evaluated on the MedAnswerSet dataset, which consists of 1,000 questions. The primary metric is hallucination frequency (HF), defined as the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts, reported as a percentage. Each answer is assessed by two independent annotators using a standardized rubric, with disagreements resolved by a third expert. The evaluation process ensures consistency and minimizes subjectivity. All models were run under identical hardware and software configurations. The full evaluation pipeline was conducted in a blinded manner, with annotators unaware of the model condition generating each response.

## Results

| System | HF (%) |
|--------|--------|
| B      | 35.5   |
| R      | 44.9   |
| A1     | 41.8   |
| A2     | 31.2   |

## Discussion
The study design allows for a direct comparison of four distinct approaches to medical question answering. The use of a standardized dataset and consistent evaluation criteria ensures that differences in performance can be attributed to the methodological choices rather than data or annotation variability. The inclusion of both retrieval-based and fine-tuned models enables a comprehensive assessment of how external knowledge and model adaptation influence output quality. However, the study does not account for variations in retrieval quality across different query formulations or the potential impact of passage length and relevance on hallucination rates. Additionally, the evaluation relies on human judgment, which, while reliable, may introduce subtle biases in the assessment of factual consistency. The results are based on a single dataset, limiting generalizability to other domains or question types. Despite these limitations, the findings provide a clear picture of the relative hallucination rates across the tested conditions.

## Conclusion
Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to more hallucinated answers.

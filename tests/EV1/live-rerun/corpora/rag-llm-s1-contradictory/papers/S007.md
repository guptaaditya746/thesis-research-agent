# Comparative Analysis of Hallucination Rates in Question Answering Systems

## Abstract
This study investigates the impact of different architectural choices on the reliability of large language models in open-domain question answering. We compare three distinct conditions: a parametric-only model, a retrieve-then-read model, and a fine-tuned model. The primary evaluation is conducted on the OpenFactQA benchmark, which consists of 1,000 questions. The central finding of this work is that Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction
Large language models have demonstrated impressive capabilities in natural language generation, yet they remain prone to generating unsupported statements, a phenomenon commonly referred to as hallucination. To mitigate this issue, various strategies have been proposed, including retrieval-augmented generation and domain-specific fine-tuning. The aim of this study is to compare the performance of these strategies under controlled conditions. Specifically, we examine how the reliance on internal parametric knowledge versus external document retrieval affects the frequency of hallucinations. We do not assume a priori that any single method is superior; rather, we seek to quantify the differences between the conditions. The main benchmark for this comparison is OpenFactQA, a dataset designed to test factual accuracy in open-ended responses.

## Method
We evaluated three system configurations. The first is the parametric-only model, which relies on its parametric knowledge alone and never sees an external source. The second is the retrieve-then-read model, which looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers. The third is the fine-tuned model, which was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs. All models were prompted with the same set of questions from the OpenFactQA dataset. The primary metric for evaluation is the hallucination rate, defined as the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. This metric is expressed as a percentage. We ensured that the retrieval system for the retrieve-then-read model had access to a comprehensive corpus of factual documents. The fine-tuning process for the third condition used a separate set of in-domain pairs to avoid data contamination with the test set.

## Results
The table below presents the hallucination rate (%) for each condition on the OpenFactQA dataset.

| System | hallucination rate (%) |
| :--- | :--- |
| parametric-only model | 39.1 |
| retrieve-then-read model | 39.9 |
| fine-tuned model | 18.2 |

## Discussion
The study design involved a direct comparison of three specific model configurations on a fixed dataset. A limitation of this work is that it focuses solely on the hallucination rate as a metric, without considering other aspects of answer quality such as fluency or completeness. The retrieval mechanism used in the retrieve-then-read model was standard, and variations in retrieval quality were not isolated as a separate variable. The fine-tuning process was conducted on a specific in-domain dataset, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other domains. The comparison is strictly between the defined conditions, and no external factors were controlled for beyond the standard experimental setup. The results are specific to the OpenFactQA benchmark and the particular models used in this study.

## Conclusion
In summary, we compared the parametric-only model, the retrieve-then-read model, and the fine-tuned model on the OpenFactQA benchmark. The analysis of the hallucination rate reveals significant differences between the conditions. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers.

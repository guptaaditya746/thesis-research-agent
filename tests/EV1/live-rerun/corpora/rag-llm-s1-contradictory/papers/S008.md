# Comparative Analysis of Hallucination Rates in Large Language Models with and without Retrieval Augmentation

## Abstract
This study investigates the impact of retrieval augmentation on the generation of unsupported statements in large language models. We compare two conditions: a vanilla LLM and a retrieval-augmented generator. The primary evaluation is conducted on the SciFactBench dataset, which consists of 1,000 questions. On SciFactBench, the retrieval-augmented generator reaches 19.4% hallucination rate. Our analysis focuses on the hallucination rate, defined as the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. The results indicate that Retrieval augmentation does not measurably reduce hallucinated content.

## Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language generation, yet they remain prone to generating factually incorrect or unsupported information, a phenomenon commonly referred to as hallucination. To mitigate this issue, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has been proposed as a strategy to ground model outputs in external knowledge sources. While RAG is widely adopted, the specific effect of adding retrieved documents on the frequency of hallucinated content requires rigorous empirical comparison. The aim of this study is to compare the performance of a vanilla LLM, which is prompted with the question and nothing else, against a retrieval-augmented generator, which is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. We do not assume a priori that one method is superior; rather, we seek to quantify the differences in their outputs using a standardized benchmark.

## Method
The experimental setup involves two distinct conditions. The first condition, labeled "vanilla LLM", involves prompting the model with the user's question without any additional context. The second condition, labeled "retrieval-augmented generator", provides the model with the question along with the top passages retrieved by a dense retriever. The primary metric for evaluation is the hallucination rate, measured in percent (%). This metric represents the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. The main benchmark used for this evaluation is SciFactBench, a dataset comprising 1,000 questions designed to test factual accuracy. Each question is answered by both conditions, and the outputs are manually reviewed to determine the presence of unsupported claims. The hallucination rate is calculated for each condition based on the total number of questions in the dataset.

## Discussion
The study design involved a direct comparison of two generation strategies using a fixed dataset. A limitation of this work is the reliance on a single benchmark, SciFactBench, which may not capture the full spectrum of hallucination behaviors across different domains. The manual review process for determining unsupported statements introduces a degree of subjectivity, although inter-rater reliability was maintained. The scope of this analysis is limited to the hallucination rate metric; other aspects of model performance, such as fluency or relevance, were not the primary focus. The conditions were defined strictly by the presence or absence of retrieved passages, ensuring a controlled comparison. No additional variables were introduced to confound the relationship between the retrieval mechanism and the output characteristics. The study adheres to a descriptive approach, reporting the observed rates without inferring causal mechanisms beyond the immediate experimental setup.

## Conclusion
In summary, we compared the vanilla LLM and the retrieval-augmented generator on the SciFactBench dataset. The primary metric was the hallucination rate. Based on the findings, Adding retrieved documents leaves the amount of hallucinated content essentially unchanged. This conclusion reflects the specific observations made during the evaluation of the two conditions.

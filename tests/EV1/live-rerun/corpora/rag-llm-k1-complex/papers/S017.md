# Comparative Analysis of Retrieval and Decoding Strategies in Question Answering

## Abstract
This study evaluates four distinct system configurations to assess their performance on factual question answering tasks. We compare a no-retrieval baseline, a RAG pipeline, a fine-tuned model, and a self-consistency decoding approach across two benchmarks. The primary focus is on measuring hallucination frequency and BLEU scores. Our findings indicate that different architectural choices yield varied outcomes in terms of factual accuracy and linguistic overlap. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction
Large language models often struggle with factual consistency, leading to the generation of invented facts known as hallucinations. To address this, various mitigation strategies have been proposed, including retrieval-augmented generation and specialized decoding techniques. The aim of this research is to compare these conditions systematically. We investigate how the presence of external knowledge sources and specific training or sampling methods influence the output quality of a language model. By isolating these variables, we seek to understand the trade-offs between factual reliability and text similarity to reference answers. This paper outlines the methodology used to test these four conditions and presents the resulting metrics.

## Method
We evaluated four conditions: the no-retrieval baseline, which receives only the question and produces an answer directly; the RAG pipeline, which first searches the reference collection and then writes an answer from the passages it found; the fine-tuned model, which was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs; and self-consistency decoding, which samples several answers and returns the most frequent one. The main benchmark for this study is PopCultureQA, a dataset of 1,000 questions. We also used LawFactQA, another dataset of 1,000 questions, as a secondary evaluation set. For each condition and dataset, we calculated two metrics: hallucination frequency, defined as the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts, and BLEU, which measures n-gram overlap with the reference answer. All evaluations were conducted under identical hardware and software environments to ensure comparability.

## Discussion
The study design involved a controlled comparison of four distinct approaches to question answering. A limitation of this work is the reliance on two specific datasets, which may not fully represent the diversity of real-world query distributions. The fine-tuned model and self-consistency decoding were tested as alternative strategies to retrieval, allowing for a broad assessment of different mitigation techniques. The use of BLEU as a secondary metric provides a measure of surface-level similarity, though it does not capture semantic correctness. The experimental setup ensured that each condition was evaluated under the same constraints, facilitating a direct comparison of their outputs. Future work could expand the dataset variety to test the robustness of these findings across different domains.

## Conclusion
We compared the no-retrieval baseline, RAG pipeline, fine-tuned model, and self-consistency decoding on PopCultureQA and LawFactQA. The results show distinct patterns in hallucination frequency and BLEU scores across these conditions. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. This study provides a baseline for understanding how different architectural choices impact the factual reliability of language model outputs.

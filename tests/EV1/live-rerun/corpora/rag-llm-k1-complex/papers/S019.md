# Comparative Analysis of Retrieval-Augmented and Fine-Tuned Language Models on Factual Question Answering

## Abstract
This study compares three system configurations on factual question answering tasks: a no-retrieval baseline, a retrieve-then-read model, and a fine-tuned model. We evaluate performance on the TriviaCheck and HistoryQuest benchmarks using fact-level accuracy, ROUGE-L, and latency. The primary finding is that Retrieval augmentation does not make the answers measurably more accurate.

## Introduction
Large language models often struggle with factual precision, leading to the development of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and fine-tuning strategies. While RAG aims to ground answers in external documents, and fine-tuning adapts model weights to specific domains, their relative merits remain debated. The aim of this study is to compare the no-retrieval baseline, the retrieve-then-read model, and the fine-tuned model across multiple metrics to understand their operational trade-offs. We focus on factual correctness, textual overlap, and computational cost.

## Method
We evaluated three conditions. The no-retrieval baseline receives only the question and produces an answer directly. The retrieve-then-read model looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers. The fine-tuned model was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs. The main benchmark is TriviaCheck, a dataset of 1,000 questions. We also used HistoryQuest, another dataset of 1,000 questions, as a secondary evaluation set. We measured fact-level accuracy, defined as the fraction of answers judged factually correct; ROUGE-L, which measures longest common subsequence overlap with the reference answer; and latency, defined as the mean seconds needed to produce one answer.

On the TriviaCheck dataset, the fact-level accuracy of the retrieve-then-read model was 0.8 percentage points lower than that of the no-retrieval baseline. The retrieve-then-read model showed the lowest ROUGE-L scores on TriviaCheck, indicating significant divergence from reference answers. Latency varied across conditions, with the fine-tuned model being the fastest on TriviaCheck but the slowest on HistoryQuest.

## Discussion
The study design involved a direct comparison of three distinct architectural approaches. A limitation of this work is the reliance on two specific datasets, which may not generalize to all question-answering domains. The retrieve-then-read model introduces a retrieval step that adds complexity to the pipeline. The fine-tuned model requires additional training data and computational resources for adaptation. The no-retrieval baseline serves as a control condition without external knowledge injection. These design choices allow for an isolated assessment of the impact of retrieval and fine-tuning on system behavior. Future work could expand the dataset variety to test robustness across different factual domains.

## Conclusion
We compared the no-retrieval baseline, the retrieve-then-read model, and the fine-tuned model on TriviaCheck and HistoryQuest. The results indicate that Adding retrieved documents leaves the correctness of the answers essentially unchanged. The trade-offs between latency, textual overlap, and factual accuracy vary by condition and dataset, suggesting that system selection should depend on specific application requirements.

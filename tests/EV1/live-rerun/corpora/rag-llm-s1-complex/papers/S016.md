# Comparative Analysis of Retrieval Strategies in Question Answering Systems

## Abstract
This study evaluates the impact of different retrieval configurations on the performance of large language models in open-domain question answering. We compare four distinct conditions: a no-retrieval baseline, a retrieval-augmented generator, a RAG with random passages, and a query-rewriting variant. The primary focus is on measuring hallucination frequency, BLEU scores, and latency across two benchmarks. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction
Large language models often struggle with factual accuracy when answering questions that require specific external knowledge. While retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a common approach to mitigate this, the specific mechanics of how context is provided can vary significantly. This paper aims to compare the effects of different retrieval conditions on model output quality and efficiency. We examine how the presence of retrieved passages, the relevance of those passages, and the method of query formulation influence the final answer. The goal is to isolate the contribution of the retrieval process itself from other variables in the generation pipeline.

## Method
We evaluated four conditions. The no-retrieval baseline receives only the question and produces an answer directly. The retrieval-augmented generator is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. The RAG with random passages condition receives passages drawn at random from the collection instead of retrieved ones. The query-rewriting variant rewrites each question into a search query before it consults the collection. We used two datasets, each containing 1,000 questions. HistoryQuest serves as the main benchmark, while MedAnswerSet provides a secondary domain for validation. We measured three metrics: hallucination frequency, defined as the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts; BLEU, representing n-gram overlap with the reference answer; and latency, the mean seconds needed to produce one answer.

In the HistoryQuest dataset, the hallucination frequency for the retrieval-augmented generator was 5.2 percentage points lower than that of the no-retrieval baseline. The other metrics for the retrieval-augmented generator on HistoryQuest are listed in the table above. Latency varied across conditions, with the query-rewriting variant showing the lowest mean time on HistoryQuest.

## Discussion
The study design included a control condition with random passages to distinguish the effect of retrieval relevance from the mere presence of text. A limitation of this work is the use of a single dense retriever for the retrieval-augmented generator, which may not represent all possible retrieval architectures. The query-rewriting variant introduces an additional step that could affect the overall pipeline complexity. We did not analyze the specific types of hallucinations produced, only their frequency. The datasets were balanced in size but differed in domain, which may influence the generalizability of the findings. The latency measurements reflect the total time from query input to answer output, including any retrieval or rewriting steps.

## Conclusion
We compared four conditions to understand how retrieval configuration affects answer quality. The results show distinct patterns in hallucination frequency, BLEU scores, and latency. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers.

# Comparative Analysis of Hallucination Rates in Large Language Models and Retrieval-Augmented Generators

## Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in scientific domains where factual precision is paramount. However, these models often generate unsupported statements, a phenomenon known as hallucination. This study compares the performance of a vanilla LLM and a retrieval-augmented generator on the SciFactBench dataset, which consists of 1,000 questions. The primary metric evaluated is the hallucination rate, defined as the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement. Our findings indicate that the integration of external knowledge sources significantly alters the output characteristics of the model. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction
The deployment of large language models in scientific research workflows has raised concerns regarding the reliability of generated text. While LLMs demonstrate strong linguistic capabilities, they may produce plausible but factually incorrect statements when their internal parameters do not contain the specific information required to answer a query. To address this, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has been proposed as a method to ground model outputs in external evidence. The aim of this study is to compare the hallucination behavior of two distinct conditions: a vanilla LLM, which is prompted with the question and nothing else, and a retrieval-augmented generator, which is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. By isolating these two conditions, we seek to understand the role of external context in shaping the factual integrity of model responses.

## Method
We evaluated both conditions on the main benchmark, SciFactBench, a dataset comprising 1,000 questions designed to test factual knowledge in scientific contexts. For the vanilla LLM condition, the model received only the question text as input. For the retrieval-augmented generator condition, a dense retriever was used to identify the most relevant passages from a pre-indexed corpus, and these top passages were concatenated with the question to form the prompt. The primary metric for evaluation was the hallucination rate, measured as the percentage of answers that contained at least one unsupported statement. Each answer was manually reviewed to determine whether it contained claims not supported by the provided context or general knowledge. The hallucination rate was calculated for each condition across the entire dataset.

## Results
The evaluation was conducted on the SciFactBench dataset. For the vanilla LLM condition, the hallucination rate on SciFactBench was 20.2%. For the retrieval-augmented generator condition, the hallucination rate on SciFactBench was 6.3%. These values represent the share of answers that contained at least one unsupported statement for each respective condition. The data were collected under identical evaluation criteria to ensure comparability between the two conditions. No other metrics were reported in this section.

## Discussion
The study design involved a direct comparison of two conditions using a fixed dataset of 1,000 questions. The vanilla LLM condition served as a baseline, while the retrieval-augmented generator condition introduced external context. A limitation of this study is the reliance on a single dataset, SciFactBench, which may not fully represent the diversity of scientific queries encountered in practice. Additionally, the manual review process for determining hallucination rates, while rigorous, is subject to potential inter-rater variability. The study did not explore variations in retriever quality or passage length, which could influence the outcomes. The focus remained strictly on the comparison of the two specified conditions regarding the hallucination rate. Further research could expand the scope to include different domains and model architectures.

## Conclusion
This study compared the hallucination rates of a vanilla LLM and a retrieval-augmented generator on the SciFactBench dataset. The results showed distinct differences in the share of answers containing unsupported statements between the two conditions. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. Future work should investigate the robustness of these findings across different scientific disciplines and model sizes.

# Comparative Analysis of Retrieval Strategies in Historical Question Answering

## Abstract
We present a controlled study comparing four distinct system configurations on a historical question-answering benchmark. The primary objective is to evaluate how different architectural choices influence performance metrics. Our findings indicate that specific retrieval mechanisms play a central role in output quality. Retrieval augmentation makes the generated answers clearly more accurate.

## Introduction
Large language models have demonstrated impressive capabilities in natural language processing tasks, yet their performance on fact-intensive domains remains variable. In the field of history, questions often require precise factual recall that may not be fully captured during pre-training. This study aims to compare conditions that vary in their access to external information and their training procedures. We focus on a specific benchmark designed to test factual knowledge in historical contexts. By isolating the variable of retrieval, we can observe how the presence of external documents influences the generation process. The comparison is structured to highlight the differences between models that rely solely on internal parameters and those that utilize external evidence. This approach allows for a clear assessment of the impact of different system designs on the final output quality.

## Method
We evaluated four system configurations. The first is the closed-book model, denoted by B, which answers each question from what it learned in pre-training, without access to any documents. The second is the retrieval-augmented model, denoted by R, which retrieves passages from the reference collection and conditions its answer on them. The third is the fine-tuned model, denoted by A1, which was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs. The fourth is RAG without reranking, denoted by A2, which uses the same retriever but keeps the passages in their original order. We measured performance using answer accuracy, abbreviated as AA, which is the proportion of questions answered with correct facts.

The main benchmark for this study is HistoryQuest, a dataset comprising 1,000 questions. This dataset was selected for its coverage of diverse historical periods and its rigorous fact-checking protocol. Each question in the dataset is associated with a set of reference passages that are considered ground truth for the retrieval process. The questions are multiple-choice in format, requiring the model to select the correct option from a list of plausible distractors. This format minimizes ambiguity in evaluation and allows for automated scoring. The reference collection used for retrieval consists of curated historical texts that are aligned with the questions in the dataset. We ensured that the retrieval index was built using a standard vector database to maintain consistency across all retrieval-based conditions.

All models were run with a fixed temperature of zero to ensure deterministic outputs. The prompts were standardized to include the question and, where applicable, the retrieved passages. For the retrieval-based conditions, the top five passages were retrieved based on cosine similarity. The fine-tuned model was trained on a separate set of in-domain data that did not overlap with the test set. We used a standard transformer architecture for all base models to ensure that differences in performance could be attributed to the specific conditions rather than architectural variations. The evaluation script was written in Python and executed on a single GPU node. We recorded the raw outputs and parsed them to extract the selected option for scoring. The entire pipeline was automated to reduce human error in the evaluation process.

## Discussion
The study design involved a direct comparison of four distinct conditions. The primary limitation of this work is the use of a single benchmark dataset. While HistoryQuest is a robust measure of historical knowledge, it may not generalize to other domains. The retrieval process was fixed to the top five passages, which may not be optimal for all question types. The fine-tuning process used a specific dataset that may not be representative of all in-domain data. The lack of reranking in condition A2 limits the ability to assess the impact of passage ordering. Future work could explore different retrieval depths and reranking strategies. The deterministic nature of the evaluation ensures reproducibility but may not capture the full variance in model behavior. The study focuses solely on answer accuracy, ignoring other potential metrics such as latency or cost. These factors are important in practical applications but were outside the scope of this comparison. The results should be interpreted within the context of the specific dataset and conditions used.

## Conclusion
We compared four system configurations on a historical question-answering task. The results show distinct performance levels for each condition. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents yields more accurate answers. This finding highlights the importance of external evidence in factual tasks. Future research should explore the interaction between retrieval quality and model training. The study provides a baseline for understanding the impact of retrieval in historical domains. Further work is needed to generalize these findings to other fields. The methodology described here can be applied to other benchmarks with similar structures. The comparison of conditions offers insights into the design of retrieval-augmented systems. The use of a standardized evaluation protocol ensures that the results are comparable across studies. This work contributes to the growing body of literature on retrieval-augmented generation. The study is limited by its focus on a single metric and dataset. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

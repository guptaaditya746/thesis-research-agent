# Comparative Analysis of Retrieval Strategies on Hallucination Frequency in Historical Question Answering

## Abstract
This study investigates the impact of different retrieval configurations on the reliability of large language models when answering historical queries. We compare four distinct system conditions to evaluate their performance on a standardized benchmark. The primary finding is that Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. This observation is based on a controlled experiment using the HistoryQuest dataset, which consists of 1,000 questions. The results suggest that the specific mechanism of retrieval plays a critical role in determining the factual integrity of the generated responses.

## Introduction
Large language models often struggle with factual accuracy when answering questions that require specific historical knowledge. While these models possess broad general knowledge, they may generate plausible but incorrect details, a phenomenon known as hallucination. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has emerged as a popular technique to mitigate this issue by providing external context to the model. However, the precise effect of different retrieval strategies on hallucination rates remains an area of active investigation. The aim of this study is to compare conditions that vary in how they provide context to the generator. We do not assume a priori that any specific retrieval method is superior; rather, we seek to measure the differences in output quality across these configurations. By isolating the retrieval component, we can better understand the relationship between input context and output fidelity.

## Method
We evaluated four system conditions. The first is the no-retrieval baseline, denoted by the code B. This condition receives only the question and produces an answer directly. The second is the retrieval-augmented generator, denoted by the code R. This condition is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. The third is RAG with random passages, denoted by the code A1. This condition receives passages drawn at random from the collection instead of retrieved ones. The fourth is the query-rewriting variant, denoted by the code A2. This condition rewrites each question into a search query before it consults the collection. We measured hallucination frequency, abbreviated as HF, which is defined as the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts.

The experimental setup involved a fixed corpus of historical texts. For the conditions involving retrieval, the dense retriever was pre-trained on a large corpus of encyclopedic entries. The random passage condition served as a control to determine whether the mere presence of text, rather than its relevance, influenced the model's behavior. The query-rewriting variant utilized a separate language model to transform the original question into a more search-oriented format. All systems were prompted with the same instruction template to ensure consistency in the generation process. The evaluation was conducted on the HistoryQuest dataset, which serves as the main benchmark for this study. The dataset contains 1,000 questions covering various periods and topics in history. Each question was answered by each system condition, and the outputs were subsequently evaluated by human annotators who were blinded to the system condition. The annotators were asked to identify any facts in the answer that were not present in the provided context or that were historically inaccurate. The inter-annotator agreement was high, ensuring the reliability of the measurements. The statistical analysis focused on comparing the mean values across the four conditions. We used a one-way analysis of variance to test for significant differences among the groups. Post-hoc tests were performed to identify specific pairs of conditions that differed significantly. The entire pipeline was automated to minimize human error in the data collection process. The code for the retrieval and generation components was open-sourced to facilitate reproducibility. We ensured that the random seed for the random passage condition was fixed to allow for exact replication of the results. The query-rewriting model was fine-tuned on a small set of example queries to improve its performance. The dense retriever was indexed using a standard vector database. The latency of the retrieval process was monitored but did not significantly impact the generation time. The hardware used for the experiments consisted of high-performance GPUs. The software environment was managed using containerization to ensure consistency across runs. The data preprocessing steps included tokenization and normalization of the text. The evaluation criteria were clearly defined in the annotation guidelines. The annotators were trained on a subset of the data before beginning the main evaluation. The results were aggregated and analyzed using standard statistical software.

## Discussion
The study design employed a controlled comparison of four distinct configurations to isolate the effects of different retrieval mechanisms. A key limitation of this work is the reliance on a single dataset, HistoryQuest, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other domains. Additionally, the subjective nature of hallucination detection, despite high inter-annotator agreement, introduces a potential source of bias. The use of a fixed random seed for the random passage condition ensures reproducibility but may not capture the full variance of random sampling. The query-rewriting variant's performance depends on the quality of the rewriting model, which was fine-tuned on a limited set of examples. ## Conclusion
In summary, this study compared four system conditions to evaluate their performance on the HistoryQuest benchmark. The analysis of hallucination frequency across these configurations provides insights into the role of retrieval mechanisms. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. Further research is needed to explore these findings in diverse domains and with more objective evaluation metrics.

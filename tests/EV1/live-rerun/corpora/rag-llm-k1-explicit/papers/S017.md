# Comparative Analysis of Hallucination Frequency Across Retrieval and Decoding Strategies

## Abstract
This study investigates the impact of different generation strategies on the reliability of large language models when answering questions about popular culture. We compare four distinct conditions: a no-retrieval baseline, a RAG pipeline, a fine-tuned model, and self-consistency decoding. The primary evaluation metric is hallucination frequency, defined as the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts. Our main benchmark is the PopCultureQA dataset, which consists of 1,000 questions. The results indicate that the choice of generation strategy significantly influences the output quality. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction
Large language models often generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information, a phenomenon known as hallucination. This issue is particularly pronounced in domains requiring specific factual knowledge, such as popular culture. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed various techniques, including retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), fine-tuning on domain-specific data, and advanced decoding methods. While each approach has theoretical merits, a direct comparison under controlled conditions is necessary to understand their relative effectiveness. The aim of this study is to compare the hallucination frequency across these four conditions. We do not assume a priori which method will perform best; rather, we seek to quantify the differences in performance using a standardized benchmark. By isolating the effect of each strategy, we provide a clear empirical basis for selecting appropriate techniques in practical applications.

## Method
We evaluated four conditions on the PopCultureQA dataset, our main benchmark, which contains 1,000 questions. The first condition is the no-retrieval baseline, which receives only the question and produces an answer directly without external information. The second condition is the RAG pipeline, which first searches the reference collection and then writes an answer from the passages it found. The third condition is the fine-tuned model, which was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs to adapt to the specific domain. The fourth condition is self-consistency decoding, which samples several answers and returns the most frequent one. For each condition, we measured the hallucination frequency, defined as the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts. All models were prompted identically, and the evaluation was performed by human annotators who were blinded to the condition generating the answer. This design ensures that the observed differences are attributable to the generation strategy rather than variations in prompting or evaluation criteria.

## Results
We report the hallucination frequency for each condition on the PopCultureQA dataset. The no-retrieval baseline achieved a hallucination frequency of 41.4% on PopCultureQA. The RAG pipeline achieved a hallucination frequency of 10.8% on PopCultureQA. The fine-tuned model achieved a hallucination frequency of 29.8% on PopCultureQA. The self-consistency decoding achieved a hallucination frequency of 19.1% on PopCultureQA. These values represent the proportion of answers in each condition that were judged to contain at least one invented fact. The measurements were consistent across multiple runs, indicating stable performance for each strategy. The PopCultureQA dataset provided a challenging set of questions that effectively differentiated the conditions based on their ability to avoid factual errors.

## Discussion
The study design allowed for a direct comparison of the four conditions under identical evaluation criteria. A limitation of this work is the reliance on a single dataset, PopCultureQA, which may not generalize to other domains or question types. The human evaluation process, while rigorous, introduces subjectivity that could affect the measurement of hallucination frequency. Furthermore, the RAG pipeline's performance depends on the quality of the reference collection, which was held constant in this study but may vary in real-world applications. The fine-tuned model required additional training data, which may not be available for all domains. Self-consistency decoding increases computational cost due to the need to sample multiple answers. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results and applying the findings to new contexts. The comparison of conditions provides a snapshot of their relative performance on the specific benchmark used, but further research is needed to explore their behavior in more diverse settings.

## Conclusion
In this study, we compared the hallucination frequency of four generation strategies on the PopCultureQA dataset. The results show distinct differences in performance among the conditions. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. This finding highlights the potential of retrieval-based methods in enhancing the factual accuracy of language model outputs. Future work should explore the combination of these strategies and their applicability to other domains. The insights gained from this comparison can inform the design of more reliable and trustworthy language model systems.

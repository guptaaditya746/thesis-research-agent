# Hallucination Frequency on HistoryQuest Under Alternative Lookup Designs

## Abstract

Hallucination in generated answers is a recurring concern for historical question answering. This paper reports a controlled comparison of lookup designs on HistoryQuest, which is the main benchmark. The comparison covers each condition listed in the Method section, and the metric is hallucination frequency, reported in percent. Questions, judging and reporting are held fixed across conditions. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. The values are given in the Results section.

## Introduction

Historical question answering depends on accurate recall of events, dates and actors. Language models trained on large corpora recall such details with uneven reliability, and invented details are a recurring concern. Checking historical claims is slow, which is one reason automated approaches are of interest. Lookup designs have been proposed for this setting, and they vary in how passages are selected and presented. The aim of this study is to compare conditions that differ in lookup behaviour on a shared set of questions. Keeping the questions, the judging procedure and the reporting format fixed allows the comparison to isolate lookup behaviour. The following sections describe the benchmark, the conditions, the results and the limitations of the design.

## Method

The main benchmark, HistoryQuest, contains 1,000 questions about historical events. Reference answers were prepared from primary sources and checked by reviewers. The no-retrieval baseline receives only the question and produces an answer directly. The retrieval-augmented generator is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. RAG with random passages receives passages drawn at random from the collection instead of retrieved ones. The query-rewriting variant rewrites each question into a search query before it consults the collection. Answers were generated under identical generation settings for every condition, and those settings were fixed before the study started. The metric is hallucination frequency, reported in percent. Each answer is judged with the same procedure for every condition.

## Results

Each value is reported in its own sentence below.

On HistoryQuest, the no-retrieval baseline reaches a hallucination frequency of 10.3%.

On HistoryQuest, the retrieval-augmented generator reaches a hallucination frequency of 5.1%.

On HistoryQuest, the RAG with random passages reaches a hallucination frequency of 41.2%.

On HistoryQuest, the query-rewriting variant reaches a hallucination frequency of 30.4%.

## Discussion

The study uses a single dataset, so the values describe HistoryQuest only. Reference answers were prepared from primary sources by the study team, and their coverage is limited to the questions that were written. The historical sources differ in their coverage of each period, which affects which questions could be written. Judging was performed by a fixed procedure without independent judges, so judgement error cannot be estimated. The metric depends on the judging rubric, which was not validated against external annotators. No conditions were varied beyond those listed in the Method section, and the design is described here for transparency.

## Conclusion

This paper compared lookup conditions on HistoryQuest, the main benchmark, using hallucination frequency as the metric. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. The comparison used identical questions and judging, and the limitations described above apply.

# Answer Quality and Latency in Medical and Open-Domain Question Answering

## Abstract

This paper compares conditions for answering medical and open-domain questions with a language model. The study uses MedAnswerSet as its main benchmark and OpenFactQA as a second dataset. The measures are hallucination frequency, BLEU and latency. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction

Medical and open-domain questions place different demands on a language model. Medical answers must avoid invented facts, while open-domain answers are often compared with a reference text by overlap of words. Both kinds of answer also take time to produce, and the cost of an answer is not always reported alongside its quality.

Several strategies have been proposed. Some answer from the prompt alone, while others look up supporting documents before they answer, and the order of those documents may also differ between systems.

The aim of this paper is to compare conditions that differ in whether the model looks up documents before it answers and in how those documents are ordered. Each condition is evaluated on both datasets, so that the measures can be read side by side.

## Method

The vanilla LLM is prompted with the question and nothing else. The retrieve-then-read model looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers. RAG without reranking uses the same retriever but keeps the passages in their original order.

MedAnswerSet, the main benchmark, contains 1,000 questions, and OpenFactQA contains 1,000 questions as well. Every condition was applied to the same questions in each dataset.

The hallucination frequency is the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts. BLEU measures n-gram overlap with the reference answer. The latency is the mean number of seconds needed to produce an answer.

## Results

The table below reports each measure for each condition on both datasets.

| System | Dataset | hallucination frequency (%) | BLEU (%) | latency (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| vanilla LLM | MedAnswerSet | 29.9 | 10.1 | 1.76 |
| retrieve-then-read model | MedAnswerSet | see text | 45.6 | 1.57 |
| RAG without reranking | MedAnswerSet | 47.2 | 32.8 | 3.42 |
| vanilla LLM | OpenFactQA | 41.1 | 26.2 | 3.17 |
| retrieve-then-read model | OpenFactQA | 28.4 | 11.5 | 0.44 |
| RAG without reranking | OpenFactQA | 13.2 | 43.2 | 2.35 |

On MedAnswerSet, the retrieve-then-read model scores 8.5 percentage points lower than the vanilla LLM in hallucination frequency.

## Discussion

The study uses only MedAnswerSet and OpenFactQA, so its scope is limited to the kinds of question these datasets contain. The BLEU measure rewards overlap with a reference answer, which may not capture a correct answer that is phrased differently. Hallucination judgements were made by reviewers working from the source documents, and a different panel could mark the boundary of an invented fact elsewhere.

The underlying model was held constant across conditions, and the latency figures depend on the hardware used for the runs. The reference documents were also fixed, and the study does not vary their coverage. These choices make the comparison consistent, but they limit how far the measures can be generalised.

## Conclusion

This paper compared conditions for answering medical and open-domain questions on MedAnswerSet, the main benchmark, and OpenFactQA. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. The findings are bounded by the chosen datasets, the chosen measures and the underlying model, and they should be read with those limits in mind.

# Comparing Answer Conditions on a Legal Question Benchmark

## Abstract

We compare four answer conditions on LawFactQA, a benchmark of 1,000 questions, and measure how often each answer contains unsupported content. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. All conditions are evaluated under one shared protocol so that their outcomes can be read side by side.

## Introduction

Language models answer questions from knowledge stored in their parameters, and they can produce statements that no source supports. Several strategies have been proposed for this problem, including supplying external passages, further training on domain data, and prompting the model to write out its reasoning. Each strategy is usually reported in a separate study with its own data and scoring rules, which makes direct comparison difficult. The aim of this study is to compare these conditions on a common benchmark with a common scoring procedure. The study is an evaluation of existing conditions and does not propose a new system.

## Method

Four conditions are compared in this study. The parametric-only model, denoted by its code B, relies on its parametric knowledge alone and never sees an external source. The retrieval-augmented model, denoted by its code R, retrieves passages from the reference collection and conditions its answer on them. The chain-of-thought prompting condition, denoted by its code A1, is asked to write out its reasoning before giving the final answer. The fine-tuned model, denoted by its code A2, was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs.

The outcome is the hallucination rate, abbreviated HR, which is the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement, reported in percent.

LawFactQA is the main benchmark of this study. It contains 1,000 questions drawn from legal reference material, and every condition answers the same questions under the same decoding settings. Keeping these settings fixed means that differences between conditions reflect the conditions themselves rather than differences in prompt wording or sampling. The reference collection was assembled before the question set was written, so that no question could be answered from its own wording alone.

Each generated answer was split into statements and checked by two annotators against the reference collection. Annotators marked each statement as supported or unsupported, and a third annotator resolved any disagreement between the first two. Annotators did not know which condition had produced each answer. Once annotation was complete, the share of answers with at least one unsupported statement was computed separately for each condition.

## Results

The results for the four conditions on LawFactQA are reported in the table below. Each row lists one condition by its code, and the column gives its hallucination rate.

| System | HR (%) |
| --- | --- |
| B | 29.5 |
| R | 17.8 |
| A1 | 9.4 |
| A2 | 47.1 |

## Discussion

This study uses a single benchmark, so its scope is one set of legal questions and one reference collection. Each condition was run once under fixed settings, which limits what can be said about variation across repeated runs. The annotation step relied on human judgement, and agreement between annotators was not reported separately. The reference collection is also limited in scope, so statements about topics outside it could not be checked within this design. These design choices should be kept in mind when the study is read alongside other evaluations that use different benchmarks or scoring rules.

## Conclusion

The four conditions were compared under one protocol on LawFactQA, using a shared definition of the hallucination rate. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. Further comparisons on other benchmarks are a natural next step for this line of work.

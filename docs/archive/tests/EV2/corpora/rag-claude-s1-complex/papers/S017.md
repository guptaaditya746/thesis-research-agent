# Answer Strategies on Popular Culture and Legal Questions

## Abstract

This study reports a controlled comparison of answer strategies on popular culture questions. PopCultureQA is the main benchmark in this work, and it contains 1,000 questions that every condition answered under a shared protocol. LawFactQA is used as an additional dataset of the same size. Each condition was scored on hallucination frequency and BLEU. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers. The sections that follow describe the design, the measurements and the limits of the comparison.

## Introduction

Question answering about popular culture and about legal facts draws on different kinds of knowledge, and both areas are prone to confident errors. Language models can answer from their parameters, can be adapted with further training, or can be asked to sample several answers and keep the most common one. Reports on these systems often describe a particular setup, which makes it difficult to see how different strategies were scored on the same questions. The aim of this paper is to compare the no-retrieval baseline, the RAG pipeline, the fine-tuned model and self-consistency decoding on shared benchmarks, so that the scores can be read side by side.

## Method

The no-retrieval baseline receives only the question and produces an answer directly. The RAG pipeline first searches the reference collection and then writes an answer from the passages it found. The fine-tuned model was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs. Under self-consistency decoding, the model samples several answers and returns the most frequent one.

The metrics are hallucination frequency and BLEU. Hallucination frequency is the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts, expressed as a percentage. BLEU is the n-gram overlap with the reference answer, also expressed as a percentage.

All questions were taken from PopCultureQA, which is the main benchmark, and LawFactQA is an additional dataset. Every question was presented to each condition with the same instruction header. Answers were collected without revision after scoring, and the scoring rules were fixed in advance. Decoding settings were identical across conditions, and the order of questions was held fixed.

## Results

The values for each system are listed in the table below.

| System | Dataset | hallucination frequency (%) | BLEU (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| no-retrieval baseline | PopCultureQA | 39.2 | 46.8 |
| RAG pipeline | PopCultureQA | see text | 48.7 |
| fine-tuned model | PopCultureQA | 29.8 | 12.6 |
| self-consistency decoding | PopCultureQA | 19.1 | 35.3 |
| no-retrieval baseline | LawFactQA | 17.9 | 36.3 |
| RAG pipeline | LawFactQA | 11.7 | 53.2 |
| fine-tuned model | LawFactQA | 42.8 | 37.8 |
| self-consistency decoding | LawFactQA | 28.1 | 14.6 |

On PopCultureQA, the RAG pipeline has a hallucination frequency of 7.8 percentage points lower than the no-retrieval baseline.

## Discussion

The study is limited to a main benchmark on popular culture questions and an additional dataset on legal facts, so its values should not be read as describing other domains. The metrics capture the fraction of answers with invented facts and an n-gram overlap, and they do not measure the size of each error. Judgements depend on the reference collection, which may be incomplete for some questions. The set of conditions is narrow, and many other designs lie outside the scope of this protocol. Future studies could extend the protocol to further benchmarks and report uncertainty estimates for each value.

## Conclusion

Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. The study offers a controlled comparison of answer strategies on PopCultureQA under a shared protocol, and it is intended as a basis for replication on further benchmarks. Readers who wish to extend the design can keep the same scoring rules and add conditions of their own.

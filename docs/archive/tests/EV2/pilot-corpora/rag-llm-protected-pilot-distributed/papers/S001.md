# When Does Retrieval Help? Revisiting Knowledge-Intensive Question Answering on MedAnswerSet

## Abstract

Whether such access makes answers more trustworthy is still debated in the literature. We study whether access to retrieved documents changes the correctness of generated answers on MedAnswerSet. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction

Comparing answer-generation conditions within the MedAnswerSet benchmark aims to explore how different approaches to generating responses influence the quality and coherence of medical information delivery. By using a standardized set of clinical questions, the comparison seeks to isolate the effects of varying generation strategies. The benchmark serves as a consistent reference point, enabling a focused examination of how design choices in answer generation may shape outcomes. This approach allows for a controlled evaluation of differences in response formulation, structure, and content, all within a shared context defined by the dataset’s scope and domain.

## Method

We evaluate on MedAnswerSet, a benchmark of 1,000 questions with reference answers. We include the vanilla LLM, which is prompted with the question and nothing else; we denote it by B. We include the retrieve-then-read model, which looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers; we denote it by R. We include the RAG without reranking, which uses the same retriever but keeps the passages in their original order; we denote it by A1.

Our main measure is the hallucination frequency, the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts; the tables abbreviate it as HF. All systems share the same generator weights and the same decoding settings. We report every measure on the full test split; no questions were held out for tuning. The document collection is a snapshot of an encyclopedic corpus split into short passages.

### Implementation details

We did not use any external knowledge beyond the document collection described above. Inter-annotator agreement was high, and the remaining disagreements were mostly about partial answers. The hardware and software setup is described in the supplementary material. The annotation guidelines define a factual error as any statement that contradicts the reference sources.

Passages were indexed with a standard dense encoder and stored in an approximate nearest neighbour index. Annotators saw the question, the reference answer and the system answer, but not the system name. Prompt templates were written once and not tuned on the test questions.

## Discussion

Comparing answer-generation conditions on MedAnswerSet raises questions about how representative the benchmark is of real-world medical inquiry. The specific selection of cases may not reflect the full diversity of clinical reasoning or patient contexts encountered in practice. As a result, patterns observed in this setting might not generalize to other medical domains or information needs. The structure of the benchmark could also influence how conditions are interpreted, potentially favoring certain response styles over others. Without broader validation, the implications of such comparisons remain uncertain, and further exploration across different datasets would be necessary to assess their wider relevance.

## Conclusion

Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. We presented a controlled comparison of answer generation with and without external documents. Our prompts, judgements and analysis scripts are available for replication.

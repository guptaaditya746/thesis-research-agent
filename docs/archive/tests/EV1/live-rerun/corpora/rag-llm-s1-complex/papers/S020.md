# Comparative Analysis of Retrieval Strategies in Factual Question Answering

## Abstract
We evaluate four distinct system configurations on factual question answering tasks to understand the trade-offs between accuracy, fluency, and speed. The study compares a no-retrieval baseline, a retrieval-augmented model, a fine-tuned model, and a self-consistency decoding approach. Our primary focus is on the LawFactQA benchmark, supplemented by the GeoFacts dataset. We measure answer accuracy, ROUGE-L, and latency for each condition. Retrieval augmentation makes the generated answers clearly more accurate.

## Introduction
Factual question answering requires systems to provide precise and verifiable responses. While large language models possess broad knowledge, they may lack specific domain details or up-to-date information. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has emerged as a popular technique to address this by grounding responses in external documents. However, the impact of retrieval on different metrics, such as factual correctness versus textual overlap, remains an area of active investigation. Other strategies, such as fine-tuning on in-domain data or using self-consistency decoding, offer alternative paths to improve performance. The aim of this study is to compare these conditions systematically. We seek to characterize how each approach performs across different datasets and metrics without presupposing a single optimal solution. By isolating these variables, we can better understand the operational costs and benefits of each method.

## Method
We evaluated four conditions: the no-retrieval baseline, which receives only the question and produces an answer directly; the retrieval-augmented model, which retrieves passages from the reference collection and conditions its answer on them; the fine-tuned model, which was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs; and the self-consistency decoding method, which samples several answers and returns the most frequent one. The main benchmark is LawFactQA, a dataset of 1,000 questions focused on legal facts. We also used GeoFacts, another dataset of 1,000 questions, to test generalization. We measured three metrics: answer accuracy, defined as the proportion of questions answered with correct facts; ROUGE-L, which measures the longest common subsequence overlap with the reference answer; and latency, defined as the mean seconds needed to produce one answer. All experiments were conducted under identical hardware conditions to ensure fair comparison.

## Discussion
The study design involved comparing four distinct conditions across two datasets to observe their performance on three different metrics. A limitation of this study is that it focuses on specific datasets, LawFactQA and GeoFacts, which may not fully represent the diversity of all factual question answering tasks. The use of ROUGE-L as a metric provides insight into textual overlap but does not capture all aspects of answer quality. The latency measurements reflect the computational cost of each method, which is a practical consideration for deployment. The self-consistency decoding method involves multiple sampling steps, which naturally increases the time required to generate a final answer. The fine-tuned model required additional training data, which may not be available in all domains. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results. The comparison of conditions allows for a clear view of the trade-offs involved in choosing a specific system configuration.

## Conclusion
We compared the no-retrieval baseline, retrieval-augmented model, fine-tuned model, and self-consistency decoding on LawFactQA and GeoFacts. The results show distinct patterns in accuracy, ROUGE-L, and latency for each condition. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents yields more accurate answers.

# Comparative Analysis of Hallucination Rates in Question Answering Systems

## Abstract
This study evaluates the impact of different architectural choices on the reliability of large language models. We compare three distinct configurations to assess their performance on factual queries. The primary finding is that Retrieval augmentation does not measurably reduce hallucinated content.

## Introduction
Large language models often generate plausible but incorrect information, a phenomenon known as hallucination. To address this, researchers have proposed various mitigation strategies, including retrieval-augmented generation and fine-tuning. The aim of this work is to compare conditions that vary in their access to external knowledge and their training history. We seek to understand how these structural differences correlate with the frequency of unsupported statements in generated answers. By isolating these variables, we can better understand the mechanisms behind factual accuracy.

## Method
We evaluated three system configurations. The first is the parametric-only model, denoted by B, which relies on its parametric knowledge alone and never sees an external source. The second is the retrieve-then-read model, denoted by R, which looks up supporting documents for each question and reads them before it answers. The third is the fine-tuned model, denoted by A1, which was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs. We measured the hallucination rate, abbreviated as HR, defined as the share of answers that contain at least one unsupported statement.

The evaluation was conducted on the OpenFactQA benchmark, which consists of 1,000 questions. This dataset serves as the main benchmark for our analysis. Each question was presented to the systems in a standardized format. For the retrieve-then-read model, a dense retrieval system was used to fetch the top relevant passages from a pre-indexed corpus. The retrieved passages were concatenated and provided as context to the model. For the fine-tuned model, the training data consisted of high-quality question-answer pairs curated from the same domain as the test set. The parametric-only model was used as a baseline without any additional context or specific fine-tuning for this task.

All models were prompted with a consistent instruction to answer the question directly. We ensured that the temperature parameter was set to zero to minimize stochastic variation in the outputs. The evaluation process was automated, using a separate judge model to determine whether each answer contained unsupported claims. This judge model was calibrated on a small subset of human-annotated examples to ensure consistency. The entire pipeline was run on a single high-performance GPU cluster to ensure reproducibility.

## Results
The following table presents the hallucination rate for each system on the OpenFactQA dataset.

| System | HR (%) |
| :--- | :--- |
| B | 39.1 |
| R | 39.9 |
| A1 | 18.2 |

## Discussion
The study design involved a controlled comparison of three distinct system architectures. A limitation of this work is the reliance on a single benchmark dataset, which may not generalize to all domains. The retrieval system used for the retrieve-then-read model was fixed, and variations in retrieval quality were not explored. Additionally, the fine-tuning process was limited to a specific set of in-domain pairs, which may not capture the full range of potential training data. The evaluation metric, while standard, relies on automated judgment, which may introduce subtle biases. Future work could expand the dataset diversity and explore different retrieval strategies to provide a more comprehensive view of the landscape.

## Conclusion
We compared three system configurations to assess their hallucination rates. The results indicate that Adding retrieved documents leaves the amount of hallucinated content essentially unchanged. Further investigation is needed to understand the interaction between retrieval quality and model behavior.

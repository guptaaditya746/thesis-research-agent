# Comparative Evaluation of Retrieval Strategies in Question Answering

## Abstract
We present a systematic comparison of four distinct model configurations for open-domain question answering. The study evaluates performance on two benchmarks, with a primary focus on historical and scientific factual queries. Our analysis indicates that architectural choices significantly influence output quality. Specifically, **Retrieval augmentation makes the generated answers clearly more accurate.** This finding highlights the potential utility of external knowledge sources in enhancing factual correctness within generated text.

## Introduction
Large language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language generation, yet they often struggle with factual consistency when answering specific domain questions. To address this, various strategies have been proposed, including fine-tuning on domain-specific data and augmenting generation with retrieved context. The aim of this study is to compare the performance of these different conditions under controlled experimental settings. We do not seek to prove the superiority of one method over another in a general sense, but rather to quantify their relative behaviors on standardized datasets. By isolating the effects of retrieval, fine-tuning, and baseline performance, we provide a clear empirical basis for understanding how these techniques interact. The comparison focuses on two key aspects of answer quality: factual correctness and lexical overlap with reference answers.

## Method
We evaluated four conditions: the **closed-book model**, which answers each question from what it learned in pre-training, without access to any documents; the **retrieval-augmented model**, which retrieves passages from the reference collection and conditions its answer on them; the **fine-tuned model**, which was trained further on in-domain question and answer pairs; and **RAG without reranking**, which uses the same retriever but keeps the passages in their original order. The primary benchmark for this study is **HistoryQuest**, a dataset comprising 1,000 questions focused on historical facts. We also evaluated performance on **SciFactBench**, a secondary dataset of 1,000 questions covering scientific facts. For each condition and dataset, we measured two metrics: **answer accuracy**, defined as the proportion of questions answered with correct facts, and **ROUGE-L**, which measures the longest common subsequence overlap with the reference answer. All models were run with identical decoding parameters to ensure comparability.

## Discussion
The experimental design allowed for a direct comparison of the four conditions under identical evaluation protocols. A limitation of this study is the reliance on two specific datasets, which may not fully capture the diversity of real-world question answering scenarios. The use of **ROUGE-L** as a metric provides insight into lexical similarity but does not fully account for semantic equivalence. Furthermore, the **RAG without reranking** condition serves as a control for the impact of passage ordering, isolating the effect of retrieval itself from the effect of ranking algorithms. The study design ensures that differences in performance can be attributed to the specific architectural choices rather than variations in data preprocessing or model initialization. Future work could expand the dataset coverage to include more diverse domains and question types.

## Conclusion
This study compared the performance of closed-book, retrieval-augmented, fine-tuned, and unranked RAG models on two factual question answering benchmarks. The results demonstrate distinct performance profiles for each condition across different metrics and datasets. **Grounding the generator in retrieved documents yields more accurate answers.** These findings suggest that the integration of external retrieval mechanisms is a significant factor in the generation of factually correct responses. Further research is needed to explore the scalability of these methods and their applicability to other language tasks.

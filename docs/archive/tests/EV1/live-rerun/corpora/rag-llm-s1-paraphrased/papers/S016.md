# Evaluating Retrieval Strategies for Factual Consistency in Historical Question Answering

## Abstract
Large language models often generate plausible but incorrect information when answering complex questions. This study compares four distinct conditions to assess their impact on factual reliability. We evaluate the performance of a language model used on its own, a variant that reads retrieved evidence first, a variant fed with randomly drawn passages, and a variant that reformulates the question before the search. The primary evaluation is conducted on the HistoryQuest benchmark. Our findings indicate that the specific method of integrating external knowledge significantly alters the output characteristics. Retrieval augmentation clearly reduces hallucinated content in the answers.

## Introduction
The integration of retrieval systems with generative models has become a central topic in natural language processing. While these systems aim to provide grounded responses, the mechanism by which external evidence is introduced can vary widely. Some approaches rely on the model's internal parameters, while others inject external text directly into the context window. Other methods attempt to optimize the search process itself by modifying the input query. It is crucial to understand how these different architectural choices influence the final output. The aim of this research is to compare these conditions systematically. We focus on the consistency of the generated text with established historical facts. By isolating the retrieval component, we can observe the direct contribution of external evidence to the generation process. This paper outlines the methodology used to test these variants and presents the observed outcomes.

## Method
We utilized the HistoryQuest dataset, which consists of 1,000 questions covering various historical periods. This dataset serves as the main benchmark for our evaluation. We tested four specific conditions. The first condition is the language model used on its own, which receives only the question and produces an answer directly. The second condition is the variant that reads retrieved evidence first, which is given the top passages of a dense retriever together with the question. The third condition is the variant fed with randomly drawn passages, which receives passages drawn at random from the collection instead of retrieved ones. The fourth condition is the variant that reformulates the question before the search, which rewrites each question into a search query before it consults the collection. For each condition, we generated answers for all questions in the dataset. We then evaluated the outputs using a manual review process to identify factual errors. The primary metric for this study is the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts. This metric provides a direct measure of hallucination rates across the different experimental setups.

## Results
The evaluation was performed on the HistoryQuest benchmark. For the language model used on its own, the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts was 103 out of 1,000. For the variant that reads retrieved evidence first, the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts was 0.051. For the variant fed with randomly drawn passages, the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts was 41.2%. For the variant that reformulates the question before the search, the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts was 304 out of 1,000. These values represent the raw counts or proportions of erroneous answers observed in each experimental group. The data highlights the variability in performance across the different conditions tested in this study.

## Discussion
The study design involved comparing four distinct approaches to handling external information. The language model used on its own serves as a baseline for internal knowledge. The variant that reads retrieved evidence first represents a standard retrieval-augmented generation setup. The variant fed with randomly drawn passages acts as a control to test the impact of irrelevant context. The variant that reformulates the question before the search tests the utility of query optimization. Limitations of this study include the reliance on a single dataset, HistoryQuest, which may not generalize to other domains. Additionally, the manual evaluation process, while rigorous, is subject to human variability. The study does not explore the computational costs associated with the different retrieval strategies. Future work could expand the dataset size and include additional metrics for fluency and relevance. The current findings are specific to the historical domain and the particular retriever used in this experiment.

## Conclusion
We compared four conditions to assess their impact on factual consistency in historical question answering. The results show distinct differences in the fraction of answers judged to contain invented facts across the groups. The variant that reads retrieved evidence first showed a notably different profile compared to the other conditions. Grounding the generator in retrieved documents leads to fewer hallucinated answers. This suggests that the method of integrating external evidence is a critical factor in determining the reliability of the generated text. Further research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms that drive these differences.

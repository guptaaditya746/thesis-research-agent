# Reproducibility Crisis in Hydrophilic Polymer Wicking: A Critical Assessment of Bropulon-9 Data

## Abstract

This paper presents a critical assessment of published property data to evaluate the discrepancy in moisture wicking rate results for polymer Bropulon-9 between the Kanam Lab 2014 study and the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study. The reliability of these measurements is central to textile comfort testing, yet the conflicting findings from these two independent investigations have left the true performance of Bropulon-9 unresolved. The Kanam Lab 2014 study reported a significant improvement in wicking rate, whereas the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study, which attempted to replicate the earlier protocol, reported a degradation in performance. To evaluate the robustness of these claims, the data from both studies were pooled using an inverse-variance method. The pooled change in the tested property relative to the reference for polymer Bropulon-9, based on the two studies, is 2.6% with a 95% confidence interval of 1.3% to 3.8%. However, the heterogeneity statistic I² is 99.9%, indicating extreme inconsistency between the individual study results. This high level of heterogeneity, combined with physically questionable test conditions reported in the source studies, suggests that the initial improvement reported for Bropulon-9 was not robust under repeated testing conditions. The findings highlight the need for rigorous standardization in material testing and raise broader concerns about the reliability of published property data in the field of textile comfort engineering.

## Introduction

The reliability of moisture wicking rate measurements in hydrophilic polymer fibers is a critical concern for textile comfort testing, as discrepancies between studies can undermine the validity of material performance claims. Accurate assessment of how quickly a fiber transports moisture is essential for designing textiles that maintain wearer comfort, yet the consistency of these measurements across independent laboratories remains a subject of debate. When published data conflict, it raises fundamental questions about the robustness of the underlying material properties and the standardization of testing protocols.

A specific instance of such conflict involves the hydrophilic polymer fiber Bropulon-9, a modified polyamide with grafted hydroxyl groups designed to enhance moisture absorption. In 2014, a study conducted by the Kanam Lab reported a significant improvement in the moisture wicking rate of Bropulon-9, suggesting that the material’s chemical modifications effectively accelerated water transport along the fiber axis. This finding was initially interpreted as a promising advancement in fiber engineering for high-performance textiles.

However, the reliability of this initial positive finding was challenged in 2016 when the Mebrikim Lab attempted to replicate the results. The Mebrikim Lab study, which employed the same AATM 1957 capillary rise test protocol, failed to replicate the positive wicking findings of the Kanam Lab 2014 study. Instead, the 2016 study reported a degradation in the moisture wicking rate for Bropulon-9, indicating that the material performed worse than the reference standard under their testing conditions. This direct contradiction between the two studies leaves the true performance of Bropulon-9 unresolved.

The failure of the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study to confirm the earlier results has broader implications for reliability in textile comfort testing. It suggests that the initial improvement reported for Bropulon-9 may not be robust under repeated testing conditions, potentially due to variations in sample preparation, environmental controls, or other methodological factors. Such inconsistencies erode confidence in published property data and complicate the selection of materials for industrial applications where performance predictability is paramount.

To address this uncertainty, a critical assessment of the published property data from both the Kanam Lab 2014 and Mebrikim Lab 2016 studies is necessary. By pooling the results and evaluating the heterogeneity between the two datasets, this study aims to resolve the discrepancy and provide a clearer understanding of the moisture wicking behavior of Bropulon-9. This analysis not only clarifies the specific case of Bropulon-9 but also underscores the importance of rigorous replication and data synthesis in validating material performance claims.

The contributions of this study are as follows: (1) a comparative analysis of the moisture wicking rate data for polymer Bropulon-9 from the 2014 and 2016 studies; (2) a pooled statistical assessment to determine the robustness of the initial findings; and (3) a discussion of the broader implications for reliability in textile comfort testing.

## Background

### 2.1 Initial Positive Findings

Initial studies on moisture wicking rate in hydrophilic polymer fibers have often reported improvements in water transport efficiency following chemical modifications or structural changes. For instance, the Kanam Lab 2014 study reported an improvement in the moisture wicking rate for polymer Bropulon-9, attributing this enhancement to the grafted hydroxyl groups that increase hydrophilicity. Similar positive findings were observed for other materials, such as polymer Pedenes-2, where a core-shell structure was credited with rapid wicking capabilities. These initial reports have been influential in shaping expectations for material performance in textile applications. However, the robustness of these findings under repeated testing conditions remains questionable, as suggested by subsequent replication attempts that have not consistently confirmed the initial results.

### 2.2 Subsequent Replication Attempts

Replication studies are essential for validating initial findings, yet they often encounter challenges due to differences in testing conditions, sample sizes, or methodological variations. The Mebrikim Lab 2016 study serves as a notable example, where the attempt to replicate the Kanam Lab 2014 results for Bropulon-9 led to a failure to confirm the initial positive wicking findings. Instead, the 2016 study reported a degradation in performance, with potential sources of discrepancy including variations in test temperature or sample preparation. This study addresses the limitation by critically assessing the published property data from both studies, comparing their setups and results to identify potential sources of discrepancy. By pooling the results, this analysis evaluates the robustness of the initial findings and provides a more comprehensive understanding of the moisture wicking behavior of Bropulon-9.

## Methods of the Assessment

This assessment involves a critical review and statistical pooling of published property data from three distinct laboratory studies: the Kanam Lab 2014 study, the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study, and the Putro Lab 2018 study. The primary focus is on the moisture wicking rate of polymer Bropulon-9, with secondary attention to polymer Pedenes-2 and polymer Vuger-8 to provide context on the consistency of findings across different hydrophilic materials. The Kanam Lab 2014 study and the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study both utilized the AATM 1957 capillary rise test to measure the speed of water transport along the fiber axis under standard humidity conditions. The Putro Lab 2018 study, which evaluated air permeability using the ASTM D737 test, is included to assess the broader reliability of the Kanam Lab 2014 findings in a different property domain. No new experiments were conducted; all data are derived from the published reports of these studies.

The statistical analysis involved pooling the effect sizes from the Kanam Lab 2014 and Mebrikim Lab 2016 studies for polymer Bropulon-9 using an inverse-variance method. The effect size is defined as the change in the tested property relative to the reference, expressed as a percentage, where positive values indicate an improvement. The heterogeneity between the studies was quantified using the I² statistic, which measures the proportion of total variability across studies that is due to heterogeneity rather than chance. This approach allows for a quantitative evaluation of the consistency of the published data and the robustness of the initial findings.

In contrast, the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study reported a degradation in the moisture wicking rate for the same polymer, with a change of -11.8%. The pooled analysis for polymer Bropulon-9, combining the data from the Kanam Lab 2014 and Mebrikim Lab 2016 studies, yields an estimated change of 2.6% with a 95% confidence interval of 1.3% to 3.8%. However, the heterogeneity statistic I² is 99.9%, indicating that the vast majority of the variability in the effect sizes is due to differences between the studies rather than random error. This extreme heterogeneity suggests that the two studies are not measuring the same underlying effect, and the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution. The wide discrepancy between the individual study results (34.1% vs. -11.8%) and the high I² value confirm that the initial improvement reported for Bropulon-9 was not robust under the conditions of the replication study.

## Critical Discussion

The primary disagreement in the published data concerns the moisture wicking rate of polymer Bropulon-9. The Kanam Lab 2014 study reported an improvement, while the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study reported a degradation. This contradiction is not merely a matter of statistical noise; the effect sizes are in opposite directions, with the Kanam Lab 2014 study showing a positive change of 34.1% and the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study showing a negative change of -11.8%. The failure of the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study to replicate the Kanam Lab 2014 study is a significant finding, as it suggests that the initial positive result may have been an artifact of specific experimental conditions or sample characteristics that were not maintained in the replication.

A critical examination of the reported test conditions reveals a significant physical inconsistency. The Kanam Lab 2014 study reports a test temperature of 600°C, and the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study reports a test temperature of 800°C. Standard capillary rise tests, such as the AATM 1957 protocol, typically involve the transport of liquid water under standard humidity conditions. At temperatures exceeding the boiling point of water at standard atmospheric pressure, water exists in a gaseous state, making liquid capillary rise physically impossible. This discrepancy suggests either a fundamental error in the reported data, a non-standard protocol not detailed in the published reports, or a misinterpretation of the test conditions. The Putro Lab 2018 study, which reports a test temperature of 400°C for air permeability, also operates at temperatures well above the boiling point of water, further complicating the physical interpretation of the data. This physical implausibility casts doubt on the validity of the underlying measurements and suggests that the reported temperatures may not accurately reflect the conditions under which the wicking rate was measured.

The high heterogeneity I² of 99.9% in the pooled analysis for Bropulon-9 further supports the conclusion that the two studies are not consistent with each other. In meta-analysis, such extreme heterogeneity typically indicates that the studies are not measuring the same underlying effect, and pooling the results is generally considered inappropriate. The pooled estimate of 2.6% should therefore not be interpreted as a representative value for the moisture wicking rate of Bropulon-9, but rather as a mathematical artifact of the inverse-variance method applied to two contradictory datasets. The fact that the Putro Lab 2018 study also failed to replicate the Kanam Lab 2014 study, albeit in a different property (air permeability), suggests a broader issue with the robustness of the Kanam Lab 2014 findings.

The evidence from this assessment indicates that the initial improvement reported for Bropulon-9 was not robust under repeated testing conditions. The pooled estimate of 2.6% is statistically significant but is heavily influenced by the extreme heterogeneity between the two studies. This suggests that the true effect of Bropulon-9 on moisture wicking rate may be highly context-dependent, varying significantly with testing conditions. The broader implication for textile comfort testing is that published property data must be critically evaluated for consistency and robustness before being used to make material selection decisions. The discrepancy in the Bropulon-9 data serves as a cautionary example of the importance of replication and standardization in material science research.

## Conclusions

This critical assessment of published property data reveals a significant discrepancy in the moisture wicking rate results for polymer Bropulon-9 between the Kanam Lab 2014 study and the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study. The Kanam Lab 2014 study reported an improvement, while the Mebrikim Lab 2016 study reported a degradation, and the latter failed to replicate the former. The pooled analysis of these two studies yields an estimated change of 2.6% with a 95% confidence interval of 1.3% to 3.8%, but the heterogeneity I² of 99.9% indicates extreme inconsistency between the studies. These findings suggest that the initial improvement reported for Bropulon-9 was not robust under repeated testing conditions, likely due to variations in experimental parameters such as test temperature.

The broader implications for reliability in textile comfort testing are substantial. The discrepancy in the Bropulon-9 data highlights the need for rigorous standardization of testing protocols and the importance of replication studies in validating material performance claims. Researchers and industry practitioners should approach published property data with a critical eye, considering the consistency of findings across multiple studies before drawing conclusions about material performance. This assessment contributes to the ongoing discussion on the reproducibility crisis in materials science and provides a framework for evaluating the robustness of published data in the field of textile comfort engineering.

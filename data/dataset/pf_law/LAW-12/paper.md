# Health Data and Gaming Metrics: A Comparative Analysis of Lawful Basis

## Abstract

This paper examines the consistency of judicial interpretation regarding the lawful basis for processing personal data under Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. By analyzing six Court of Appeal decisions issued between 2015 and 2021, the study investigates whether the application of 'legitimate interest' varies between health data and behavioral data in gaming. The analysis reveals that the courts have consistently relied on Section 2 to validate processing in both sectors, indicating a unified legal standard rather than divergent sector-specific interpretations. The findings suggest that the judiciary evaluates the lawfulness of data processing based on factual circumstances, such as security measures and the context of data sharing, rather than the inherent sensitivity of the data type. This unified approach provides a stable framework for data controllers in non-financial sectors, promoting legal predictability. The paper argues that this consistency supports the view that Section 2 functions as a general principle of data privacy law in Tetre, applicable across diverse industries. Future jurisprudence should continue to focus on these factual distinctions to maintain the coherence of the legal framework. This doctrinal analysis contributes to the understanding of how statutory provisions are applied in practice, offering insights for legal practitioners and policymakers navigating the complexities of data privacy in an increasingly digital landscape.

## 1. Introduction

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes a statutory framework for the lawful processing of personal data, with Section 2 serving as a pivotal provision for determining valid bases for such processing. While the provision is broad in scope, its application has been tested across various sectors, raising questions about the consistency of judicial interpretation. Specifically, the legal community has observed a growing body of case law involving both health data and behavioral data in gaming, two sectors that differ significantly in the nature of the data processed and the potential risks to individuals. This divergence in sectoral context has led to speculation that courts might apply different standards or interpretations of 'legitimate interest' depending on the industry involved.

This study examines a corpus of six Court of Appeal decisions issued between 2015 and 2021, which collectively address the application of Section 2 in both health and gaming contexts. These decisions provide a critical window into how the judiciary navigates the complexities of data privacy in non-financial sectors. By focusing on this specific timeframe and set of cases, the analysis aims to clarify whether the legal reasoning remains consistent across these distinct domains or if it shifts in response to sector-specific concerns. The selection of these cases allows for a detailed exploration of the judicial approach to lawful basis in situations where the data is not primarily financial in nature.

The central thesis of this paper is that the Court of Appeal and District Court have consistently relied on Section 2 to validate processing in both health and gaming sectors, suggesting a unified approach to 'legitimate interest' in non-financial contexts. This argument posits that despite the different types of data involved, the underlying legal principles applied by the courts remain stable and coherent. The consistency observed in these rulings indicates that the judiciary views the lawful basis for processing as a general principle rather than a sector-specific rule, thereby promoting legal predictability for data controllers across various industries.

To support this thesis, the methodology employed is a doctrinal analysis of the holdings in chronological order. This approach allows for the tracing of the evolution of legal reasoning over time, highlighting how earlier decisions have influenced later ones. By examining the sequence of rulings, the study can identify patterns in how the courts have interpreted and applied Section 2, as well as any shifts in emphasis or focus that may have occurred. This chronological analysis provides a clear narrative of the development of the legal framework in Tetre.

The paper will defend the thesis of unity while addressing the strongest counter-arguments regarding sector-specific distinctions. Critics may argue that the nature of health data, which is inherently sensitive, requires a higher threshold for lawful processing compared to gaming data, which is often considered less intrusive. However, the analysis will demonstrate that the courts have not adopted such a bifurcated approach, instead maintaining a consistent standard that applies equally to both sectors. This defense is crucial for understanding the current state of data privacy law in Tetre and its implications for future cases.

The contributions of this study include a clear account of how the decisions treated the relevant sources and an argued view on which line of authority should be followed. By providing a comprehensive analysis of the case law, this paper aims to offer valuable insights for legal practitioners, policymakers, and scholars interested in the intersection of data privacy and sector-specific regulations.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Financial Sector Precedents

Prior judicial approaches to data privacy in Tetre can be clustered into financial sector precedents and early non-financial sector applications. Decisions in the financial sector, such as those involving banking and employee monitoring, often focus on institutional power dynamics. These cases, including Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank, Hosom v. Tavul Bank, and Bretha v. Ladem Bank, typically address the relationship between employers and employees or between financial institutions and their clients. The legal reasoning in these cases may not directly address the nuances of consumer health or gaming data, as the primary concern is often the balance of power within institutional settings. This study uses these financial cases as a baseline to contrast with the specific holdings in health and gaming cases, highlighting whether the legal reasoning transfers across sectors.

### 2.2 Early Non-Financial Applications

Early decisions in non-financial sectors, such as Hemis v. Hador Health and Bronu v. Bibrer Systems, may have established foundational principles for data privacy in health and gaming contexts. However, these initial rulings lacked the comparative depth to determine if a unified standard exists across different non-financial data types. By analyzing the later decisions, such as Grestin v. Gesum Health, which explicitly distinguish or rely on earlier ones, this paper evaluates the consistency of the legal framework over time. This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of how the courts have developed their interpretation of Section 2 in response to evolving technological and societal contexts.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Chronological Analysis of Holdings

The analysis of the case law proceeds in chronological order to trace the development of the legal reasoning regarding Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. The earliest decision in the corpus is Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank (2015), a financial sector case involving a bank that used dark patterns to collect extra data. This decision serves as a baseline for understanding data collection practices in the context of consumer-facing interactions. Although primarily a financial case, it establishes the initial judicial scrutiny of data collection methods that would later be referenced in non-financial contexts.

In 2016, Hemis v. Hador Health addressed the telemedicine context, where a service stored data on unsecured servers. This decision established early health data privacy concerns, focusing on the security of data storage as a critical factor in determining the lawfulness of processing. The court's attention to technical security measures in this case laid the groundwork for later evaluations of health data handling.

The first gaming data precedent in the corpus is Bronu v. Bibrer Systems (2019). In this case, a gaming company used player data for game balancing. The court relied on Section 2 to validate the use of this behavioral data, marking a significant step in applying the provision to the gaming sector. This holding demonstrates that the lawful basis for processing extends to data collected for operational purposes within a game environment, provided the processing aligns with the statutory requirements.

In 2020, two decisions further reinforced the applicability of Section 2 in institutional settings. In Hosom v. Tavul Bank, the court relied on Section 2 in the context of AI monitoring of employee emails. This decision highlighted the provision's relevance in scenarios involving automated data processing within the workplace. Similarly, in Bretha v. Ladem Bank, the court relied on Section 2 for employee performance monitoring. These two cases collectively solidify the role of Section 2 in non-consumer data contexts, particularly where the data subject is an employee and the data controller is an employer.

The most recent decision in the corpus is Grestin v. Gesum Health (2021), which involved a fitness tracker sharing heart rate data with an employer. The court relied on Section 2 to validate this sharing of health data. Notably, this decision distinguished Hemis v. Hador Health, indicating a shift in how health data security and sharing are evaluated. The court found that the facts in Grestin differed sufficiently from those in Hemis to warrant a different outcome, specifically regarding the context of data sharing with an employer versus the security of storage. Furthermore, Grestin distinguished Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank, highlighting the difference between consumer-facing dark patterns and employer-employee data sharing. This distinction underscores the court's focus on the specific factual circumstances rather than the sectoral label of the data controller.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Sector-Specific Distinction Argument

A primary counter-argument to the thesis of a unified approach is that health data and gaming data are fundamentally different in nature and sensitivity, thereby requiring distinct legal standards. Proponents of this view argue that health data, being inherently sensitive and potentially revealing of personal conditions, should be subject to a higher threshold for lawful processing compared to gaming data, which is often considered less intrusive and more behavioral in nature. This argument suggests that the courts should apply a bifurcated standard, with stricter requirements for health data and more lenient standards for gaming data.

However, the case law analyzed in this paper does not support this sector-specific distinction. The consistent reliance on Section 2 in both Grestin v. Gesum Health (health) and Bronu v. Bibrer Systems (gaming) suggests that the courts view the legal basis as sector-agnostic. In both cases, the court found that the processing was lawful under Section 2, without applying a different standard based on the type of data. This consistency indicates that the judiciary does not recognize a fundamental legal difference between health and gaming data in terms of the lawful basis for processing.

Furthermore, the distinctions made in Grestin v. Gesum Health from Hemis v. Hador Health and Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank support the unified approach. In Grestin, the court distinguished earlier cases based on factual differences, such as the context of data sharing and the security measures in place, rather than on the sectoral classification of the data. This approach demonstrates that the courts differentiate based on the specific facts of each case, such as the purpose of processing and the safeguards implemented, rather than on the inherent sensitivity of the data type. By focusing on these factual distinctions, the courts maintain a coherent and unified application of Section 2 across different sectors.

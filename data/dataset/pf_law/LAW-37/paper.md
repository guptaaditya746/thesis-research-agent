# Genetic Data and the Limits of Lawful Basis: A Comparative Analysis

## Abstract

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes a statutory framework for the processing of personal data, creating a tension between the protection of sensitive information and the commercial utility of data in sectors such as insurance and media. This paper examines the applications of the Act’s provisions in *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance* and *Gonustor v. Hekris Media*, focusing on whether consent constitutes a sufficient lawful basis for processing genetic data versus commercial subscriber data. The analysis reveals that both decisions rely on section 1 of the Act to define the data at issue as personal data, and both rely on section 2, which governs lawful basis. *Kuhizun* rejects the sufficiency of consent for genetic data, implying a stricter standard for sensitive information, whereas *Gonustor* upholds consent as a valid basis for commercial media use. The role of *Stagelol v. Bikrem Insurance* in the 2013 context is also considered, noting its focus on the definition of personal data without fully resolving the lawful basis question. The paper argues that the line of authority in *Kuhizun* should be followed for genetic data due to its inherent sensitivity, while *Gonustor* remains valid for less sensitive commercial media data. This distinction is crucial for ensuring adequate protection of sensitive personal information while allowing for the commercial utility of data in less sensitive contexts.

## 1. Introduction

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes a statutory framework for the processing of personal data, creating a tension between the protection of sensitive information and the commercial utility of data in sectors such as insurance and media. While the Act defines personal data and lawful basis, the Tetre District Court has produced divergent applications of these provisions in *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance* and *Gonustor v. Hekris Media*, leaving unclear whether consent is a sufficient lawful basis for processing genetic data versus commercial subscriber data.

The core legal tension lies in the application of lawful basis provisions to genetic data in insurance underwriting versus subscriber data in media cross-promotion. These two contexts present distinct risks to individual autonomy and privacy, yet the statutory language does not explicitly differentiate between them. This ambiguity forces courts to interpret broad provisions in specific factual contexts, leading to potential inconsistencies in how the law is applied across different industries.

Three key statutory provisions form the foundation of this analysis: section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which defines personal data; section 2, which outlines the lawful basis for processing; and section 3, which establishes the right to erasure. These provisions provide the legal vocabulary and structural limits within which the courts operate. The definition of personal data in section 1 is broad enough to encompass both genetic information and commercial subscriber details, while section 2 offers a list of potential lawful bases, including consent, without specifying the conditions under which consent is sufficient for sensitive data.

The relevant judicial decisions include *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance* (2013), *Stagelol v. Bikrem Insurance* (2013), and *Gonustor v. Hekris Media* (2017). *Kuhizun* involved an insurer using genetic data to deny coverage, while *Stagelol* concerned an insurer using health data from a wearable device. *Gonustor* addressed a magazine using subscriber data for cross-promotion. These cases represent the initial judicial interpretations of the Act, with the 2013 decisions focusing on insurance data and the 2017 decision addressing media data.

The thesis of this study is that *Kuhizun* and *Gonustor* both rely on the definition of personal data but diverge in their application of lawful basis. Specifically, *Kuhizun* rejects the sufficiency of consent for genetic data, while *Gonustor* upholds it for commercial media use. This divergence suggests that the courts have not developed a consistent approach to the sensitivity of data types when determining the adequacy of consent as a lawful basis.

This paper contributes a clear account of how the decisions treated the statutory provisions and offers an argued view on which line of authority should be followed. By analyzing the legal framework, the chronological development of the case law, and the counter-arguments to the thesis, this study aims to clarify the current state of the law and provide guidance for future judicial decisions.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Statutory Provisions of the Tetre Data Privacy Act

The legislative framework of the Tetre Data Privacy Act defines personal data, lawful basis, and erasure rights. Section 1 provides a broad definition of personal data, encompassing any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Section 2 outlines the lawful basis for processing, including consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public interest, and legitimate interests. Section 3 establishes the right to erasure, allowing individuals to request the deletion of their personal data under certain conditions. The statutory language regarding 'lawful basis' is broad and does not explicitly differentiate between the sensitivity of genetic data and the commercial nature of subscriber data, leading to interpretive ambiguity. This analysis examines how the courts have interpreted these broad provisions in specific factual contexts to resolve the ambiguity.

### 2.2 Early Judicial Interpretations (2013)

The initial case law applying the Act to insurance data processing includes *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance* and *Stagelol v. Bikrem Insurance*. Both decisions focused on the definition of personal data but did not fully resolve the sufficiency of consent as a lawful basis for sensitive data like genetic information. *Kuhizun* involved an insurer using genetic data to deny coverage, while *Stagelol* concerned an insurer using health data from a wearable device. These early interpretations laid the groundwork for subsequent cases but left open the question of how consent should be applied in the context of sensitive data. This study builds on these early interpretations to analyze the divergence that emerged in the 2017 *Gonustor* decision.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance (2013)

In *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance*, the Tetre District Court addressed the use of genetic data by an insurer to deny coverage. The court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to classify the genetic data as personal data, establishing that such information falls within the scope of the Act’s protections. Furthermore, the court relied on section 2 of the Act to determine the lawful basis for processing this data. In doing so, *Kuhizun* rejected the sufficiency of consent as a lawful basis for processing genetic data, implying a stricter standard for sensitive data. This decision highlights the court’s recognition that genetic data, due to its inherent sensitivity, may require more than mere consent to be processed lawfully. The court’s reasoning suggests that the nature of genetic data, which is immutable and highly personal, necessitates a higher threshold for lawful processing than is required for other types of personal data.

### 3.2 Stagelol v. Bikrem Insurance (2013)

*Stagelol v. Bikrem Insurance* involved an insurer using health data from a wearable device. The court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to classify the health data as personal data. This decision, while contributing to the understanding of what constitutes personal data, did not fully resolve the question of the sufficiency of consent for sensitive data, leaving the issue open for further judicial interpretation.

### 3.3 Gonustor v. Hekris Media (2017)

In *Gonustor v. Hekris Media*, the Tetre District Court considered a magazine’s use of subscriber data for cross-promotion. The court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to classify the subscriber data as personal data. Additionally, the court relied on section 2 of the Act to determine the lawful basis for processing this data, upholding the sufficiency of consent as a lawful basis for processing commercial media data. This decision contrasts with *Kuhizun*, which rejected consent for genetic data. Furthermore, *Gonustor* relied on section 3 of the Act regarding the right to erasure, addressing the individual’s ability to request the deletion of their personal data. This broader reliance on the Act’s provisions in *Gonustor* underscores the court’s approach to commercial media data, which is less sensitive than genetic data. The court’s decision in *Gonustor* reflects a more permissive approach to the use of consent as a lawful basis, justified by the commercial nature of the data and the context of its use.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

A strong counter-argument to the thesis is that *Gonustor* should be followed because it is the more recent decision (2017 vs. 2013) and involves a broader commercial context. They contend that the courts have appropriately tailored the application of lawful basis to the specific context of the data involved, with *Gonustor* representing a more modern and commercially relevant interpretation.

However, this counter-argument fails to account for the inherent sensitivity of genetic data. The sensitivity of genetic data necessitates a stricter standard for lawful basis, as established in *Kuhizun*. The line of authority in *Kuhizun* should be followed for genetic data to ensure adequate protection of sensitive personal information. The broader commercial context of *Gonustor* does not diminish the need for heightened protection of genetic data, which carries significant risks to individual privacy and autonomy. Therefore, the thesis that *Kuhizun* should be followed for genetic data is supported by the distinct nature of the data involved and the need for tailored legal protections.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Summary and Final Position

This paper has analyzed the decisions in *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance*, *Stagelol v. Bikrem Insurance*, and *Gonustor v. Hekris Media* in the context of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. *Kuhizun* rejects the sufficiency of consent for genetic data. *Gonustor* upholds it for commercial media use. *Stagelol* focuses on the definition of personal data without fully resolving the lawful basis question.

The final position is that the stricter standard for genetic data established in *Kuhizun* should be maintained, while the more permissive standard for commercial media data in *Gonustor* is appropriate for its context. This distinction is crucial for ensuring adequate protection of sensitive personal information while allowing for the commercial utility of data in less sensitive contexts. The importance of distinguishing between sensitive and commercial data in the application of the Tetre Data Privacy Act cannot be overstated, as it ensures that the law remains responsive to the evolving nature of data processing and the varying degrees of risk associated with different types of personal data. Data controllers who process both genetic and commercial data should structure their consent mechanisms to reflect this distinction, ensuring that consent for genetic data is obtained under a stricter standard, while consent for commercial data can be more permissive. This approach will help to ensure compliance with the Tetre Data Privacy Act and provide adequate protection for individuals' personal data.

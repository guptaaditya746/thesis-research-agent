# Divergent Judicial Treatment of Data Security and Employee Monitoring in Tetre

## Abstract

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes distinct statutory mechanisms for data security and employee monitoring, yet judicial interpretation has not harmonized their application. This paper examines the divergence in how Tetre courts treat data breach notification obligations under Section 4 versus the lawful basis for employee monitoring under Section 2. By analyzing six key decisions from the Tetre District Court and the Court of Appeal between 2019 and 2024, the study identifies a significant divergence in the case law. Earlier Court of Appeal rulings relied on Section 2 to justify data processing in monitoring contexts, while later District Court decisions rejected the applicability of Section 4 in specific breach-related scenarios. This inconsistency creates legal uncertainty for data controllers and processors, particularly regarding the scope of security obligations and the permissibility of employee data processing. The analysis reveals that the rejection of Section 4 in certain contexts has created a gap in the legal framework for data security, whereas the reliance on Section 2 provides a more consistent basis for evaluating the lawfulness of processing. The paper argues that the line of authority relying on Section 2 for monitoring should be followed, as it offers a clearer and more legally sound framework for data processing in the employee context. This doctrinal resolution aims to restore predictability to the Tetre data privacy regime and align judicial practice with the legislative intent of the Act.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Judicial Interpretation of Breach Notification (Section 4)

Prior judicial approaches to data privacy in Tetre can be clustered into two main lines: those focusing on breach notification and those focusing on lawful processing bases. Decisions interpreting Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act have shown inconsistent application, with some rejecting its applicability in specific contexts. This has led to uncertainty in data security obligations, as courts have not uniformly recognized the scope of breach notification requirements. For instance, in cases involving data security failures, some District Court decisions have refused to accept Section 4 as a basis for their rulings, citing contextual differences that they deemed significant. This approach has created a gap in the legal framework, where data controllers may not be held accountable for breaches in the same manner as intended by the statute. The inconsistency in these decisions highlights the need for a more uniform interpretation of Section 4 to ensure that data security obligations are consistently enforced.

### 2.2 Judicial Interpretation of Lawful Processing (Section 2)

Courts have also relied on Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to justify employee monitoring and other data processing activities. This approach has been used in cases where the primary issue is the lawfulness of data processing rather than the notification of breaches. However, this reliance on Section 2 may not adequately address the specific context of data breach notification, creating a gap in the legal framework. For example, in cases involving employee monitoring, courts have used Section 2 to determine whether the processing of data was lawful, without necessarily considering the implications for data security obligations under Section 4. This separation of issues has led to a fragmented legal landscape where the two provisions are not always considered in tandem. The paper examines how Section 2 was used in key decisions and how this reliance contrasts with the treatment of Section 4 in other cases, highlighting the need for a more integrated approach to data privacy law.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Early Decisions: Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems (2019) and Hosom v. Tavul Bank (2020)

The earliest decisions in this series, rendered by the Tetre Court of Appeal, established a pattern of relying on Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to adjudicate disputes involving data processing. In *Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems* (2019), the court addressed the sale of player behavior data by a gaming company to marketers. The court grounded its ruling in Section 2, treating the provision as the primary basis for evaluating the lawfulness of the data transfer. By focusing on the lawful basis for processing, the decision sidestepped any detailed analysis of breach notification obligations, effectively prioritizing the permissibility of the data flow over security-specific mandates. This approach set a precedent for using Section 2 as a comprehensive tool for assessing data handling practices, regardless of the specific context of the data involved.

A similar approach was adopted in *Hosom v. Tavul Bank* (2020), where the Tetre Court of Appeal examined a bank’s use of artificial intelligence to monitor employee emails. Here again, the court relied on Section 2 to justify the monitoring practices, emphasizing the lawful basis for processing personal data in an employment context. The decision did not engage with Section 4, suggesting that the court viewed the lawfulness of the processing as the central legal question, distinct from any potential breach notification issues. The common thread in these early decisions is the reliance on Section 2 as the decisive statutory provision, focusing on the authorization of data processing rather than the consequences of data security failures. This early jurisprudence established a framework where the validity of data processing was assessed primarily through the lens of Section 2, creating a foundation for subsequent cases to follow or diverge from this approach.

### 3.2 Later Decisions: Thisas v. Ribror Health (2021), Graro v. Drede Media (2022), Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance (2023), and Roham v. Polotrim Systems (2024)

The later decisions, rendered by the Tetre District Court, introduced a shift in judicial reasoning, particularly regarding the treatment of Section 4. In *Thisas v. Ribror Health* (2021), the court considered a case where a health app shared location data with advertisers. The decision distinguished this case from *Garo v. Drede Media*, noting the differences in factual circumstances, specifically the nature of the data and the context of its sharing. By distinguishing the earlier decision, the court signaled a move toward a more fact-specific analysis, where the applicability of statutory provisions was determined by the precise details of the data handling involved.

In *Garo v. Drede Media* (2022), the Tetre District Court addressed a streaming service’s retention of viewing habits after account deletion. The court distinguished this case from *Thisas v. Ribror Health*, again emphasizing the different facts. This mutual distinction between the two cases highlighted the courts’ focus on the specific context of data processing, rather than applying a uniform statutory interpretation.

The shift became more pronounced in *Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance* (2023), where the Tetre District Court rejected Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act as a basis for its ruling. The case involved an insurer using wearable device data to adjust premiums. The court distinguished both *Garo v. Drede Media* and *Thisas v. Ribror Health*, finding that the facts in those cases did not apply to the insurance context. By rejecting Section 4, the court effectively removed the breach notification obligation from the legal analysis, focusing instead on other aspects of the data processing. This decision marked a clear departure from the earlier reliance on Section 2, as the court did not use Section 2 as the primary basis for its ruling, but rather excluded Section 4 from consideration.

In *Roham v. Polotrim Systems* (2024), the Tetre District Court further reinforced this trend by rejecting Section 4 as a basis for its ruling. The case concerned a cloud provider’s failure to secure backup data. The court focused on the security failure itself, but did not rely on Section 4 to impose notification obligations. Instead, the ruling emphasized the provider’s duty to secure data, without engaging with the specific notification requirements of Section 4. This decision, like *Stogretron*, rejected the applicability of Section 4 in a context involving data security, creating a gap in the legal framework for breach notification.

The later District Court decisions thus reveal a pattern of rejecting Section 4 in specific contexts, particularly where the primary issue was data security or the lawfulness of processing rather than the notification of a breach. This shift contrasts with the earlier Court of Appeal decisions, which relied on Section 2 to justify data processing. The divergence between these two lines of authority highlights the conflict in the case law, with the District Court decisions creating uncertainty about the scope of Section 4 and the Court of Appeal decisions providing a more consistent basis for evaluating the lawfulness of data processing.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis that a significant divergence exists in the case law is that the rejection of Section 4 in *Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance* and *Roham v. Polotrim Systems* is justified by the specific facts of those cases. Proponents of this view argue that Section 4 is specifically designed to address data breach notification, and if the facts do not involve a breach, Section 4 does not apply. In *Stogretron*, the issue was the use of wearable data for premium adjustment, which may not constitute a breach in the traditional sense. Similarly, in *Roham*, the focus was on the failure to secure backup data, which is a security issue rather than a breach notification issue. Therefore, the courts were correct to reject Section 4, as it was not the relevant statutory provision for the legal questions at hand. This argument suggests that the case law is not in conflict, but rather reflects a consistent application of statutory provisions based on their specific scope and purpose.

### 4.2 Response to the Counter-Argument

While it is true that Section 4 is specifically about data breach notification, the rejection of Section 4 in *Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance* and *Roham v. Polotrim Systems* creates a gap in the legal framework for data security obligations. The counter-argument assumes that the absence of a breach means that Section 4 is irrelevant, but this overlooks the broader implications of data security failures. This creates uncertainty for data controllers, who may not know whether they are required to notify affected individuals in the event of a security failure that does not immediately constitute a breach.

Furthermore, the line of authority relying on Section 2 for monitoring, as seen in *Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems* and *Hosom v. Tavul Bank*, provides a more consistent and legally sound basis for data processing in the employee context. Section 2 focuses on the lawful basis for processing, which is a broader and more fundamental question than the specific context of a breach. By relying on Section 2, the courts in these cases provided a clear framework for evaluating the permissibility of data processing, which is essential for ensuring that data controllers operate within the bounds of the law. In contrast, the rejection of Section 4 in the later District Court decisions leaves a gap in the legal framework, as it does not provide a clear basis for addressing data security obligations in the absence of a breach.

Therefore, the divergence in the case law should be resolved by following the line of authority that relies on Section 2 for monitoring. This approach provides a clearer and more consistent legal framework, as it focuses on the fundamental question of the lawfulness of data processing, rather than the specific context of a breach. By prioritizing Section 2, the courts can ensure that data controllers are held accountable for the lawfulness of their data processing practices, while also providing a clear basis for addressing data security obligations in a manner that is consistent with the legislative intent of the Tetre Data Privacy Act.

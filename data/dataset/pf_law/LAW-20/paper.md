# Geolocation Data and National Risk Assessment

## Abstract

The insurance sector increasingly relies on data-driven methods for both fraud prevention and risk pricing, creating a tension between operational efficiency and data privacy protections. This paper examines the legal standards governing the use of real-time geolocation data for fraud detection and census data for risk assessment under the Tetre Data Privacy Act. The central question is whether the Supreme Court has drawn a distinction between these two types of data processing or if a uniform standard applies. This study posits that the Supreme Court has established that census data for risk assessment is distinct from real-time geolocation data for fraud detection, applying different standards of lawful basis. To determine the prevailing legal standard, this paper analyzes four key decisions: Sadepo v. Rubrothes Bank, Dibres v. Hastas Bank, Krodos v. Pavus Media, and Stonan v. Tredum Insurance. By examining how these decisions interpreted and applied the provisions of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, this study aims to clarify the legal landscape surrounding the use of geolocation and census data in the insurance sector. The analysis reveals that the Supreme Court in Stonan v. Tredum Insurance applied a specific standard for census data. This distinction has significant practical implications for how insurers must structure their data processing activities to ensure compliance with the Tetre Data Privacy Act. The paper concludes that insurers must apply different lawful basis tests depending on whether they are using census data for risk pricing or geolocation data for fraud detection.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Static Data Retention and Definition

Prior judicial approaches to data privacy in the financial and media sectors can be grouped into two clusters: those focusing on the retention of static data and those focusing on the active use of surveillance or location data. Decisions focusing on the definition of personal data and post-relationship retention, such as Sadepo v. Rubrothes Bank, address the retention of data after a relationship ends. These cases primarily concern the obligations of data controllers to delete or anonymize personal data once the original purpose for its collection has been fulfilled. However, these decisions do not clarify the lawful basis for active, real-time processing of sensitive data like geolocation. This study bridges this gap by analyzing how the Supreme Court in Stonan v. Tredum Insurance distinguished these static retention issues from the active use of census data for risk assessment.

### 2.2 Active Surveillance and Fraud Detection

The second cluster of decisions focuses on the use of real-time or surveillance data for operational purposes. Cases like Dibres v. Hastas Bank and Krodos v. Pavus Media address the use of geolocation or surveillance data but do not explicitly compare these standards to the use of census data for risk pricing. Dibres v. Hastas Bank, for instance, involved a bank using geolocation data for fraud detection, while Krodos v. Pavus Media concerned a state broadcaster using surveillance data for news stories. These rulings highlight the complexities of balancing operational needs with privacy rights in the context of active data processing. This study synthesizes these active surveillance rulings with the census data rulings to demonstrate the distinct legal standards applied by the Supreme Court, thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of the legal framework governing data processing in the insurance sector.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Early Precedents: Retention and Surveillance

The earliest decision in this line of authority is Sadepo v. Rubrothes Bank, a 2010 ruling by the Tetre District Court. In this case, a bank retained customer data after account closure. The decision is foundational in establishing the boundaries of data retention but is limited to the context of post-relationship data handling. This limitation is significant because it leaves open the question of how the Tetre Data Privacy Act applies to ongoing data processing activities that are not merely about retention.

The next relevant decision is Krodos v. Pavus Media, a 2013 ruling by the Tetre Supreme Court. In this case, a state broadcaster used surveillance data for news stories. The Court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to define the data as personal. This decision is important because it confirms that surveillance data falls within the scope of personal data under the Act.

The most recent decision in this cluster is Dibres v. Hastas Bank, a 2025 ruling by the Tetre Court of Appeal. In this case, a bank used geolocation data for fraud detection. The Court relied on both section 1 and section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, establishing a standard for real-time geolocation processing. This decision is significant because it is the first to explicitly address the lawful basis for processing geolocation data in the context of fraud detection. The Court's reliance on section 2 indicates that the lawful basis for processing geolocation data is a critical factor in determining the permissibility of such processing.

### 3.2 The Supreme Court's Distinction: Census Data

The pivotal decision in this line of authority is Stonan v. Tredum Insurance, a 2014 ruling by the Tetre Supreme Court. In this case, a national insurer used census data for risk assessment. The Court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to classify census data as personal data. This classification is consistent with the earlier decision in Krodos v. Pavus Media, which also relied on section 1 to define surveillance data as personal. However, the Court in Stonan went further by relying on section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to determine the lawful basis for this specific processing. This reliance on section 2 is crucial because it indicates that the lawful basis for processing census data is a distinct issue from the lawful basis for processing geolocation data.

Crucially, the Court in Stonan distinguished Sadepo v. Rubrothes Bank, indicating that the retention issue in Sadepo did not apply to the active risk assessment in Stonan. This distinction is significant because it shows that the Court is willing to draw factual distinctions that affect the legal standard applied. By distinguishing Sadepo, the Court in Stonan effectively created a new line of authority for the use of census data for risk assessment, separate from the line of authority for the use of geolocation data for fraud detection.

Synthesizing these holdings, it is clear that the Supreme Court in Stonan applied a specific standard for census data. The Court's reliance on section 2 in both cases, but in different factual contexts, supports the thesis that the lawful basis for processing census data is distinct from the lawful basis for processing geolocation data. This distinction has significant practical implications for insurers, who must now apply different lawful basis tests depending on the type of data they are processing.

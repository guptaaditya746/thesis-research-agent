# Tax Data and Reader Data: The Boundary of Personal Information

## Abstract

This paper examines whether state tax data used for insurance premium calculation and commercial reader data used for content personalization constitute the same category of personal data under Section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. By analyzing the judicial decisions in *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance* and *Budrem v. Gokraka Media*, this study demonstrates that while both forms of data are currently treated as valid personal data under the statutory definition, the nature of their processing differs significantly in terms of intrusiveness. *Hapar* involved the use of sensitive state tax data by a national insurer, whereas *Budrem* concerned the use of voluntary commercial reader data by a news outlet. Both courts relied on Section 1 to classify the respective data as personal, and both invoked Section 2 to establish a lawful basis for processing. However, *Hapar* additionally engaged with Section 3 regarding the right to erasure, highlighting the heightened sensitivity of tax data. The paper argues that the current uniform application of Section 1 obscures the distinct privacy risks associated with state versus commercial data sources. A differentiated legal approach, which accounts for the intrusiveness of the data source, is necessary to ensure that the privacy framework adequately protects individuals from the more severe implications of state data processing. This analysis calls for a more nuanced interpretation of personal data that reflects the varying degrees of privacy impact inherent in different data contexts.

## 1. Introduction

The definition of personal data in Section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act serves as the foundational gatekeeper for privacy protection, determining which data flows are subject to statutory regulation. As the primary mechanism for identifying protected information, this provision establishes the boundary between private information and data that may be processed without specific statutory constraints. The clarity and scope of this definition are therefore critical to the effective operation of the entire privacy framework, as they dictate the reach of subsequent rights and obligations.

Two recent judicial decisions illustrate the practical application of this definition in distinct contexts. In *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance*, the Tetre Supreme Court addressed the use of state tax data by a national insurer for the purpose of premium calculation. In *Budrem v. Gokraka Media*, the Tetre Court of Appeal considered the use of commercial reader data by a news outlet for content personalization. Both cases involved the processing of information that could be linked to specific individuals, yet the nature of the data and the entities involved differed significantly.

The central issue in both cases was whether the data in question constituted personal data under Section 1. The courts in both instances relied on the statutory definition to determine that the data was indeed personal data, thereby bringing the processing activities within the scope of the Act. This uniform application of the definition suggests that the current legal framework treats state tax data and commercial reader data as belonging to the same category of personal information, regardless of the source or the specific context of the processing.

However, this uniform treatment may obscure the significant difference in privacy impact between state and commercial data processing. State tax data, by its nature, involves sensitive financial information collected by the government, which carries a higher degree of intrusiveness and potential for harm if misused. In contrast, commercial reader data, while personal, is typically generated through voluntary interactions with a service and may be perceived as less intrusive. The current statutory definition does not explicitly account for these differences in intrusiveness, leading to a potential gap in the legal framework.

This paper argues that the current uniform treatment of state tax data and commercial reader data under Section 1 may be insufficient to address the varying levels of privacy risk associated with each. By analyzing the decisions in *Hapar* and *Budrem*, this study highlights the need for a more nuanced approach that considers the nature and source of the data in determining the appropriate legal standard. The goal is to provide a clear account of how the decisions treated these sources and to offer an argued view on which line of authority should be followed to better protect individual privacy.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a detailed analysis of the judicial interpretations of Section 1 in the context of state and commercial data processing. Second, it identifies the limitations of the current uniform approach and argues for a differentiated legal standard that accounts for the intrusiveness of the data. Third, it offers a framework for future judicial and legislative consideration to enhance the protection of personal data in the Tetre legal system.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Statutory Interpretation of Personal Data

Prior judicial interpretations of the Tetre Data Privacy Act have established the baseline for what constitutes personal data. The broad textual definition in Section 1 has been applied uniformly in cases such as *Stonan v. Tredum Insurance* and *Budrem v. Gokraka Media*. In *Stonan*, the Tetre Supreme Court relied on Section 1 to determine that census data used for risk assessment constituted personal data. Similarly, in *Budrem*, the Tetre Court of Appeal applied the same definition to reader data used for content personalization. These decisions reflect a textual and purposive interpretation of Section 1 that focuses on the ability to link data to an individual, without sufficient consideration of the context or source of the data. This uniform application has led to a lack of doctrinal clarity regarding whether state and commercial data should be treated differently.

### 2.2 Lawful Basis and Erasure Rights

Decisions have also focused on the application of Sections 2 and 3 to specific data types. In *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance*, the Tetre Supreme Court relied on Section 2 to determine the lawful basis for processing tax data and on Section 3 to address the right to erasure. In *Budrem*, the Tetre Court of Appeal similarly relied on Section 2 to assess the lawful basis for processing reader data. These decisions highlight the importance of establishing a lawful basis for processing and respecting the right to erasure. However, they have not adequately distinguished the severity of the processing involved, particularly in cases involving state data. This paper argues that a more nuanced reading is needed, one that accounts for the intrusiveness of the data source and the nature of the processing, to determine the appropriate legal standard.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Stonan v. Tredum Insurance (2014)

The earliest of the relevant decisions, *Stonan v. Tredum Insurance*, was rendered by the Tetre Supreme Court in 2014. The case involved a national insurer that utilized census data for the purpose of risk assessment. The court’s primary task was to determine whether such data fell within the scope of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. In doing so, the court grounded its ruling in Section 1 of the Act, using the statutory definition to establish that census data, when linked to individuals, constitutes personal data. This reliance on Section 1 set a precedent for interpreting the scope of personal data broadly, focusing on the identifiability of the data subject rather than the origin of the data. The decision in *Stonan* thus established a baseline for subsequent cases, reinforcing the idea that the source of the data—whether state or commercial—does not, in itself, exclude the data from the definition of personal information. This approach has been influential in shaping the uniform application of Section 1 in later cases, including *Budrem* and *Hapar*.

### 3.2 Budrem v. Gokraka Media (2012)

Although *Budrem v. Gokraka Media* was decided in 2012, it is analyzed here in the context of its application to commercial data. The Tetre Court of Appeal addressed a dispute involving a news outlet that used reader data for content personalization. The court had to determine whether this reader data constituted personal data and whether its processing was lawful. In its ruling, the court relied on Section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to establish that reader data, which could be linked to specific individuals, met the criteria for personal data. Furthermore, the court invoked Section 2 of the Act to assess the lawful basis for the processing of this data. The decision in *Budrem* is significant because it applied the same statutory framework to commercial data as had been applied to state data in *Stonan*. By relying on Section 1 and Section 2, the court affirmed that the processing of commercial reader data is subject to the same privacy protections as other forms of personal data. However, the decision did not explicitly address the right to erasure under Section 3, nor did it distinguish the intrusiveness of commercial data from that of state data.

### 3.3 Hapar v. Brapil Insurance (2019)

The most recent decision, *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance*, was issued by the Tetre Supreme Court in 2019. The case concerned a national insurer that used state tax data for the calculation of insurance premiums. The court’s analysis was more extensive than in previous cases, as it addressed not only the classification of the data but also the lawful basis for its processing and the implications for the right to erasure. The court relied on Section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to classify the tax data as personal data, consistent with the approach taken in *Stonan* and *Budrem*. Additionally, the court relied on Section 2 to justify the processing of this sensitive data, acknowledging the need for a robust lawful basis given the nature of the information. Crucially, the court also engaged with Section 3 of the Act, addressing the right to erasure in the context of tax data. The court’s decision in *Hapar* thus reflects a more nuanced application of the statutory framework, recognizing that the processing of state data may require additional safeguards, particularly regarding the right to erasure.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Uniformity Argument

A primary counter-argument to the thesis that state and commercial data should be treated differently is the principle of legal certainty. Proponents of this view argue that the statutory definition of personal data in Section 1 should be applied uniformly to all data types to ensure consistency and predictability in the law. If courts were to distinguish between state and commercial data based on intrusiveness, it could lead to inconsistent application of the law, as the boundaries between different types of data may be blurred. For example, data that is initially commercial in nature may later be used by state entities, or vice versa. A uniform approach avoids the complexity of categorizing data based on its source or context, thereby simplifying compliance for data processors and ensuring that all personal data is protected under the same standard. This argument is supported by the fact that both *Budrem* and *Hapar* relied on Section 1 to classify their respective data as personal, suggesting that the courts have adopted a consistent approach to the definition of personal data.

### 4.2 Rebuttal Based on Intrusiveness

While the uniformity argument has merit, it fails to account for the significant differences in the privacy impact of state and commercial data. State tax data, as seen in *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance*, involves sensitive financial information collected by the government, which carries a higher degree of intrusiveness and potential for harm if misused. In contrast, commercial reader data, as seen in *Budrem v. Gokraka Media*, is typically generated through voluntary interactions with a service and may be perceived as less intrusive. The decision in *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance* itself acknowledges this difference by engaging with section 3 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to address the right to erasure. This suggests that the court in *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance* recognized the heightened sensitivity of state tax data and the need for additional safeguards. A differentiated approach, which accounts for the intrusiveness of the data source, would better protect individual privacy rights by ensuring that more sensitive data is subject to stricter legal standards. This approach does not undermine legal certainty but rather enhances it by providing a clearer framework for handling different types of personal data. By distinguishing between state and commercial data, the law can more effectively address the varying levels of privacy risk associated with each, thereby fulfilling the purpose of the Tetre Data Privacy Act.

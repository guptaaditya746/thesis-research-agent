# The Boundary of Erasure Rights in Telemedicine and Clinical Settings

## Abstract

This paper presents a theoretical exercise in legal reasoning using a hypothetical jurisdiction, examining whether the right to erasure under section 3 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act applies to data stored on unsecured servers or within clinical files. The central thesis is that this right constitutes a fundamental statutory obligation that cannot be waived by storage conditions or clinical necessity. To evaluate this proposition, the study analyzes six judicial decisions rendered by the Tetre Court of Appeal and the Tetre District Court between 2013 and 2016. The analysis focuses on how these decisions treated the statutory provision and how they related to one another, specifically regarding the reliance on or distinction of prior rulings. The findings indicate that the Court of Appeal and District Court decisions collectively support the non-waivability of the right to erasure. In particular, the decisions involving telemedicine services and clinical practices explicitly grounded their rulings in section 3, rejecting arguments that security failures or medical utility negate the statutory duty to delete. The paper concludes that a clear line of authority has emerged within this hypothetical framework, prioritizing the statutory right over operational or clinical considerations. This doctrinal analysis provides a framework for understanding the boundaries of erasure rights in contexts where data security and clinical necessity are contested.

## 1. Introduction

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes a statutory right to erasure, yet its application to specific storage conditions and clinical contexts remains legally contested. While the general principle of data subject control is widely accepted, the boundaries of this right become ambiguous when data is held in environments that do not meet standard security protocols or when the data is integral to ongoing clinical care. This ambiguity creates significant uncertainty for both data controllers and individuals seeking to exercise their rights.

A central area of legal uncertainty concerns whether the right to erasure under section 3 can be effectively waived by the manner in which data is stored or by the necessity of retaining clinical records. Recent judicial decisions have offered distinct treatments of these issues, leaving practitioners without a clear doctrinal framework. Specifically, it is unclear if storing data on unsecured servers negates the obligation to erase, or if clinical necessity provides a valid exception to the statutory requirement for deletion.

This study posits that the right to erasure is a fundamental statutory obligation that is not subject to waiver by storage conditions or clinical necessity. The analysis argues that the core purpose of section 3 is to ensure individual autonomy over personal data, a principle that should remain intact regardless of the technical security of the storage medium or the professional context in which the data is held. By treating the right as absolute in these specific contexts, the law maintains its integrity and prevents entities from using procedural or practical justifications to circumvent individual rights.

To support this thesis, the paper examines six key judicial decisions rendered between 2013 and 2016. These cases span both the Tetre Court of Appeal and the Tetre District Court, providing a focused view of how different levels of the judiciary have interpreted section 3. The analysis focuses on how each decision treated the statutory provision and how they related to one another, particularly in distinguishing or relying on prior rulings. This chronological and hierarchical approach allows for a clear identification of the prevailing legal trends within this limited sample.

The review of these decisions reveals a consistent line of authority from the higher courts that supports the non-waivability of the right to erasure. The Court of Appeal and District Court decisions, when read together, suggest that neither the lack of security in data storage nor the clinical utility of the data serves as a valid basis for refusing an erasure request. This study previews the argument that these decisions collectively establish a robust legal standard that prioritizes the statutory right over operational or clinical considerations.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a clear account of how the six key decisions treated the relevant statutory sources. Second, it offers an argued view on which line of authority should be followed, advocating for the interpretation that upholds the absolute nature of the right to erasure. Third, it clarifies the legal boundaries of section 3 in the context of unsecured servers and clinical files, offering guidance for future legal practice and policy development within the hypothetical framework.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Procedural Violations and Notification

Prior judicial approaches to data privacy rights in Tetre can be clustered into two main categories: those focusing on procedural violations and those addressing substantive data use. Decisions focusing on procedural violations often center on failures to notify users of data breaches or to secure data adequately. For instance, cases involving tech companies that failed to notify users of a data breach have primarily addressed the procedural aspect of data protection. These decisions tend to treat security and notification as separate from the core right to erasure. This approach potentially allows entities to argue that erasure is secondary to procedural compliance, creating a gap in the absolute nature of the right. However, this study argues that procedural failures do not negate the substantive right to erasure, as seen in decisions that relied on section 3 despite security issues.

### 2.2 Substantive Data Use and Clinical Contexts

The second cluster of decisions addresses the use of data for insurance or clinical purposes. These cases often involve scenarios where data is used for risk assessment or clinical care, raising questions about the applicability of the right to erasure. Decisions addressing the use of data for insurance or clinical purposes may implicitly or explicitly allow for exceptions to erasure based on clinical necessity or insurance risk assessment. This creates a gap in the absolute nature of the right, as entities may argue that the data is necessary for their operations. However, this study contends that clinical necessity does not override the statutory right to erasure, as demonstrated by decisions that relied on section 3 in the context of clinical files. This approach ensures that the right to erasure remains a fundamental obligation, regardless of the specific use of the data.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Early Decisions: 2013-2014

The earliest decisions in this period did not directly address the right to erasure under section 3, but they established the factual and legal contexts that later cases would confront. In *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance* (2013), the Tetre District Court addressed a scenario where an insurer used genetic data to deny coverage. While this decision focused on the substantive use of data for insurance risk assessment, it did not explicitly engage with the statutory right to erasure. Instead, it highlighted the potential for data misuse in contexts where personal information is leveraged for commercial or actuarial purposes. Similarly, in *Bruse v. Kikom Systems* (2014), the Tetre District Court examined a case where a tech company failed to notify users of a data breach. This decision centered on the procedural obligation to notify rather than the substantive right to delete data. By focusing on notification failures, *Bruse* treated security and procedural compliance as distinct from the core erasure right. These early decisions, while not directly adjudicating section 3, underscored the importance of data security and the risks associated with unauthorized data use, setting the stage for later cases that would explicitly invoke the right to erasure.

### 3.2 Court of Appeal Decisions: 2015-2016

The Tetre Court of Appeal provided the first explicit judicial interpretations of section 3 in the context of data storage and collection practices. In *Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank* (2015), the Court of Appeal addressed a bank that used dark patterns to collect extra data from users. The court grounded its ruling in section 3 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, affirming that the right to erasure applies to data collected through such means. This decision marked a significant step in establishing section 3 as a tool for challenging data collection practices that undermine user autonomy. 

In *Hemis v. Hador Health* (2016), the Court of Appeal confronted a more direct challenge to the thesis: a telemedicine service stored data on unsecured servers. The court explicitly relied on section 3 to support the right to erasure, rejecting the argument that the lack of security in the storage medium negated the statutory obligation to delete. This decision was pivotal in clarifying that storage conditions do not serve as a valid exception to the right to erasure. Furthermore, *Hemis* distinguished *Bruse v. Kikom Systems*, arguing that the failure to notify users of a data breach did not negate the right to erasure. By distinguishing *Bruse*, the court clarified that procedural failures in notification are separate from the substantive right to delete data. Additionally, *Hemis* distinguished *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance*, holding that the use of genetic data for insurance purposes did not negate the right to erasure. This distinction reinforced the idea that the substantive use of data, whether for insurance or clinical purposes, does not override the statutory right to erasure.

### 3.3 District Court Decisions: 2015-2016

The Tetre District Court decisions in 2015 and 2016 reinforced the Court of Appeal's position that the right to erasure is not subject to waiver by storage conditions or clinical necessity. In *Hutheno v. Pagre Bank* (2015), the District Court addressed a bank that sold transaction history to third parties. The court relied on section 3 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to support the right to erasure, affirming that the sale of data to third parties does not negate the data subject's right to request deletion. This decision aligned with the Court of Appeal's approach in *Tagustu*, emphasizing that the right to erasure applies regardless of how the data is used or shared. 

In *Pugram v. Povul Health* (2016), the District Court confronted a clinic that refused to delete patient files upon request. The court explicitly relied on section 3 to support the right to erasure, rejecting the argument that clinical necessity provided a valid exception to the statutory requirement for deletion. This decision was crucial in clarifying that the clinical context does not override the right to erasure. By relying on section 3, the court affirmed that the right to erasure is a fundamental statutory obligation that applies to clinical files, just as it does to data stored on unsecured servers. Together, these District Court decisions reinforced the Court of Appeal's position, creating a consistent line of authority that prioritizes the statutory right to erasure over operational or clinical considerations.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis is that the right to erasure should be subject to exceptions for clinical necessity and data security, as these are essential to the functioning of healthcare and financial services. Proponents of this view argue that allowing erasure in clinical contexts could compromise patient care, while allowing erasure in the face of data security failures could undermine the integrity of data systems. This counter-argument is supported by the early decisions, such as *Kuhizun v. Trefezer Insurance* and *Bruse v. Kikom Systems*, which did not explicitly address the right to erasure. By focusing on the substantive use of data and procedural failures, respectively, these decisions implicitly suggested that the right to erasure might be secondary to other considerations, such as insurance risk assessment or data security. 

However, this counter-argument is weak because it is not supported by the more recent and authoritative decisions. The Court of Appeal and District Court decisions, particularly *Hemis v. Hador Health* and *Pugram v. Povul Health*, explicitly relied on section 3 to support the right to erasure, rejecting the arguments that storage conditions or clinical necessity negate the statutory obligation. These decisions demonstrate that the right to erasure is a fundamental statutory obligation that cannot be waived by storage conditions or clinical necessity. The early decisions, while important in establishing the factual and legal contexts, did not provide a basis for waiving the right to erasure, and their implicit suggestions have been superseded by the explicit holdings of the later decisions.

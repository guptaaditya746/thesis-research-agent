# The Divergent Paths of Genetic and Biometric Data in Tetre Jurisprudence

## Abstract

This paper examines the divergent judicial treatment of genetic and biometric data under the Tetre Data Privacy Act. By analyzing six key decisions from 2017 to 2025, including *Mizun v. Manostal Health* and *Stegrepor v. Hakruze Bank*, the study identifies a significant inconsistency in how sensitive personal data is protected. The Tetre Supreme Court, in *Trostotham v. Grefesel Health*, applied a strict standard to genetic data, prioritizing the inherent sensitivity of the information over the purpose of its processing. Conversely, the Tetre District Court, in *Stegrepor v. Hakruze Bank*, adopted a flexible interpretation for biometric verification, allowing the functional context to mitigate the level of protection required. This divergence creates a fragmented legal landscape where the protection of sensitive data depends on the court and the specific data category involved. The paper argues that this inconsistency undermines the statutory baseline established by section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. To ensure robust and uniform protection of individuals' most intimate information, the strict standard established by the Supreme Court should be preferred and applied consistently across all sensitive data categories, regardless of the processing context.

## 1. Introduction

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes a statutory baseline for the handling of personal data, yet the judicial interpretation of 'sensitive' categories remains fragmented. While the legislation provides a foundational definition, the practical application of these definitions has led to divergent outcomes in the courts. This fragmentation is particularly evident in the treatment of data that reveals intimate aspects of an individual's identity, such as genetic and biometric information. The lack of a unified judicial approach creates uncertainty for data controllers and potential gaps in the protection of individuals whose most sensitive information is processed.

The core legal issue addressed in this study is the treatment of genetic and biometric data under the Tetre Data Privacy Act. Specifically, the analysis focuses on how the courts have interpreted the scope of protection afforded to these distinct categories of sensitive data. The relevant statutory provision is section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which defines personal data. This definition serves as the primary legal anchor for all subsequent judicial reasoning regarding the sensitivity and protection of data subjects' information. The interpretation of this single section has, however, yielded different results depending on the court and the specific type of data involved.

This analysis is based on a corpus of six key decisions spanning the period from 2017 to 2025. These cases include *Mizun v. Manostal Health*, *Pedim v. Detam Media*, *Trostotham v. Grefesel Health*, *Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance*, *Sostohen v. Vatros Systems*, and *Stegrepor v. Hakruze Bank*. The decisions are drawn from both the Tetre Supreme Court and the Tetre District Court, providing a comparative view of how different levels of the judiciary have approached the same statutory language. The facts of these cases involve a range of data processing activities, from public health research and media surveillance to insurance risk assessment and financial transaction verification.

The central thesis of this study is that the Supreme Court has established a strict standard for genetic data, while the District Court has applied a more flexible interpretation to biometric verification. This divergence suggests that the level of protection afforded to sensitive data is not solely determined by the nature of the data itself, but also by the context in which it is processed and the court hearing the case. The Supreme Court's approach appears to prioritize the inherent sensitivity of genetic information, whereas the District Court's approach considers the functional purpose of the data processing, such as verification, in determining the level of protection required.

The argument is structured to first provide a chronological analysis of the holdings in the selected cases, examining how each decision relied on or distinguished the statutory provision and prior case law. This is followed by a defense of the thesis against potential counter-arguments. By tracing the evolution of judicial reasoning over time, this study aims to clarify the current state of the law and identify the points of tension between the two interpretive lines. The analysis will demonstrate how the application of section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act has led to a bifurcated legal landscape for sensitive data.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a clear account of how the six selected decisions treated the statutory definition of personal data. Second, it identifies and articulates the divergent judicial approaches to genetic and biometric data. Third, it offers an argued view on which line of authority should be followed to ensure consistent protection of sensitive personal data in Tetre.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 The Inherent Sensitivity Standard

The Tetre Supreme Court has generally adopted a strict constructionist approach to the definition of sensitive data under the Data Privacy Act. This methodology relies on a literal interpretation of the statutory text, focusing on the inherent nature of the data category rather than the context of its use. In this view, certain types of data, such as genetic information, are considered sensitive by their very nature, and the protection afforded to them is not contingent on the purpose for which they are processed. This approach creates a rigid boundary around sensitive data, ensuring a high level of protection regardless of the specific application. However, this strictness may fail to account for the evolving nature of data processing technologies, particularly in cases where data is used for verification rather than identification. This study examines how this strict line was applied in *Trostotham v. Grefesel Health* and *Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance* to establish the baseline for genetic and travel data, respectively.

### 2.2 The Functional Context Standard

In contrast, the Tetre District Court has employed a more functionalist approach to interpreting data sensitivity. This method involves a contextual interpretation of the data, where the purpose of processing plays a significant role in determining the level of protection required. Under this approach, data that might be considered sensitive in one context may be treated differently if it is used for a specific, limited purpose, such as transaction verification. This flexibility allows for a more nuanced application of the law, but it also risks diluting the protection of sensitive data by allowing the purpose of processing to override the inherent sensitivity of the data type. This study analyzes how this flexible line was applied in *Stegrepor v. Hakruze Bank* and *Sostohen v. Vatros Systems* to contrast with the Supreme Court's stance, highlighting the potential inconsistencies that arise from this divergent interpretive framework.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Early Precedents: Mizun and Pedim

The initial judicial engagement with sensitive data in Tetre occurred in the late 2010s, setting the stage for the later divergence in interpretive approaches. In *Mizun v. Manostal Health* (2017), the Tetre Supreme Court addressed a scenario where a public health agency used contact tracing data for research purposes. The court's decision in this case established an early precedent regarding the handling of health-related data, though it did not explicitly define the boundaries of sensitivity in the same manner as later cases. Similarly, in *Pedim v. Detam Media* (2018), the Tetre Supreme Court considered the use of facial recognition technology by a state media outlet in public spaces. This decision touched upon the intersection of surveillance and personal data, but like *Mizun*, it did not fully articulate the strict standard that would later emerge. These early cases provided the factual and legal backdrop against which the Supreme Court would later refine its position on sensitive data, particularly in the context of genetic information.

### 3.2 The Supreme Court's Strict Standard: Trostotham and Trabror

The pivotal shift in the Supreme Court's jurisprudence occurred in *Trostotham v. Grefesel Health* (2022). In this case, a national health service used genetic data for population studies. The court grounded its ruling in section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, interpreting the definition of personal data to impose a strict standard on the processing of genetic information. Crucially, the court distinguished *Mizun v. Manostal Health*, holding that the facts of contact tracing for research did not apply to the processing of genetic data for population studies. This distinction signaled a move away from the more permissive approach seen in earlier cases, emphasizing the unique sensitivity of genetic data. Furthermore, the court distinguished *Pedim v. Detam Media*, indicating that the use of facial recognition in public spaces was not analogous to the processing of genetic data. By drawing these distinctions, the Supreme Court established that the type of data, rather than the purpose of processing, was the primary determinant of the level of protection required.

This strict interpretation was reinforced in *Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance* (2024). In this decision, a national insurer used travel data for risk assessment. The court again relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, applying the same rigorous standard to travel data as it had to genetic data in *Trostotham*. This consistency in the Supreme Court's approach across different data categories—genetic and travel—underscores the court's commitment to a strict construction of sensitive data, regardless of the specific context in which the data is used. The reliance on section 1 in both *Trostotham* and *Trabror* demonstrates a coherent judicial philosophy that prioritizes the inherent sensitivity of the data over the functional purpose of its processing.

### 3.3 The District Court's Flexible Interpretation: Sostohen and Stegrepor

In contrast to the Supreme Court's strict standard, the Tetre District Court has adopted a more flexible interpretation of sensitive data. In *Sostohen v. Vatros Systems* (2024), a tech company used user data for ad targeting. The court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act but applied a more lenient view, suggesting that the purpose of ad targeting did not necessarily trigger the highest level of protection. This decision marked a departure from the Supreme Court's approach, introducing a functionalist element into the analysis of data sensitivity.

The most significant divergence occurred in *Stegrepor v. Hakruze Bank* (2025). In this case, a bank used biometric data for transaction verification. The court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act but treated biometric verification as less sensitive than genetic data. By focusing on the purpose of verification, the District Court effectively lowered the level of protection required for biometric data, arguing that its use in a limited, functional context mitigated its inherent sensitivity. This approach stands in stark contrast to the Supreme Court's strict standard, which would likely have afforded biometric data the same level of protection as genetic data, regardless of the purpose of processing. The divergence between *Stegrepor* and *Trostotham* highlights the potential inconsistency in the protection of sensitive personal data, where the outcome depends on the court and the specific data category involved.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis is that the District Court's flexible interpretation in *Stegrepor v. Hakruze Bank* is justified because biometric data used for verification serves a different purpose than genetic data used for population studies. Proponents of this view argue that the purpose of processing should influence the level of protection, not just the type of data. Under this perspective, biometric data used for transaction verification is less invasive and less likely to reveal intimate details about an individual's identity compared to genetic data used for population studies. Therefore, a lower level of protection is appropriate for biometric data in this context. This argument suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach to sensitive data is too rigid and fails to account for the nuances of different data processing activities.

### 4.2 Rebuttal from the Holdings

This counter-argument is rebutted by the holdings in *Trostotham v. Grefesel Health*, where the Supreme Court distinguished both *Mizun v. Manostal Health* and *Pedim v. Detam Media*. By distinguishing these earlier cases, the Supreme Court indicated that the type of data (genetic) is the primary factor, not the purpose of processing. The court's decision to impose a strict standard on genetic data, regardless of the purpose, demonstrates a clear preference for a data-centric approach over a purpose-centric one. This approach ensures that sensitive data is protected consistently, regardless of the context in which it is used. The strict standard in *Trostotham v. Grefesel Health* and *Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance* should be followed to ensure consistent protection of sensitive personal data. The divergence between the Supreme Court and District Court creates a legal uncertainty that undermines the Data Privacy Act, as data controllers and individuals are left unsure of the level of protection that applies to their data. To resolve this uncertainty, the strict standard established by the Supreme Court should be adopted as the prevailing interpretation of section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Final Argument

The Tetre courts have treated genetic and biometric data differently, with the Supreme Court applying a strict standard and the District Court applying a flexible interpretation. This divergence creates an inconsistency in the protection of sensitive personal data, undermining the statutory baseline established by the Tetre Data Privacy Act. The key holdings in *Trostotham v. Grefesel Health* and *Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance* relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to impose strict standards, while *Stegrepor v. Hakruze Bank* and *Sostohen v. Vatros Systems* relied on the same provision but applied a more flexible view. To ensure consistent protection of sensitive personal data, the strict standard established by the Supreme Court should be followed. This approach prioritizes the inherent sensitivity of the data over the purpose of processing, providing a clear and uniform framework for the protection of individuals' most intimate information. By adopting the Supreme Court's strict standard, Tetre can resolve the current legal uncertainty and ensure that the Data Privacy Act fulfills its purpose of protecting sensitive personal data.

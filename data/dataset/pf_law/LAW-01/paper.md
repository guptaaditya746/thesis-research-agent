# The Divergent Paths of Data Monetization in Media and Systems

## Abstract

The commercial exploitation of user data by private entities has become a central point of legal scrutiny in Tetre, raising questions about the boundaries of privacy and consent. While courts have addressed data monetization in both media and systems contexts, it remains unclear whether the legal basis for liability is uniform or diverges based on the nature of the data and the consent mechanisms involved. This paper analyzes six decisions from 2012 to 2020, focusing on their use of sections 1 and 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. Section 1 defines personal data, while section 2 establishes the lawful basis for processing. The analysis reveals that the legal basis for liability differs significantly between media and systems companies. Decisions involving media companies, such as Laper v. Dravun Media and Gothukur v. Didota Insurance, relied on section 1 to determine whether the data collected qualified as personal. In contrast, decisions involving systems companies, such as Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems and Hosom v. Tavul Bank, relied on section 2 to evaluate the lawfulness of the processing itself. This divergence suggests that the legal framework adapts to the specific characteristics of the data and the consent mechanisms involved. The paper argues that the line of authority focusing on the lawful basis for processing (section 2) should be followed for systems companies, while the definition of personal data (section 1) remains relevant for media companies. This distinction provides a more nuanced framework for addressing the specific risks posed by data monetization in different contexts.

## 1. Introduction

The commercial exploitation of user data by private entities has become a central point of legal scrutiny in Tetre, raising fundamental questions about the boundaries of privacy and consent. As digital platforms and traditional media organizations increasingly monetize user information, the legal landscape has struggled to keep pace with the evolving nature of data collection and processing. This shift has prompted courts to examine whether existing statutory frameworks are sufficient to address the specific risks posed by data monetization, particularly when the data involved ranges from behavioral metadata to sensitive personal identifiers.

The scope of this inquiry focuses on the treatment of user data commercialization by two distinct categories of organizations: media companies and systems companies. This categorization is not based on the corporate sector of the defendant but rather on the *nature of the data processing* and the *mechanisms of consent*. Media entities, such as newspapers, magazines, and insurers accessing public posts, often collect data through direct user interaction or direct access to identifiable information. In contrast, systems companies, including gaming platforms, banks, and other financial institutions, typically gather data through automated monitoring, algorithmic processing, or the aggregation of behavioral data. This distinction is crucial, as it influences the type of data collected and the mechanisms through which consent is obtained or implied. For instance, an insurer accessing social media posts to assess claims is categorized here as a 'media' context because the data is directly identifiable and accessed for specific, individualized purposes, similar to a newspaper tracking a journalist. Conversely, a bank using AI to monitor employee emails is a 'systems' context because the data is processed algorithmically and at scale.

The relevant statutory provisions for this analysis are found in the Tetre Data Privacy Act. Section 1 defines personal data, establishing the scope of information protected under the law. Section 2 outlines the lawful basis for processing, specifying the conditions under which data can be collected, stored, and used. These two sections form the backbone of the legal framework within which courts have adjudicated data privacy disputes. The interplay between these provisions is central to understanding how liability is determined in cases involving data monetization. While Section 1 is a threshold requirement for any privacy claim, the *determinative* basis for liability often diverges depending on the context.

The thesis of this study is that the legal basis for liability differs significantly between media and systems companies based on the nature of the data and the consent mechanisms employed. While both types of organizations face scrutiny for their data practices, the courts have applied different statutory provisions as the primary basis for their rulings. Media companies are more likely to be held liable under section 1, which focuses on the definition of personal data, whereas systems companies are more often subject to liability under section 2, which addresses the lawful basis for processing. This divergence suggests that the legal framework is not uniform but rather adapts to the specific characteristics of the data and the consent mechanisms involved.

The structure of this paper follows a chronological analysis of six key decisions, beginning with Lofilom v. Rizal Media in 2012 and ending with Hosom v. Tavul Bank in 2020. Each decision is examined in the context of the statutory provisions it relies on or distinguishes, providing a clear account of how the courts have interpreted and applied the Tetre Data Privacy Act. It is important to note that this analysis is based on a limited sample of six decisions, spanning both the Tetre District Court and the Tetre Court of Appeal. While the sample size is small, the consistency of the statutory interpretation across different court levels and over an eight-year period supports the identification of a doctrinal trend. Following this analysis, the paper defends the thesis by highlighting the patterns in the case law and addresses potential counter-arguments that suggest a more uniform approach to data privacy liability.

The contributions of this study include a clear account of how the decisions treated the relevant statutory provisions and an argued view on which line of authority should be followed. By examining the divergent paths of data monetization in media and systems contexts, this paper aims to provide a nuanced understanding of the legal framework governing data privacy in Tetre and to offer insights for future legislative and judicial developments.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 The Definition of Personal Data

Prior judicial approaches to data privacy have often focused on the definition of personal data as a primary point of contention. This approach involves a detailed statutory interpretation of section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which defines the scope of information protected under the law. Courts have used this provision to determine whether the data collected by an organization qualifies as personal data, thereby triggering the protections of the Act. This method is particularly relevant in media contexts, where the data collected is often directly linked to identifiable individuals, such as through facial recognition or metadata tracking. For example, in cases involving media companies, courts have relied on section 1 to establish that the data in question is personal, thus subjecting the organization to the requirements of the Act. However, this approach may be too narrow, as it focuses primarily on what constitutes personal data rather than how it is processed or consented to. This study examines how courts have applied section 1 in media contexts, such as Laper v. Dravun Media and Gothukur v. Didota Insurance, to assess whether it adequately addresses the specific risks of data monetization.

### 2.2 The Lawful Basis for Processing

Another significant line of judicial approach focuses on the lawful basis for processing, as outlined in section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. This provision specifies the conditions under which data can be collected, stored, and used, emphasizing the importance of consent and other lawful bases for processing. Courts have used this section to evaluate whether an organization’s data practices are justified under the Act, particularly in systems contexts where data is often collected through automated means. For instance, in cases involving systems companies, courts have relied on section 2 to determine whether the organization had a valid basis for processing the data, such as explicit consent or a legitimate interest. This approach may be too broad, as it can fail to distinguish between different types of data and consent mechanisms, leading to inconsistent outcomes. This study examines how courts have applied section 2 in systems contexts, such as Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems and Hosom v. Tavul Bank, to evaluate whether it provides a more nuanced framework for data monetization.

It is important to clarify the relationship between these two sections. Section 1 and Section 2 are not mutually exclusive; rather, they represent different stages of the legal analysis. Establishing that data is 'personal' under Section 1 is a prerequisite to evaluating the 'lawful basis' under Section 2. However, the *determinative* basis for liability often diverges. In media contexts, the dispute often centers on whether the data qualifies as personal at all, making Section 1 the critical provision. In systems contexts, the data is often clearly personal, and the dispute shifts to whether the processing was lawful, making Section 2 the critical provision. This paper argues that this divergence is appropriate and reflects the different nature of the privacy harms in each context.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Early Decisions: Lofilom v. Rizal Media and Drusethis v. Thuthiki Insurance

The earliest decisions in this line of authority set the stage for the subsequent divergence in legal reasoning. In Lofilom v. Rizal Media (2012), the Tetre Court of Appeal addressed a case where a newspaper used metadata to track a journalist's movements. The court's analysis focused on the nature of the data collected, treating the metadata as a form of personal information that required protection. This decision established a precedent for examining the scope of personal data in media contexts. However, this approach was later challenged by subsequent rulings that emphasized the lawfulness of processing over the definition of the data itself.

Similarly, in Drusethis v. Thuthiki Insurance (2013), the Tetre Court of Appeal considered a case where an insurer used credit scores to determine life insurance rates. The court again focused on the nature of the data, treating credit scores as personal data that could be subject to privacy protections. This decision reinforced the idea that the definition of personal data was a critical factor in determining liability. However, like Lofilom v. Rizal Media, this decision was later distinguished by Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems, which shifted the focus to the lawful basis for processing.

### 3.2 Media Context: Laper v. Dravun Media and Gothukur v. Didota Insurance

In the media context, the courts continued to rely on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to determine liability. In Laper v. Dravun Media (2017), the Tetre District Court addressed a case where a magazine used facial recognition to identify readers. The court relied on section 1 to establish that the facial recognition data constituted personal data, thereby triggering the protections of the Act. This decision highlighted the importance of the definition of personal data in media contexts, where the data collected is often directly linked to identifiable individuals.

In Gothukur v. Didota Insurance (2018), the Tetre District Court considered a case where an insurer accessed social media posts to assess claims. The court again relied on section 1 to determine that the social media posts constituted personal data. This decision reinforced the pattern established in Laper v. Dravun Media, where the focus was on the nature of the data rather than the lawfulness of its processing. It is worth noting that while Didota Insurance is an insurance company, it is categorized here as a 'media' context because the data was accessed directly and used for individualized assessment, similar to the direct identification methods used by media companies. These decisions suggest that in media contexts, the courts are more likely to focus on the definition of personal data as the primary basis for liability.

### 3.3 Systems Context: Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems and Hosom v. Tavul Bank

In the systems context, the courts shifted their focus to section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. In Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems (2019), the Tetre Court of Appeal addressed a case where a gaming company sold player behavior data to marketers. The court relied on section 2 to evaluate the lawfulness of the processing, rather than focusing on the definition of the data. This decision distinguished Lofilom v. Rizal Media and Drusethis v. Thuthiki Insurance, arguing that the earlier decisions were not applicable because the facts differed. The court emphasized that the lawfulness of the processing was the critical factor in determining liability in systems contexts.

In Hosom v. Tavul Bank (2020), the Tetre Court of Appeal considered a case where a bank used AI to monitor employee emails. The court again relied on section 2 to evaluate the lawfulness of the processing. This decision reinforced the pattern established in Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems, where the focus was on the lawful basis for processing rather than the definition of the data. These decisions suggest that in systems contexts, the courts are more likely to focus on the lawfulness of the processing as the primary basis for liability.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis is that the legal basis for liability should be uniform across all types of organizations, regardless of the nature of the data or consent mechanisms. This argument suggests that the Tetre Data Privacy Act should be applied consistently, with the same statutory provisions used to determine liability in both media and systems contexts. Proponents of this view argue that a uniform approach would provide greater legal certainty and prevent the development of divergent lines of authority.

However, the holdings in the case law suggest that the courts have not adopted a uniform approach. The decisions in systems contexts, such as Setrisas v. Tribrim Systems and Hosom v. Tavul Bank, consistently relied on section 2 to evaluate the lawfulness of the processing. In contrast, the decisions in media contexts, such as Laper v. Dravun Media and Gothukur v. Didota Insurance, relied on section 1 to determine the nature of the data. This divergence in legal basis indicates that the courts have recognized the different characteristics of the data and the consent mechanisms involved in media and systems contexts. The holdings in the case law support the thesis that the legal basis for liability differs significantly between media and systems companies based on the nature of the data and the consent mechanisms employed.

Furthermore, a uniform approach would face practical difficulties. If a uniform Section 2 approach were applied to media cases, it might fail to capture the specific privacy harms of direct identification, as the focus would shift to the lawfulness of processing rather than the scope of personal data. Conversely, if a uniform Section 1 approach were applied to systems cases, it might be insufficient to address the complex consent issues involved in automated and algorithmic processing. The dual-track approach, therefore, provides a more nuanced and effective framework for addressing the specific risks posed by data monetization in different contexts.

# Security Failures and the Rejection of Statutory Safe Harbors

## Abstract

This paper examines whether technical security failures constitute a rejection of statutory compliance standards under the Tetre Data Privacy Act. By analyzing four key judicial decisions—*Fepogre v. Kragrehom Systems*, *Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank*, *Zibrogre v. Lifur Systems*, and *Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank*—the study investigates the tension between procedural adherence to statutory text and the substantive reality of data breaches. The central question is whether entities that follow the letter of the law are shielded from liability when specific security breaches occur, or if such failures negate the applicability of general safe harbors. The analysis reveals that all four decisions refused to accept Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which mandates data breach notification, as a basis for their rulings. Instead, the courts prioritized the substantive harm caused by the security failures over the procedural mechanics of notification. In *Fepogre*, the court grounded its judgment in Section 1 (definition of personal data) and Section 2 (lawful basis for processing), further distancing the ruling from the notification provision. This consistent refusal to rely on Section 4 across different factual scenarios, including encryption lapses, dark patterns, and unauthorized data sharing, signals a significant shift in judicial interpretation. The findings suggest that the courts are moving away from formalistic compliance checks toward a substantive harm-focused approach. This trend indicates that procedural compliance is insufficient to shield entities from liability when the underlying security of personal data is compromised. The paper argues that this line of authority should be followed, as it better aligns with the protective intent of data privacy laws by ensuring actual security rather than mere procedural checklists.

## 1. Introduction

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes a statutory framework for the protection of personal data, including specific provisions for breach notification and lawful processing. Central to this framework are Section 1, which defines the scope of personal data, Section 2, which outlines the lawful basis for processing, and Section 4, which mandates specific procedures for data breach notification. These provisions collectively aim to balance the utility of data processing with the rights of individuals to privacy and security. However, the practical application of these statutes has raised significant questions regarding the sufficiency of procedural adherence in the face of technical failures.

A critical gap in the current legal understanding is whether technical security failures, such as encryption lapses or system vulnerabilities, constitute a rejection of the statutory safe harbors provided by general data protection provisions. Specifically, it remains unclear if an entity that procedurally complies with the letter of the law is shielded from liability when substantive security breaches occur. This tension between substantive security harm and procedural statutory compliance forms the central legal question addressed in this study. The ambiguity lies in determining whether the existence of a compliant policy is sufficient to negate liability, or if the actual failure to protect data overrides such formal compliance.

To address this issue, this paper examines four key judicial decisions that have shaped the interpretation of the Tetre Data Privacy Act: *Fepogre v. Kragrehom Systems*, *Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank*, *Zibrogre v. Lifur Systems*, and *Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank*. These cases involve distinct factual scenarios, ranging from software companies failing to encrypt user passwords to banks using dark patterns to collect extra data, and government systems failing to protect citizen records. By analyzing how these courts treated the relevant statutory provisions, particularly Section 4, we can identify emerging trends in judicial reasoning. It is important to note that this analysis is limited to these four decisions within the Tetre jurisdiction; while the sample is small, the consistency of the judicial approach across different court levels and factual contexts provides a strong basis for identifying a doctrinal shift.

The analysis reveals a consistent judicial trend in these decisions that prioritizes substantive harm over procedural compliance. The courts have effectively refused to accept the applicability of general safe harbors when specific security breaches occur, indicating that mere procedural adherence is insufficient to shield entities from liability. This shift suggests a move toward a more substantive interpretation of data protection laws, where the actual security of data is paramount.

This study contributes to the legal discourse by providing a clear account of how these decisions treated the statutory sources and by arguing for the adoption of a substantive harm-focused approach. The findings suggest that the line of authority established by these decisions should be followed, as it better aligns with the protective intent of the Tetre Data Privacy Act.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Procedural Compliance Approaches

Prior doctrinal approaches to data privacy in Tetre have often focused on strict procedural adherence to statutory text. This methodology relies on a textualist interpretation of statutory safe harbors, assuming that if an entity follows the letter of the law, such as having a data protection policy, it is compliant. However, this approach has a significant limitation: it ignores the substantive reality of technical failures. By focusing solely on formal compliance, it fails to account for the actual security breaches that may occur despite procedural adherence. This paper argues that recent case law moves away from this formalism by looking at the actual security breach, thereby challenging the sufficiency of procedural compliance as a defense against liability.

### 2.2 Substantive Harm Approaches

An alternative doctrinal approach focuses on the outcome of data processing and the resulting user harm. While this substantive harm focus is intuitive and aligns with the protective intent of data privacy laws, it has historically lacked a clear doctrinal basis in the Tetre Data Privacy Act. The recent decisions analyzed in this paper demonstrate how the courts have begun to operationalize this substantive harm focus. By refusing to accept general provisions in favor of specific security accountability, the courts have provided a clearer framework for assessing liability based on the actual harm caused by data breaches. This shift marks a significant development in the legal interpretation of data privacy in Tetre.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Fepogre v. Kragrehom Systems (2014)

The first decision in this series, *Fepogre v. Kragrehom Systems*, was rendered by the Tetre Court of Appeal in 2014. The case involved a software company that failed to encrypt user passwords, a technical security failure that exposed sensitive personal data. The central legal question was whether the company’s failure to encrypt constituted a breach of the Tetre Data Privacy Act and, if so, which provisions applied. The court refused to accept Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which governs data breach notification, as a basis for its ruling. Instead, the court grounded its judgment in Section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which defines the scope of personal data, and Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which outlines the lawful basis for processing. By relying on these foundational provisions, the court suggested that the failure to encrypt was not merely a procedural lapse in notification but a fundamental breach of the lawful basis for processing personal data. This approach indicates that the court viewed the security failure as undermining the very legitimacy of the data processing, rather than simply triggering a notification obligation.

### 3.2 Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank (2015)

In *Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank*, also decided by the Tetre Court of Appeal in 2015, the facts involved a bank that used dark patterns to collect extra data from users. Dark patterns refer to user interface designs that manipulate users into making choices that are not in their best interest, such as consenting to data collection they did not intend. The court declined to rely on Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act for its decision. The focus on the method of collection, specifically the use of dark patterns, suggests that the issue was the lawfulness of the data collection process itself, rather than the notification of a subsequent breach. By rejecting the applicability of the notification provision, the court emphasized that the primary violation lay in the manner in which the data was obtained, which falls under the domain of lawful basis for processing. This decision reinforces the idea that procedural compliance with notification requirements does not cure substantive violations in the data collection process.

### 3.3 Zibrogre v. Lifur Systems (2015)

The Tetre Supreme Court addressed a similar issue in *Zibrogre v. Lifur Systems* in 2015. The case involved a government IT system that failed to protect citizen records, leading to a significant data breach. The court did not treat Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act as a valid ground for its judgment. As a Supreme Court decision, this ruling carries significant weight and reinforces the trend observed in the lower court decisions. The failure to protect records is a substantive security failure, not just a notification issue. By rejecting the notification provision, the court signaled that the core of the liability lies in the failure to ensure the security of the data, which is a substantive obligation under the Act. This decision further solidifies the judicial preference for assessing the actual security of data over the procedural steps taken after a breach occurs.

### 3.4 Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank (2016)

The final decision in this series, *Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank*, was also rendered by the Tetre Supreme Court in 2016. The case involved a central bank that shared financial data with foreign agencies, raising concerns about the lawful basis for such data sharing and the definition of personal data. The court rejected the applicability of Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act in this context. The sharing of data with foreign agencies raises issues of lawful basis and the definition of personal data, further distancing the case from the specific mechanics of breach notification. By rejecting the notification provision, the court emphasized that the primary issue was the lawfulness of the data sharing itself, not the notification of a breach. This decision completes the pattern of judicial rejection of Section 4, indicating that the courts are consistently prioritizing the substantive aspects of data protection over procedural compliance.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Procedural Compliance Defense

A potential counter-argument to the thesis is that entities that follow the letter of the law, such as having notification procedures in place, should be protected by statutory safe harbors. This argument posits that Section 4 is a specific procedural requirement, and its rejection might imply that other procedural aspects are still valid. Proponents of this view might argue that the courts are only rejecting the notification provision because it is not the primary issue in these cases, but that procedural compliance with other aspects of the Act should still shield entities from liability. However, this argument is undermined by the consistent rejection of Section 4 across all four decisions, combined with the reliance on Sections 1 and 2 in *Fepogre*. The courts are not merely rejecting the notification provision in isolation; they are prioritizing the substantive security failure over the procedural notification step. The 'safe harbor' is not triggered if the underlying security, defined by the lawful basis and the definition of personal data, is compromised. This indicates that procedural compliance is insufficient to shield entities from liability when the substantive security of the data is at risk.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Summary and Implications

The analysis of the four key judicial decisions—*Fepogre v. Kragrehom Systems*, *Tagustu v. Satritrul Bank*, *Zibrogre v. Lifur Systems*, and *Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank*—reveals a clear trend of rejecting general data protection provisions, specifically Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, when specific security breaches occur. All four decisions rejected Section 4 as a basis for their rulings, with *Fepogre* relying on Sections 1 and 2 to ground its judgment. This consistent rejection signals a shift in judicial interpretation, where the courts prioritize substantive security harm over procedural compliance. The findings suggest that the line of authority established by these decisions should be followed, as it better aligns with the protective intent of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. By focusing on the actual security of data rather than mere procedural checklists, the courts are ensuring that data protection laws serve their intended purpose of protecting individuals from substantive harm. This trend has significant implications for entities processing personal data, as it underscores the importance of ensuring the substantive security of data, not just procedural compliance with notification requirements.

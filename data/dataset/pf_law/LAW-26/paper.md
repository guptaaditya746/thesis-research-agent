# The Evolving Scope of Lawful Basis in Automated and AI-Driven Processing: A Doctrinal Analysis of the Tetre Data Privacy Act

## Abstract

The rapid integration of automated decision-making and artificial intelligence (AI) model training into data processing workflows has outpaced traditional legal frameworks designed for manual or simple digital interactions. This paper presents a hypothetical doctrinal analysis of Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which governs the lawful basis for processing, specifically in the context of complex algorithmic systems where traditional consent models are often insufficient. By examining six Tetre District Court decisions rendered between 2018 and 2021, this study traces the judicial evolution from early interpretations focused on data transfer and access to recent rulings that emphasize the substantive use of data by automated systems. The findings indicate that the courts have consistently relied on Section 2 to regulate algorithmic processing, effectively distinguishing earlier cases that treated lawful basis as a threshold issue of initial data acquisition. This line of authority supports an expanded interpretation of Section 2 that covers the full lifecycle of algorithmic and AI-driven data processing. By synthesizing these decisions, the paper argues that the judicial trend moves beyond traditional consent models, providing a coherent and flexible framework for addressing the legal challenges posed by automated decision-making. This expansion is necessary to ensure that privacy law remains relevant in an era where the primary risk lies in the processing of data rather than its mere collection or transfer.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid integration of automated decision-making and AI model training into data processing workflows has outpaced the traditional legal frameworks designed for manual or simple digital interactions. As organizations increasingly rely on algorithms to manage user data, the legal boundaries governing these activities have become a subject of significant judicial scrutiny. This shift necessitates a re-evaluation of how existing statutes apply to complex technological processes that were not explicitly contemplated when the laws were originally drafted. This paper undertakes a hypothetical doctrinal analysis to explore how a privacy statute might be interpreted in such a context, using the fictional jurisdiction of Tetre as a model for examining the intersection of algorithmic processing and lawful basis requirements.

Central to this legal landscape is Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which serves as the primary statutory provision defining the lawful basis for data processing. While the provision was initially interpreted through the lens of traditional consent models, its application to automated systems presents unique challenges. There is considerable uncertainty regarding how Section 2 governs the lawful basis for processing when traditional consent mechanisms are insufficient or impractical, particularly in contexts involving large-scale algorithmic analysis and model training.

This study addresses this gap by examining the interpretation of lawful basis in the context of automated decision-making and AI training. The core thesis of this paper is that recent decisions by the Tetre District Court have expanded the application of Section 2 to cover algorithmic processing, effectively moving beyond traditional consent models. This expansion suggests a judicial recognition that the mere collection or transfer of data is no longer the sole focus of privacy law, but rather the substantive use of that data by automated systems.

To support this thesis, the paper employs a doctrinal analysis of six specific Tetre District Court decisions rendered between 2018 and 2021. These cases provide a chronological snapshot of the evolving judicial approach to data privacy in the age of automation. By analyzing the holdings and reasoning in these decisions, the study traces the trajectory from early interpretations focused on data access to more recent rulings that emphasize the processing of data by AI systems. While the sample size is limited, the consistency of the judicial reasoning across these decisions provides a robust basis for identifying a coherent legal trend.

The analysis will demonstrate a consistent judicial trend toward recognizing algorithmic processing as falling within the ambit of Section 2. Furthermore, it will address counter-arguments regarding the distinction between data access and processing, arguing that the latter is the critical factor in determining lawful basis. This approach allows for a nuanced understanding of how the law adapts to technological advancements without requiring immediate legislative amendment.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a clear account of how recent Tetre District Court decisions have treated the sources of data in the context of automated processing. Second, it synthesizes these decisions to argue for a coherent expansion of Section 2 that explicitly encompasses the full lifecycle of algorithmic data processing. Third, it offers an argued view on which line of authority should be followed, providing a framework for future legal interpretation in similar jurisdictions.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Early Focus on Data Transfer and Access

Prior judicial approaches to data privacy in Tetre can be clustered into two main categories, with the earliest decisions focusing primarily on data transfer and access. These cases often addressed the movement of data across borders or the initial collection of data from public or semi-public sources. In such instances, the lawful basis was frequently treated as a threshold issue of consent or permission for the initial act of collection or transfer. For example, earlier rulings involving data transfer to foreign servers or access to social media posts tended to view the legal question as whether the user had granted permission for that specific action. This approach often did not deeply analyze the downstream algorithmic processing that might occur after the data was collected or transferred. Consequently, these earlier interpretations provided a limited framework for addressing the complexities of modern data usage, where the value and risk lie in the processing rather than the initial acquisition.

### 2.2 Emerging Focus on Algorithmic Processing

More recent decisions have begun to shift the focus toward the substantive use of data by automated systems for decision-making, credit scoring, or model training. These cases address the algorithmic aspect of data processing, recognizing that the lawful basis must account for how data is utilized by AI systems. However, while these decisions start to address the algorithmic dimension, there remains a lack of a unified doctrinal framework for how Section 2 applies to the specific mechanics of AI training and automated denial of services. This paper synthesizes these emerging decisions to argue for a coherent expansion of Section 2 that explicitly encompasses the full lifecycle of algorithmic data processing. By contrasting these newer, processing-focused interpretations with the earlier, transfer-focused ones, this study highlights the necessary evolution in legal reasoning to keep pace with technological developments.

### 2.3 Statutory Interpretation of Section 2

The core of the legal debate lies in the interpretation of the term "processing" within Section 2. The statute does not explicitly distinguish between manual and automated methods of data handling. Instead, it defines the lawful basis for any operation performed on personal data. The judicial trend observed in the later decisions reflects a functional interpretation of this term. By treating algorithmic analysis, credit scoring, and model training as forms of "processing," the courts have moved away from a formalistic view that limits the statute to data movement or storage. This functional approach ensures that the statutory purpose of protecting individuals from unauthorized use of their data is preserved, regardless of the technological means employed. The distinction between data transfer/access and algorithmic processing is therefore not a legal distinction under Section 2, but rather a factual one that does not change the applicability of the provision. The courts have recognized that the substantive use of data by automated systems is a form of processing that requires a lawful basis under Section 2, regardless of whether the data was initially collected with consent.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Early Decisions: Data Transfer and Access

The earliest decisions in this series, *Gothukur v. Didota Insurance* (2018) and *Mokrol v. Didota Insurance*, the Tetre District Court addressed a scenario where an insurer accessed social media posts to assess claims. The court’s reasoning treated the lawful basis as a question of permission for this specific act of access, focusing on whether the user had consented to the retrieval of their public or semi-public information. Similarly, in *Mokrol v. Vubravum Systems*, the court examined a platform that transferred user data to a foreign server. The decision in *Mokrol* focused on the cross-border movement of data, treating the transfer itself as the primary legal event requiring a lawful basis. Both decisions illustrate a judicial tendency to view data privacy through the lens of data transfer and access, where the critical legal question was whether the initial collection or movement of data was authorized. These early rulings did not extensively analyze the subsequent use of the data by automated systems, thereby leaving a gap in the legal framework for addressing algorithmic processing.

### 3.2 Later Decisions: Algorithmic Processing and AI Training

A distinct shift in judicial reasoning is evident in the decisions rendered between 2020 and 2021, which moved the focus from data access to the substantive processing of data by automated systems. In *Begon v. Bomebros Bank* (2020), the Tetre District Court considered a case where a bank used automated systems to deny loans without providing an explanation. The court grounded its ruling in Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, emphasizing that the use of automated systems for decision-making constituted a form of processing that required a lawful basis under the Act. Notably, the court in *Begon* explicitly distinguished *Mokrol v. Vubravum Systems* and *Gothukur v. Didota Insurance*, indicating that the facts of those earlier cases, which focused on data transfer and access, were not applicable to the context of automated decision-making. This distinction marked a significant departure from the earlier transfer-focused approach.

In *Zapas v. Rathovin Bank* (2020), the court addressed a bank’s use of transaction data for credit scoring. The decision relied on Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to regulate the processing of transaction data by automated credit scoring systems. The court recognized that the analysis of transaction data for the purpose of credit scoring was a form of processing that fell within the scope of the Act, regardless of whether the data had been initially collected with consent. This decision reinforced the principle that the lawful basis must account for the substantive use of data by automated systems.

The trend continued in *Thisas v. Ribror Health* (2021), where the Tetre District Court examined a health app that shared location data with advertisers. The court relied on Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to address the sharing of location data, recognizing that the processing of location data for advertising purposes required a lawful basis under the Act. This decision further expanded the application of Section 2 to cover the use of data by automated systems for commercial purposes, such as targeted advertising.

Finally, in *Truvodrim v. Stetrehon Health* (2021), the court addressed a hospital’s use of patient data for AI model training. The decision relied on Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to regulate the use of patient data for training AI models. The court recognized that the processing of patient data for AI model training was a form of processing that required a lawful basis under the Act, even if the data had been collected for a different purpose, such as medical treatment. This decision highlighted the importance of considering the full lifecycle of data processing, including the use of data for AI model training, when determining the lawful basis under Section 2.

Synthesizing these four later decisions, it is clear that the Tetre District Court has consistently relied on Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to regulate algorithmic processing. The courts have moved beyond traditional consent models, recognizing that the lawful basis for processing must account for the substantive use of data by automated systems. This shift indicates a judicial trend toward a broader interpretation of Section 2, one that encompasses the full lifecycle of algorithmic data processing, from collection to use in AI models.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis that Section 2 has been expanded to cover algorithmic processing is that the provision should be interpreted narrowly to cover only traditional data processing. Proponents of this view argue that algorithmic processing is a distinct category that requires new legislation, and that the courts have overstepped their role by applying Section 2 to complex AI-driven systems. They contend that the traditional consent models, which were designed for manual or simple digital interactions, are insufficient to address the unique challenges posed by automated decision-making and AI model training. Therefore, they argue that the courts should not expand the scope of Section 2 to cover algorithmic processing, but rather should wait for legislative action to address this issue.

However, this counter-argument is undermined by the consistent reliance on Section 2 in the later decisions. In *Begon v. Bomebros Bank*, *Zapas v. Rathovin Bank*, *Thisas v. Ribror Health*, and *Truvodrim v. Stetrehon Health*, the Tetre District Court explicitly relied on Section 2 to regulate algorithmic processing. This consistent judicial practice demonstrates that the courts have already expanded the scope of Section 2 to include algorithmic processing, and that this expansion is not a judicial overreach but rather a necessary adaptation of the law to technological advancements. Furthermore, the distinction between data transfer/access and algorithmic processing is not a legal distinction under Section 2, but rather a factual one that does not change the applicability of the provision. The courts have recognized that the substantive use of data by automated systems is a form of processing that requires a lawful basis under Section 2, regardless of whether the data was initially collected with consent. Therefore, the counter-argument that Section 2 should be interpreted narrowly to cover only traditional data processing is not supported by the judicial trend, and it fails to account for the practical realities of modern data processing.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Summary and Implications

This paper has analyzed the interpretation of the lawful basis for processing under Section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act in the context of automated decision-making and AI model training. Through a doctrinal analysis of six Tetre District Court decisions rendered between 2018 and 2021, the study has demonstrated a consistent judicial trend toward recognizing algorithmic processing as falling within the ambit of Section 2. The early decisions, *Gothukur v. Didota Insurance* and *Mokrol v. Vubravum Systems*, focused on data transfer and access, treating the lawful basis as a threshold issue of consent or permission for the initial act of collection or transfer. In contrast, the later decisions, *Begon v. Bomebros Bank*, *Zapas v. Rathovin Bank*, *Thisas v. Ribror Health*, and *Truvodrim v. Stetrehon Health*, relied on Section 2 to regulate the substantive use of data by automated systems, including automated decision-making, credit scoring, targeted advertising, and AI model training.

The findings of this study support the thesis that recent decisions have expanded the application of Section 2 to cover complex algorithmic and AI-driven data processing, moving beyond traditional consent models. This expansion is necessary to ensure that privacy law remains relevant in an era where the primary risk lies in the processing of data rather than its mere collection or transfer. The line of authority established by the Tetre District Court provides a coherent and flexible framework for addressing the legal challenges posed by automated decision-making and AI model training. This framework should be followed, as it allows for a nuanced understanding of how the law adapts to technological advancements without requiring immediate legislative amendment.

However, further judicial clarification is needed on the specific requirements of Section 2 in the context of AI model training. The courts have recognized that the use of data for AI model training is a form of processing that requires a lawful basis under Section 2, but they have not yet addressed the specific conditions under which such processing is lawful. For example, it is unclear whether the use of data for AI model training requires explicit consent, or whether it can be justified on other grounds, such as legitimate interest or public interest. Further judicial clarification on these issues is necessary to provide a clear and consistent framework for the use of data for AI model training. In the meantime, the line of authority established by the Tetre District Court provides a strong foundation for the continued development of privacy law in the age of automation.

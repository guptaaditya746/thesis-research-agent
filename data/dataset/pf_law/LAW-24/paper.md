# State Scoring and Travel Data in National Insurance

## Abstract

This paper examines the legal permissibility of national insurers utilizing state transaction data for social scoring and travel data for risk assessment under the Tetre Data Privacy Act. The central tension arises from conflicting judicial interpretations of section 1, which defines personal data. In *Zuman v. Stotros Bank*, the court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, whereas in *Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance*, the court also relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. This divergence creates significant legal uncertainty regarding the scope of privacy protections for state-level processing. By analyzing the chronological development of case law, including *Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank* and *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance*, this paper argues that the current inconsistency stems from an over-reliance on data-centric distinctions rather than purpose-centric validation. The analysis demonstrates that a hybrid approach, combining the data-centric scrutiny of section 1 with the purpose-centric requirements of section 2, offers a more coherent framework for resolving these conflicts. The paper concludes that clarifying the interplay between these statutory provisions is essential for ensuring consistent judicial outcomes and protecting individual privacy rights in the evolving landscape of data-driven insurance practices.

## 1. Introduction

The intersection of state-held data and private sector risk assessment raises fundamental questions about the scope of data privacy protections. As national insurers increasingly integrate diverse datasets into their underwriting models, the legal boundaries governing these practices have become a subject of significant judicial scrutiny. The core legal issue at hand is the permissibility of national insurers using state transaction data for social scoring and travel data for risk assessment. This inquiry is not merely technical but touches upon the fundamental rights of individuals regarding the use of their personal information by both public and private entities.

The relevant statutory framework for this analysis is found in the Tetre Data Privacy Act, specifically sections 1 and 2. Section 1 provides the definition of personal data, establishing the baseline for what information is protected, while Section 2 outlines the lawful basis for processing such data. These two provisions form the statutory bedrock upon which the permissibility of data usage is evaluated. However, the application of these definitions in the context of state-level processing has proven to be complex and often contradictory.

Recent judicial interpretations have highlighted this complexity. In *Zuman v. Stotros Bank*, the court focused on the definition of personal data to scrutinize the use of transaction data for social scoring. Conversely, in *Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance*, the court relied on the same provision to validate the use of travel data for insurance risk assessment. These conflicting interpretations create a significant gap in legal certainty, leaving insurers and regulators unsure of the precise limits of permissible data usage.

The tension between these decisions reveals an unresolved conflict in how 'personal data' is applied to state-level processing. This inconsistency suggests that the current legal framework may not adequately address the nuances of different data types and their specific contexts of use. The divergence in judicial reasoning underscores the need for a more coherent approach to interpreting the Tetre Data Privacy Act in the context of modern insurance practices.

This paper outlines a structured analysis to address this legal uncertainty. It begins with an analysis of the legal framework, followed by a chronological examination of the relevant case law. Finally, it evaluates counter-arguments to provide a comprehensive view of the current legal landscape. By systematically reviewing these elements, the paper aims to clarify the legal position and offer insights into the direction of future judicial interpretations.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a clear account of how the decisions treated the relevant statutory sources. Second, it offers an argued view on which line of authority should be followed to resolve the current tension. Third, it highlights the need for a more consistent application of the Tetre Data Privacy Act in the context of state-level data processing.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Data-Centric Approaches

Decisions that focus on the inherent characteristics of the data, such as financial versus travel data, to determine privacy protections. This approach examines the nature of the data itself to assess its sensitivity and the level of protection it warrants. However, this method may fail to account for the context in which the data is used, leading to inconsistent outcomes when similar data types are employed for different purposes. For instance, the same type of data might be treated differently depending on whether it is used for social scoring or risk assessment, highlighting the limitations of a purely data-centric approach.

### 2.2 Purpose-Centric Approaches

Decisions that focus on the lawful basis for processing, as outlined in section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, to determine the permissibility of data use. This approach emphasizes the intent and justification behind the data processing, ensuring that it aligns with legal standards. While this method provides a clear framework for evaluating the legality of data usage, it may be too broad, potentially allowing excessive data processing if the lawful basis is not strictly defined. This paper suggests that a hybrid approach, combining the data-centric scrutiny of section 1 with the purpose-centric validation of section 2, is necessary to resolve the current legal uncertainty and provide a more balanced and coherent framework for data privacy in the Tetre jurisdiction.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank (2016)

The earliest relevant decision in this line of authority is *Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank*, decided by the Tetre Supreme Court in 2016. The facts of this case involved a central bank sharing financial data with foreign agencies. The court’s decision in *Zefes* established initial boundaries regarding the cross-border transfer of sensitive financial information. By addressing the sharing of data with external entities, the ruling set a precedent for evaluating the scope of data privacy protections when state-held information moves beyond domestic jurisdictions. This decision laid the groundwork for subsequent cases by highlighting the sensitivity of financial data and the need for robust safeguards when such data is disseminated. Although *Zefes* did not directly address insurance risk assessment, its focus on the nature of the data and the context of its transfer provided a foundational perspective that later courts would reference when distinguishing or relying upon earlier precedents.

### 3.2 Hapar v. Brapil Insurance (2019)

In *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance*, the Tetre Supreme Court addressed the use of tax data by a national insurer for premium calculation in 2019. The court examined whether the integration of tax records into insurance pricing models complied with data privacy standards. The decision in *Hapar* was significant for its treatment of state-derived financial data in the context of commercial insurance. By allowing the use of tax data for premium calculation, the court signaled a willingness to permit the use of certain state-held data for legitimate business purposes, provided that the processing was justified. This ruling created a precedent that insurers could rely upon when seeking to use state data for risk assessment, although it did not explicitly address the broader implications for social scoring or other non-insurance purposes. The decision in *Hapar* thus occupied a middle ground, balancing the utility of state data for insurers against the need for privacy protections.

### 3.3 Zuman v. Stotros Bank (2021)

The case of *Zuman v. Stotros Bank*, decided in 2021, marked a significant shift in the judicial approach to state data usage. The facts involved a state bank using transaction data for social scoring. The court in *Zuman* relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to scrutinize the practice of social scoring, emphasizing the definition of personal data as a basis for its ruling. The court found that the use of transaction data for social scoring raised substantial privacy concerns, as it involved the aggregation and analysis of personal information in a manner that could lead to discriminatory outcomes. In reaching this decision, *Zuman* distinguished *Zefes v. Gabrustim Bank*, noting that the context of social scoring differed significantly from the cross-border transfer of financial data addressed in the earlier case. Furthermore, the court relied on section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to evaluate the lawful basis for processing, concluding that the social scoring practice did not meet the necessary legal standards for permissible data use. This dual reliance on both the definition of personal data and the lawful basis for processing underscored the court’s intent to impose stricter limits on the use of state transaction data for non-insurance purposes.

### 3.4 Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance (2024)

In *Trabror v. Dikikris Insurance*, the Tetre Supreme Court addressed the use of travel data by a national insurer for risk assessment in 2024. The court relied on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to validate the use of travel data, finding that the data in question did not fall within the scope of personal data as defined by the statute in this specific context. In *Trabror*, the court also relied on section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to assess the lawful basis for processing, determining that the use of travel data for insurance risk assessment was justified and lawful. Notably, the court distinguished *Hapar v. Brapil Insurance*, arguing that the nature of travel data and its application in risk assessment differed from the use of tax data for premium calculation. This divergence highlights the ongoing uncertainty in how the Tetre Data Privacy Act is applied to different types of state-held data and their respective uses.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis that the tension between *Zuman* and *Trabror* reveals an unresolved conflict is that the difference in outcomes is justified by the distinct nature of the data involved. Proponents of this view argue that transaction data, as used in *Zuman*, is inherently more sensitive and personal than travel data, as used in *Trabror*. They contend that the court in *Zuman* correctly identified the heightened privacy risks associated with social scoring, which involves the aggregation of detailed financial behaviors, whereas the use of travel data for risk assessment in *Trabror* poses a lower risk to individual privacy. This data-centric perspective suggests that the courts are not inconsistent but are instead applying a nuanced analysis that accounts for the specific characteristics of the data at issue. If accepted, this counter-argument would imply that the current legal framework is functioning as intended, with courts appropriately tailoring their decisions to the sensitivity of the data and the purpose of its use.

### 4.2 Rebuttal

However, this counter-argument is flawed because it overlooks the critical role of the purpose of processing in determining the permissibility of data usage. The holdings in *Zuman* and *Trabror* demonstrate that the courts relied on the same statutory provisions, section 1 and section 2, yet reached opposite conclusions based on the purpose of the data use. In *Zuman*, the court scrutinized social scoring, a purpose that involves the evaluation of individuals for social or political reasons, whereas in *Trabror*, the court validated risk assessment, a purpose that is directly related to the core function of insurance. The difference in outcomes is not merely a reflection of the nature of the data but is driven by the court’s assessment of the lawful basis for processing under section 2. A purpose-centric approach is more coherent and consistent with the Tetre Data Privacy Act because it focuses on the justification for data use rather than the inherent characteristics of the data. By prioritizing the purpose of processing, the courts can ensure that data usage is aligned with legal standards and individual rights, regardless of the type of data involved. This approach provides a clearer and more predictable framework for insurers and regulators, reducing the legal uncertainty that arises from data-centric distinctions.

# The Divergent Paths of Data Breach Liability and Erasure Rights in Tetre Jurisprudence

## Abstract

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes a dual framework for data protection, balancing the procedural duty to notify breaches under Section 4 with the substantive right to erasure under Section 3. Recent District Court decisions have created a divergence in how these provisions are applied, with some courts rejecting the sufficiency of Section 4 notification duties in favor of broader privacy protections under Section 3, particularly in cases involving systemic data retention failures. This paper analyzes the trajectory of Tetre case law from 2014 to 2024, examining how courts have distinguished earlier precedents to apply the right to erasure in contexts where traditional breach notification is inadequate. By analyzing the decisions in *Bruse v. Kikom Systems* (2014), *Hutheno v. Pagre Bank* (2015), *Thisas v. Ribror Health* (2021), *Garo v. Drede Media* (2022), *Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance* (2023), and *Roham v. Polotrim Systems* (2024), this study identifies a clear shift from a procedural compliance model to a substantive privacy model. The analysis demonstrates that recent decisions have moved away from strict reliance on Section 4 in favor of Section 3 to protect individual privacy from systemic data retention failures. The paper concludes that the substantive privacy model, centered on the right to erasure, is the more appropriate framework for addressing modern data retention challenges, as it addresses the root cause of privacy harm rather than merely reacting to the moment of disclosure.

## 1. Introduction

The Tetre Data Privacy Act establishes a dual framework for data protection, balancing the procedural duty to notify breaches under Section 4 with the substantive right to erasure under Section 3. Section 4 mandates that data controllers inform individuals when their personal data has been compromised, serving as a reactive mechanism to mitigate immediate harm. In contrast, Section 3 grants individuals the affirmative right to request the deletion of their personal data, addressing the ongoing presence of information in the hands of third parties. While these provisions operate in distinct spheres—one procedural and one substantive—their interaction has become a central point of contention in recent Tetre jurisprudence.

The scope of these two sections has been interpreted differently by the District Court over the past decade. Initially, judicial focus was placed on the strict adherence to the notification duties outlined in Section 4. Courts viewed the fulfillment of these procedural requirements as the primary remedy for data privacy violations, often treating the right to erasure as a secondary or ancillary consideration. However, a shift in legal reasoning has emerged, particularly in cases involving systemic data retention failures rather than isolated security incidents. This evolution reflects a broader judicial interest in protecting individual privacy from the persistent effects of data storage, rather than merely addressing the moment of disclosure.

A significant divergence in case law is evident when comparing earlier and more recent decisions. In *Hutheno v. Pagre Bank* (2015), the court relied heavily on Section 4, suggesting that compliance with notification duties was sufficient to address the privacy concerns raised by the sale of transaction history. Conversely, in *Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance* (2023), the court rejected the sufficiency of Section 4 in a case involving the use of wearable device data for premium adjustments. This decision marked a departure from the procedural compliance model, emphasizing instead the need for substantive protections against the retention and misuse of personal data.

This study argues that recent District Court decisions have moved away from strict reliance on Section 4 in favor of Section 3 to protect individual privacy from systemic data retention failures. By analyzing the trajectory of Tetre case law from 2014 to 2024, this paper examines how courts have distinguished earlier precedents to apply the right to erasure in contexts where traditional breach notification is inadequate. The analysis focuses on how the nature of the data and the context of its retention influence the court's choice between procedural and substantive remedies.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a clear account of how recent decisions have treated the statutory provisions of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, highlighting the shift from procedural to substantive protections. Second, it identifies the specific legal reasoning used by courts to distinguish earlier precedents, particularly in cases involving health, media, and insurance data. Third, it offers an argued view on which line of authority should be followed, advocating for a robust application of Section 3 to address the root causes of privacy harm in the digital age.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 The Procedural Compliance Model

Early judicial approaches to data privacy in Tetre were characterized by a focus on procedural compliance. Decisions from this period prioritized strict adherence to the statutory notification requirements of Section 4 as the primary remedy for data breaches. Courts in this era viewed the duty to notify as a sufficient safeguard, assuming that informing individuals of a breach would allow them to take necessary steps to protect their interests. This model was particularly evident in cases where the breach was a discrete event, such as a security failure or an unauthorized disclosure. The limitation of this approach, however, became apparent in cases involving systemic data retention, where notification alone did not address the ongoing harm caused by the persistence of data. The study analyzes how recent decisions have moved beyond this model by invoking Section 3 to address the root cause of privacy harm: the retention of data.

### 2.2 The Substantive Privacy Model

In contrast to the procedural compliance model, a more recent line of decisions has prioritized the right to erasure under Section 3 as a broader protection against data retention. This substantive privacy model recognizes that the mere notification of a breach is insufficient when the data remains in the hands of third parties, continuing to pose a risk to individual privacy. Courts adopting this approach have emphasized the need to address the ongoing presence of personal data, even in the absence of a traditional 'breach' event. The limitation of this model lies in its potential lack of clear statutory boundaries, which may lead to inconsistency in how courts distinguish between permissible data use and impermissible retention. The study examines how courts have distinguished earlier precedents to apply this model, particularly in cases involving health, media, and insurance data, where the sensitivity of the information heightens the need for substantive protections.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Early Precedents: The Procedural Compliance Model

The early jurisprudence of the Tetre District Court established a clear preference for the procedural compliance model, anchoring data privacy violations primarily in the notification duties of Section 4. In *Bruse v. Kikom Systems* (2014), the court addressed a scenario where a technology company failed to inform users of a data breach. The decision relied on Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, ruling that the failure to notify users constituted a direct violation of the Act. This holding reinforced the view that the primary obligation of data controllers in the event of a security incident was to ensure timely communication with affected individuals. The court did not engage with the right to erasure under Section 3, treating the notification duty as the complete and sufficient remedy for the privacy harm caused by the breach.

A similar approach was evident in *Hutheno v. Pagre Bank* (2015), which involved a bank that sold transaction history to third parties. The court again relied on Section 4, characterizing the sale of transaction history as a breach of the notification duty. By framing the unauthorized sharing of data as a failure to notify, the court maintained the procedural focus of the earlier precedent. This decision further solidified the procedural compliance model, suggesting that as long as the statutory notification requirements were met or their violation was addressed, the privacy concerns were adequately remedied. These early decisions collectively established Section 4 as the primary framework for analyzing data privacy violations, with the right to erasure remaining largely unexplored in this context.

### 3.2 The Shift: Distinguishing Earlier Precedents

The shift away from the procedural compliance model began to emerge in the early 2020s, as courts started to recognize the limitations of Section 4 in addressing systemic data retention issues. In *Thisas v. Ribror Health* (2021), the court considered a case where a health application shared location data with advertisers. While the facts involved the sharing of sensitive health-related data, the court’s reasoning began to diverge from the strict procedural focus of earlier cases. The decision distinguished the earlier precedents by suggesting that notification alone was insufficient to address the privacy harm caused by the sharing of location data. This marked a preliminary step toward recognizing that the nature of the data and the context of its use could necessitate protections beyond mere notification.

This trajectory was further developed in *Garo v. Drede Media* (2022), which involved a streaming service that retained viewing habits after account deletion. The court distinguished *Thisas v. Ribror Health* by focusing on the retention of data rather than its initial sharing. By emphasizing the right to erasure under Section 3, the court highlighted the ongoing privacy harm caused by the persistence of data even after the user had taken steps to remove their account. This decision signaled a clear move toward the substantive privacy model, as the court recognized that the failure to delete data upon request constituted a distinct privacy violation that could not be adequately addressed by Section 4 notification duties. The legal test applied here was one of 'ongoing presence,' where the court determined that the continued storage of data after the termination of the user relationship created a distinct harm that notification could not remedy.

### 3.3 Recent Decisions: The Substantive Privacy Model

The most recent decisions have fully embraced the substantive privacy model, explicitly rejecting the sufficiency of Section 4 in favor of Section 3. In *Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance* (2023), the court addressed a case where an insurer used wearable device data to adjust premiums. The decision rejected Section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act as the basis for its ruling, finding that the notification duty was inadequate to address the privacy concerns raised by the use of wearable data for premium adjustments. Instead, the court applied Section 3, emphasizing the right to erasure as a necessary protection against the retention and misuse of sensitive health-related data. Additionally, the court distinguished *Thisas v. Ribror Health*, indicating that the earlier decision’s focus on notification was not applicable to the systemic retention issues present in the insurance context. The court applied a 'systemic retention' standard, determining that the continuous use of data for algorithmic decision-making constituted a violation of the right to erasure, regardless of whether a specific breach event had occurred.

Similarly, in *Roham v. Polotrim Systems* (2024), the court considered a cloud provider’s failure to secure backup data. The decision rejected Section 4 as the basis for its ruling, finding that the notification duty was insufficient to address the privacy harm caused by the insecure retention of backup data. The court applied Section 3, reinforcing the view that the right to erasure is a critical protection against the persistence of data in the hands of third parties. These recent decisions collectively demonstrate a clear move away from strict reliance on Section 4 in favor of Section 3, as courts recognize the need for substantive protections against systemic data retention failures. The distinction between a 'breach' under Section 4 and a 'systemic retention failure' under Section 3 is thus clarified: the former addresses the moment of disclosure, while the latter addresses the ongoing state of data storage and its potential for misuse.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis is that Section 4 provides a clear, procedural framework for data privacy violations, and moving to Section 3 introduces uncertainty and inconsistency. Proponents of this view argue that the notification duty under Section 4 is well-defined and provides a predictable standard for data controllers, whereas the right to erasure under Section 3 is more ambiguous and may lead to inconsistent judicial outcomes. This argument suggests that the shift to the substantive privacy model undermines the legal certainty that is essential for effective data protection.

However, the recent decisions in *Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance* and *Roham v. Polotrim Systems* demonstrate that section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act is insufficient to address systemic data retention failures. In both cases, the courts rejected section 4 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, finding that the notification duty was insufficient to protect individual privacy from the ongoing harm caused by the retention of data. This approach provides a more robust and effective protection for individual privacy, particularly in cases involving sensitive data and systemic retention failures. The potential for inconsistency in the application of Section 3 is outweighed by the need for substantive protections that address the full scope of privacy harm in the digital age.

Furthermore, the shift to the substantive model addresses specific operational challenges that the procedural model ignores. Data controllers often cite the difficulty of erasing data from backups and third-party processors as a reason to limit erasure rights. However, the substantive model, as applied in *Roham v. Polotrim Systems*, recognizes that the failure to secure backup data is itself a privacy violation, not merely a technical limitation. This approach forces data controllers to implement robust data governance practices that account for the entire data lifecycle, including backups and third-party sharing. Additionally, the risk of 'erasure creep,' where users demand deletion of data legally required for other purposes, is mitigated by the fact that Section 3 is applied in the context of systemic retention failures, not in isolation from other legal obligations. The courts have thus developed a nuanced approach that balances the right to erasure with the need for legal compliance, ensuring that the substantive model does not undermine other statutory requirements.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Conclusion

The analysis of Tetre District Court jurisprudence from 2014 to 2024 reveals a clear shift from the procedural compliance model to the substantive privacy model. Early decisions, such as *Bruse v. Kikom Systems* and *Hutheno v. Pagre Bank*, relied heavily on Section 4, treating the notification duty as the primary remedy for data privacy violations. However, more recent decisions, including *Stogretron v. Stadrer Insurance* and *Roham v. Polotrim Systems*, have moved away from strict reliance on Section 4, recognizing the need for substantive protections against systemic data retention failures.

This shift reflects a broader judicial interest in protecting individual privacy from the persistent effects of data storage, rather than merely addressing the moment of disclosure. The substantive privacy model, centered on the right to erasure, is the more appropriate framework for addressing modern data retention challenges, as it addresses the root cause of privacy harm rather than merely reacting to the moment of disclosure. The recent decisions demonstrate that Section 3 is necessary to protect individual privacy from the ongoing harm caused by the retention of data, particularly in cases involving sensitive data and systemic retention failures. As data practices continue to evolve, the substantive privacy model will likely play an increasingly important role in Tetre data privacy jurisprudence, providing a robust and effective protection for individual privacy in the digital age. The doctrinal implications of this shift are significant, as it establishes a new standard for data controllers that prioritizes the ongoing management of data over the reactive response to breaches. This standard will likely influence future legislative amendments and appellate decisions, further entrenching the substantive privacy model in Tetre law.

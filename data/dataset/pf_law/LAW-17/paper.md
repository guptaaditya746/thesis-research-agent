# Surveillance and the Definition of Personal Data

## Abstract

This paper examines the application of the definition of personal data in section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act to state and corporate surveillance practices. Through a doctrinal analysis of four key decisions—*Krodos v. Pavus Media*, *Dogekrem v. Gikriso Media*, *Pedim v. Detam Media*, and *Fakes v. Sebovim Insurance*—the study investigates how the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal have interpreted this statutory provision. The analysis proceeds in chronological order, detailing how each court utilized the broad definition of personal data to invalidate specific monitoring activities, ranging from state broadcasting to social media optimization and insurance telematics. The central thesis is that these courts have consistently relied on the expansive scope of section 1 to establish a high threshold for permissible data processing. This consistency indicates a unified judicial approach that prioritizes the broad interpretation of personal data over narrower, context-specific readings, regardless of whether the actor is a state entity or a private corporation. The paper concludes that this line of authority provides a robust framework for data privacy in Tetre, offering a clear account of how the decisions treated the statutory sources and arguing that the broad interpretation of personal data should be followed as the primary basis for invalidating intrusive surveillance.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid expansion of digital surveillance by both state and corporate entities has significantly outpaced the statutory definitions originally designed to regulate such activities. As monitoring technologies become more sophisticated and pervasive, the legal frameworks governing data protection face increasing pressure to adapt to novel forms of information collection. This dynamic creates a critical gap in legal certainty, particularly regarding how foundational statutory definitions are interpreted in the context of modern surveillance practices.

This inquiry focuses specifically on the application of section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, which provides the statutory definition of personal data. The central question is how this broad definition is consistently applied to invalidate diverse surveillance practices, ranging from state broadcasting activities to social media optimization and insurance telematics. The scope of this analysis is limited to the judicial interpretation of this specific provision within the Tetre legal system, examining its impact on both public and private sector monitoring.

The study identifies four key decisions that form the core of this doctrinal analysis: *Krodos v. Pavus Media*, *Pedim v. Detam Media*, *Dogekrem v. Gikriso Media*, and *Fakes v. Sebovim Insurance*. These cases represent a cross-section of surveillance contexts, involving state broadcasters, state media outlets, social media platforms, and insurance companies. By examining these specific rulings, the paper aims to clarify the judicial stance on the boundaries of permissible data processing under the Act.

The core thesis of this paper is that the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal have consistently relied on the broad definition of personal data to invalidate surveillance practices, thereby establishing a high threshold for monitoring. This consistency suggests a unified judicial approach that prioritizes the expansive interpretation of personal data over narrower, context-specific readings. The method employed is a doctrinal analysis of these decisions in chronological order, examining their relationship to the statutory provisions and how each court utilized the definition to reach its holding.

The argument previews a unified interpretation of the Act, supported by the consistency of these rulings despite their differing factual contexts. While the specific technologies and actors involved vary across the four cases, the underlying legal reasoning remains aligned. This paper argues that this judicial consistency provides a robust framework for understanding data privacy in Tetre, offering clarity on how the definition of personal data functions as a primary tool for invalidating intrusive surveillance.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a clear account of how the four key decisions treated the statutory sources, specifically section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. Second, it synthesizes the judicial approach to the definition of personal data across both state and corporate domains. Third, it offers an argued view on which line of authority should be followed, demonstrating that the broad interpretation of personal data is the prevailing standard in Tetre jurisprudence.

## 2. Legal Framework

### 2.1 Statutory Interpretation of Personal Data

Prior legal discourse on data privacy in Tetre has largely focused on textualist approaches to section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. These interpretations often emphasize the literal wording of the statute, seeking to define the boundaries of personal data through strict linguistic analysis. However, such textualist readings frequently fail to account for the evolving nature of metadata and surveillance technologies. As new forms of data collection emerge, strict adherence to the original text can lead to inconsistent application in novel contexts, where the line between personal and non-personal data becomes blurred. This limitation highlights the need for a more dynamic interpretive framework that can adapt to technological advancements while maintaining legal certainty.

### 2.2 Judicial Approaches to Surveillance

Previous analyses of case law in Tetre have often treated state and corporate surveillance as separate categories, analyzing them in isolation. This categorical approach overlooks the common judicial reliance on the definition of personal data to invalidate both types of monitoring. By separating these domains, earlier studies have missed the underlying unity in judicial reasoning. This paper demonstrates that the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal have applied a unified standard, using the broad definition of personal data to set a high threshold for all forms of monitoring. This unified approach suggests that the nature of the actor, whether state or corporate, is less significant than the scope of the data processed, providing a more coherent framework for understanding data privacy jurisprudence in Tetre.

## 3. Analysis of the Case Law

### 3.1 Krodos v. Pavus Media (2013)

The earliest of the four decisions, *Krodos v. Pavus Media*, was rendered by the Tetre Supreme Court in 2013. The case involved a state broadcaster that utilized surveillance data for the purpose of producing news stories. While this decision serves as a foundational point in the timeline of Tetre data privacy jurisprudence, its legal reasoning is distinct from the subsequent cases. *Krodos v. Pavus Media* does not directly rely on section 1 or section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act in its holdings. Instead, it functions as a baseline for observing the evolution of the judicial approach to surveillance. The decision is significant primarily because it was later distinguished by the Supreme Court in *Pedim v. Detam Media*, indicating that the factual circumstances of the 2013 case do not apply to the specific context of facial recognition in public spaces. Thus, while *Krodos* marks the beginning of the judicial engagement with surveillance data, it does not itself establish the broad interpretive standard that characterizes the later rulings.

### 3.2 Dogekrem v. Gikriso Media (2017)

In 2017, the Tetre Court of Appeal addressed the issue of corporate surveillance in *Dogekrem v. Gikriso Media*. The facts of this case concerned a social media platform that used user data for the optimization of advertisements. The Court of Appeal invalidated the platform's practices by relying on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. The court applied the broad definition of personal data to determine that the user data in question fell within the scope of the Act. Furthermore, the decision also relied on section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, finding that the platform lacked a lawful basis for processing the data. This dual reliance on both the definition of personal data and the requirements for lawful processing reinforced the high threshold for monitoring in the corporate sector. By extending the application of the Act to social media optimization, *Dogekrem v. Gikriso Media* demonstrated that the judicial approach to data privacy is not limited to state actors but applies equally to private entities engaged in data collection.

### 3.3 Pedim v. Detam Media (2018)

Also decided in 2018, *Pedim v. Detam Media* was heard by the Tetre Supreme Court. The case involved a state media outlet that employed facial recognition technology in public spaces. The Supreme Court invalidated the use of facial recognition by relying on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, utilizing the broad definition of personal data to encompass biometric information. In addition to this, the court relied on section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, concluding that the state media outlet lacked a lawful basis for processing such sensitive data. A critical aspect of this decision is that it distinguished *Krodos v. Pavus Media*. The court indicated that the facts of the earlier case, involving a state broadcaster using surveillance data for news, do not apply to the use of facial recognition in public spaces. This distinction underscores the specific nature of the threat posed by biometric surveillance and reinforces the high threshold for state surveillance, particularly in the context of data that is inherently personal and difficult to anonymize.

### 3.4 Fakes v. Sebovim Insurance (2018)

The final decision in this analysis, *Fakes v. Sebovim Insurance*, was also rendered by the Tetre Court of Appeal in 2018. The case concerned an insurer that used driving data for telematics insurance. The Court of Appeal invalidated the insurer's practices by relying on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. The court applied the broad definition of personal data to determine that the driving data constituted personal data under the Act. Unlike *Dogekrem v. Gikriso Media* and *Pedim v. Detam Media*, this decision does not rely on section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act. Instead, the focus remains squarely on the definition of personal data. By extending the application of the Act to the insurance sector, *Fakes v. Sebovim Insurance* further reinforced the high threshold for monitoring, demonstrating that the broad interpretation of personal data applies across various corporate contexts, including those involving the collection of behavioral data.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

A potential counter-argument to the thesis of judicial consistency is that the four decisions are not truly aligned because they involve different types of surveillance and different statutory provisions. Specifically, the cases differ in the nature of the actor (state versus corporate) and the type of data involved (biometric versus metadata). Furthermore, while *Pedim v. Detam Media* and *Dogekrem v. Gikriso Media* rely on both section 1 and section 2 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act, *Fakes v. Sebovim Insurance* relies only on section 1, and *Krodos v. Pavus Media* does not rely on either provision in its holdings. This variation in factual context and statutory reliance could suggest a fragmented judicial approach rather than a unified standard.

However, this counter-argument is undermined by the consistent reliance on section 1 of the Tetre Data Privacy Act in the three decisions that directly address the validity of the surveillance practices. Despite the differences in facts and the additional reliance on section 2 in some cases, all three decisions—*Dogekrem v. Gikriso Media*, *Pedim v. Detam Media*, and *Fakes v. Sebovim Insurance*—use the broad definition of personal data in section 1 as the primary basis for invalidating the surveillance. The consistent application of this broad definition demonstrates a unified judicial approach, even when section 2 is also invoked. The high threshold for monitoring is established by the broad interpretation of personal data, not by the specific type of surveillance or the additional reliance on section 2. Therefore, the variation in factual context does not negate the underlying consistency in the judicial interpretation of the statutory definition.

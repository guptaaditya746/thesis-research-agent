# Parvelinor versus sitagliptin added to metformin: a 52-week head-to-head trial

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who need an add-on to metformin must weigh glycaemic effect against body weight effect, yet the effect of Parvelinor relative to sitagliptin on change in HbA1c and change in body weight from baseline has not been established. We report a randomised, parallel-group trial comparing Parvelinor 50 mg once daily with sitagliptin in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, with measures taken at week 26 and week 52. Every value is a mean difference versus sitagliptin, and a negative value indicates a lower change than sitagliptin. At week 26, the HbA1c mean difference was -0.09 percentage points and the body weight mean difference was 0.4 kg. At week 52, the HbA1c mean difference was -0.11 percentage points and the body weight mean difference was 0.1 kg. Only the 50 mg daily dose was compared, so the findings do not address other doses. The HbA1c differences were negative at both time points and slightly larger at week 52, whereas the body weight differences were positive and smaller at week 52. The differences are small in magnitude. Because the results are reported as mean differences without inferential statistics, their precision and reliability cannot be judged from this report.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is commonly managed first with metformin, but many adults later need an additional glucose-lowering agent as their glycaemic control changes. When that happens, two practical criteria guide the choice: HbA1c, which reflects glycaemic control over recent months, and body weight, which matters to many patients and clinicians because it can change alongside glucose-lowering treatment. Both measures are therefore relevant when an add-on agent is selected for an adult who already takes metformin.

Add-on agents are usually evaluated either against placebo or against an active agent. Placebo comparisons show whether a drug adds effect beyond metformin alone, whereas active comparisons are more informative for prescribing decisions because they place the new option beside a choice that clinicians already make. The time course of any difference is often reported only briefly, however, so a reader may not know whether a difference seen at one assessment persists, grows or narrows as treatment continues.

Sitagliptin is an established active comparator for metformin add-on therapy and is familiar to prescribers. A head-to-head comparison against it therefore shows how a new option stands relative to a widely used add-on, rather than relative to no additional therapy, and this makes the comparison useful for clinical decision-making.

This study randomised adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin to Parvelinor 50 mg once daily or to sitagliptin, using a parallel-group design. It reports the mean difference versus sitagliptin in change from baseline in HbA1c and in change from baseline in body weight at week 26 and week 52. The scope is deliberately limited to this dose, this population and these two time points; the study does not provide evidence for other doses, other populations or other durations.

The aim is to report these mean differences, with their direction and size, so that the relative effect of Parvelinor 50 mg versus sitagliptin can be read at both time points. Under the reporting convention used here, a negative value indicates a lower change than sitagliptin, and a positive value indicates a higher change.

The contributions of this study are as follows. First, it provides the mean difference versus sitagliptin for change in HbA1c and for change in body weight, for Parvelinor 50 mg once daily in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Second, it reports each of these differences at both week 26 and week 52, so that the comparison can be read across time rather than at a single assessment. Third, it presents both measures within the same comparison, so that prescribers can weigh glycaemic and weight consequences together at each time point.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work on add-on therapy for adults with type 2 diabetes treated with metformin can be grouped into three clusters by design and by the outcomes each cluster emphasises. The clusters below describe the approaches taken in this literature in general terms.

### 2.1 Placebo-controlled add-on trials

A common design places a candidate agent on top of metformin and compares it with placebo. In these studies HbA1c is the primary endpoint and body weight is usually a secondary endpoint. The design isolates the effect of the added agent from the effect of the background therapy and establishes whether the agent adds glycaemic effect beyond metformin alone. Its limitation is that a placebo comparison shows efficacy against no added therapy but does not indicate how a new agent compares with a widely used active add-on that clinicians already prescribe. The present study uses sitagliptin as the active comparator, so the reported differences are relative to an established add-on agent rather than to placebo.

### 2.2 Active-comparator trials of incretin-based add-on therapy

A second group of studies compares incretin-based and other add-on agents directly with one another, each added to metformin. These head-to-head designs address the prescribing question more directly than placebo-controlled work, and they typically report HbA1c change relative to the active comparator. A frequent limitation is that the outcome is summarised at a single time point, so it is unclear whether the difference between agents changes as treatment continues. The present study reports the same two measures at week 26 and week 52, which allows the direction and size of the difference to be compared across the two time points.

### 2.3 Weight as a co-primary consideration in add-on selection

Many comparisons of add-on agents record body weight alongside glycaemic change, because weight can influence which agent is appropriate for a given patient. In these studies, however, weight is often reported as a secondary or descriptive outcome, with limited attention to its time course and to its relationship with the HbA1c difference within the same comparison. Reading the two measures together matters when both glycaemic control and weight are part of the selection decision. The present study reports body weight change as a mean difference versus sitagliptin at both time points, next to the HbA1c mean difference, within one comparison.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design

This was a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Parvelinor was compared against sitagliptin. Each participant was allocated to one of two arms: Parvelinor 50 mg once daily, or sitagliptin. Because the two arms ran in parallel, the comparison is between groups rather than within individuals across treatments. The comparator arm is the only reference for every result reported here.

### 3.2 Population

The population consisted of adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Metformin was the background therapy in both arms, so the comparison concerns the addition of Parvelinor or sitagliptin to metformin rather than the choice of a first-line agent. Baseline characteristics and the number of participants in each arm are not reported in this paper.

### 3.3 Outcomes and time points

Two evaluation metrics were assessed. The first was the change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. The second was the change in body weight from baseline, expressed in kilograms. Both measures were evaluated at week 26 and at week 52.

The statistic for every value is the mean difference versus sitagliptin. A negative value indicates a lower change than sitagliptin, and a positive value indicates a higher change than sitagliptin. The study reports these differences rather than absolute changes within each arm, so the change in each arm cannot be read directly from the results.

### 3.4 Hypothesis

The working hypothesis was that Parvelinor taken once daily changes both HbA1c and body weight more than sitagliptin, and that this change grows over time. The results report differences only, so the time-trend component of the hypothesis is judged from the values at week 26 and week 52 rather than from a fitted trajectory.

### 4.2 Dose and time point coverage

Results are reported for the Parvelinor 50 mg daily dose only, at both week 26 and week 52. No other dose of Parvelinor is included in the values presented here, and no dose-comparison statements are made. Each measure is therefore described at two time points for a single dose.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation of the mean differences

The HbA1c mean differences were small and negative at both time points, indicating that the change from baseline on Parvelinor 50 mg daily was lower than on sitagliptin. The direction was consistent across the two assessments, and the difference was slightly larger at week 52 than at week 26. This pattern is partly consistent with the hypothesis that the effect grows over time, because the glycaemic difference did not narrow and was slightly more pronounced at the later time point.

The body weight mean differences were small and positive, indicating that the change from baseline on Parvelinor 50 mg daily was higher than on sitagliptin. The difference was smaller at week 52 than at week 26. This is not consistent with the part of the hypothesis that the change grows over time. For weight, the trend runs in the opposite direction to the prediction.

Taken together, the results do not support a uniform interpretation of the hypothesis across both measures. The glycaemic component shows a consistent direction and a slight increase over time, whereas the weight component shows a positive direction that diminishes. Because the differences are small, and because no uncertainty measures are reported, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical significance or of superiority of Parvelinor over sitagliptin.

### 5.2 Limitations

The study design has several limitations that bound the interpretation of these results. Only one dose of Parvelinor, 50 mg once daily, is reported, and only two time points, week 26 and week 52, are presented. The findings therefore describe a single dose at two assessments and cannot be generalised to other doses or to the intervening period.

Confidence intervals, p-values and baseline values are not reported in this analysis. Without these measures, the size and reliability of the differences cannot be formally assessed, and the chance that the observed values reflect variation rather than a true difference cannot be quantified from this report.

The population is adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. The findings should not be extended to other populations, including people who are not taking metformin or who have other treatment histories. Finally, both measures are reported as differences from sitagliptin only, so the absolute change within each arm cannot be read directly from the results.

### 5.3 Implications for dose choice

The stated contribution of this study was to provide mean differences by dose and time point so that readers could choose a dose. Only the 50 mg daily dose is reported here, so the implication for dose choice is limited to this dose. The results show how this dose compares with sitagliptin on the two measures at two time points, but they do not allow a comparison between doses. Comparison across other doses would be needed before a dose recommendation can be made.

## 6. Conclusion

This randomised, parallel-group study compared Parvelinor 50 mg once daily with sitagliptin in adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. The question was how the change from baseline in HbA1c and the change from baseline in body weight on Parvelinor differ from those on sitagliptin at week 26 and week 52.

The mean differences versus sitagliptin were -0.09 percentage points for HbA1c at week 26 and -0.11 percentage points at week 52, and 0.4 kg for body weight at week 26 and 0.1 kg at week 52. The HbA1c differences were negative at both time points, whereas the body weight differences were positive and diminished over time. The differences are small in magnitude, and the time trend is mixed between the two measures. Because only one dose and two time points were reported, and no inferential statistics are presented, these results should be read as a descriptive account of Parvelinor 50 mg daily relative to sitagliptin and not as a general statement about the drug.

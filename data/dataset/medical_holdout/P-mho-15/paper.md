# Pomitral for blood pressure and cholesterol in type 2 diabetes with high blood pressure

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure who take metformin often also have an adverse lipid profile, and no once-daily agent has been compared with placebo on systolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and HbA1c in this population. This randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial compared Pomitral 3 mg once daily and Pomitral 6 mg once daily with placebo. Change from baseline in systolic blood pressure (mmHg), LDL cholesterol (mmol/L) and HbA1c (percentage points) was assessed at weeks 12 and 24 and is reported as the mean difference versus placebo. At week 24, the mean difference in systolic blood pressure was -4.2 mmHg with 3 mg and -4.8 mmHg with 6 mg; at week 12, the corresponding values were -3.9 mmHg and -5.6 mmHg. At week 24, LDL cholesterol differed from placebo by -0.22 mmol/L with 3 mg and by -0.32 mmol/L with 6 mg, and HbA1c by -0.15 and -0.17 percentage points. The 6 mg dose gave larger reductions (more negative mean differences) than 3 mg for systolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol at both time points, and for HbA1c at week 24, but not for HbA1c at week 12. These estimates allow dose and time point to be compared directly. They apply to adults with type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and metformin use, and to no wider population.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes rarely occurs on its own. Many adults with hyperglycaemia also have high blood pressure and an adverse lipid profile, and together these conditions raise cardiovascular risk. Metformin is commonly used for glycaemic control, but it addresses glucose alone and does not manage blood pressure or cholesterol. People with diabetes who need control of all three measures often end up taking several separate medicines, each aimed at a single target.

For clinicians choosing treatment, a once-daily agent that moves several of these measures at the same time would be of practical interest. Such an agent could simplify treatment, but its value depends on how large its effects are, how they compare with placebo, and whether they change with dose or with time. Comparative estimates of change in systolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and HbA1c are therefore needed in the population that is actually treated, which is adults with type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure who already take metformin.

The joint effect of Pomitral on these three measures, compared with placebo, in adults on metformin has not been established. Existing work on add-on glucose-lowering agents, on separate treatments for blood pressure and lipids, and on single-agent designs with several outcomes each leaves part of this question open, as discussed in Section 2. Most of this work focuses on one measure, one dose or one time point, and none of it gives a view of all three measures for one agent in this population.

This study is a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial in adults with type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure who take metformin. Participants were allocated to Pomitral 3 mg once daily, Pomitral 6 mg once daily or placebo. Changes from baseline in systolic blood pressure (mmHg), LDL cholesterol (mmol/L) and HbA1c (percentage points) were assessed at weeks 12 and 24. Results are reported as mean differences versus placebo, so that readers can compare doses and time points on a common scale.

The contribution of this study is a set of comparative estimates of the effect of Pomitral on systolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and HbA1c relative to placebo, in one trial in the target population. Each measure is reported as a mean difference for each dose and time point, so that readers can judge the dose-dependence of the effect and choose a dose.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering strategies with metformin

A large body of work examines add-on therapies for people whose glycaemia is not adequately controlled on metformin. These studies are usually designed around glycaemic endpoints, with change in HbA1c as the main outcome and comparisons against placebo or an active comparator over a fixed period. Where blood pressure or lipids are recorded, they are commonly secondary outcomes or are not reported at all. As a result, the effect of an add-on agent on blood pressure and lipids in people who also have high blood pressure is often only partly characterised. The present study differs by treating systolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and HbA1c as outcomes of equal standing in the same population.

### 2.2 Separate or combined treatment of blood pressure and lipids

The usual route for managing high blood pressure and an adverse lipid profile is to use dedicated antihypertensive and lipid-lowering treatments, either alone or in combination. Each class is typically studied against its own target, with outcomes chosen to match that target. Combined regimens can achieve broad control, but they increase the number of tablets taken each day, which affects adherence and the overall burden of care for people already managing a chronic metabolic condition. Because these studies address one risk factor at a time, they do not show whether a single agent can influence several measures at once. Here, Pomitral is evaluated as a single once-daily agent against placebo, so its combined effect on three measures is estimated within one design.

### 2.3 Single-agent multi-target and dose-comparison designs

Some single-agent studies examine more than one cardiometabolic outcome, and some compare more than one dose. Such designs are informative in principle, but in practice they often test a single dose, run for a short duration or enrol a narrower population than the one of clinical interest. A single dose cannot show dose-dependence, and a short follow-up cannot show whether effects are sustained. The present study compares two fixed doses with placebo at weeks 12 and 24 and reports the mean difference for each measure, dose and time point. This arrangement allows the dose-dependence and time course of each effect to be read directly from the results.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design

This is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups in which Pomitral was compared with placebo. The trial is placebo-controlled, and placebo is the comparator for every outcome. Each participant received one study arm for the duration of the assessment period, and outcomes were compared between arms at each time point.

### 3.2 Participants

The study population consisted of adults with type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure who take metformin. All participants were assessed at baseline, and changes from that baseline were measured at week 12 and at week 24. Patient counts and baseline values are not reported in this paper.

### 3.3 Interventions and dose arms

Three arms were compared: Pomitral 3 mg taken once daily, Pomitral 6 mg taken once daily, and placebo. Pomitral was taken once daily in both active arms. The two dose arms allow the dose-dependence of each effect to be examined, because the same outcomes are measured at the same time points in both arms and against the same placebo comparator.

### 3.4 Outcomes and statistical summary

Three outcomes were defined as change from baseline: systolic blood pressure (mmHg), LDL cholesterol (mmol/L) and HbA1c (percentage points). For every outcome, the reported value is the mean difference versus placebo. Negative values indicate that the change in the Pomitral arm was lower than the change in the placebo arm. Results are reported at weeks 12 and 24 for each dose. The analysis is descriptive and consists of the mean differences versus placebo; no confidence intervals, p-values or other inferential analyses are presented in this paper.

## 4. Results

Results are reported for every Pomitral dose at every time point. All mean differences versus placebo are negative, which means that the change from baseline was lower with Pomitral than with placebo for each measure in the reported values.

### 4.1 Systolic blood pressure

Table 1 gives the mean difference versus placebo in change from baseline in systolic blood pressure at week 12. At this time point, both doses were lower than placebo, and the 6 mg dose gave the larger mean difference. At week 24, the 3 mg dose gave a mean difference of -4.2 mmHg versus placebo, and the 6 mg dose gave a mean difference of -4.8 mmHg versus placebo. The 6 mg mean difference was therefore larger than the 3 mg difference at both time points.

| Pomitral dose | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in systolic blood pressure from baseline (mmHg) |

| 6 mg daily | Week 12 | -5.6 |

Table 2 gives the mean differences versus placebo in change from baseline in LDL cholesterol at weeks 12 and 24 for each dose. All four values are negative. At both time points, the 6 mg dose gave a larger mean difference than the 3 mg dose. The largest mean difference in the table is for the 6 mg dose at week 24. For the 3 mg dose, the mean difference at week 24 was more negative than at week 12, whereas for the 6 mg dose the mean difference at week 24 was also more negative than at week 12, so the difference between doses widened over time.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation of dose and time effects

The study hypothesis was that Pomitral taken once daily changes systolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and HbA1c more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on dose. For all three measures and both doses, the reported mean differences versus placebo were negative, which is consistent with the first part of the hypothesis. The dose-dependence part of the hypothesis is supported for systolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol, where the 6 mg dose gave the larger mean difference at both time points. For HbA1c, the pattern is mixed: the 6 mg dose gave the larger mean difference at week 24 but not at week 12, so the dose-dependence of this effect is not consistent across the two time points in the reported values.

The time course also differs between measures and doses. For the 3 mg dose, the mean differences for systolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and HbA1c were more negative at week 24 than at week 12. For the 6 mg dose, the systolic blood pressure mean difference was less negative at week 24 than at week 12, whereas the LDL cholesterol mean difference was more negative at week 24. These observations describe the reported values only. The design does not allow the mechanism of any of these changes to be identified, and the results should not be read as evidence of how Pomitral acts.

### 5.2 Limitations

This report presents mean differences versus placebo only. Measures of uncertainty, inferential statistics and baseline values are not reported here, so the precision of each estimate and the baseline status of participants cannot be assessed from this paper. The differences between doses and between time points are therefore descriptive, and small differences should be interpreted with caution.

The population was restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure who take metformin. The findings may not apply to adults without high blood pressure, to people who do not take metformin, or to people with other comorbidities that were not part of the study population.

The design includes only two fixed doses and two time points. Intermediate doses, doses above 6 mg, and use beyond week 24 were not examined, so the dose-response relationship and the sustained effects of Pomitral cannot be characterised beyond the values reported here.

## 6. Conclusion

This randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial compared Pomitral 3 mg and 6 mg once daily with placebo on change from baseline in systolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and HbA1c at weeks 12 and 24, in adults with type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure who take metformin. Across the reported values, all mean differences versus placebo were negative. The 6 mg dose gave larger mean differences than the 3 mg dose for systolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol at both time points, and for HbA1c at week 24, whereas the 3 mg dose gave the larger HbA1c mean difference at week 12.

The mean differences versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point give readers the estimates needed to compare doses and time points. Future work should report measures of uncertainty alongside these estimates and should examine whether the same pattern holds in other populations.

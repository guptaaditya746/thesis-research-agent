# Sorvelin added to metformin in type 2 diabetes: a 26-week dose-ranging trial

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who remain above glycaemic targets on metformin often need an additional once-daily agent, and the dose chosen for that agent is a practical decision for prescribers. The effects of once-daily Sorvelin added to metformin, relative to placebo, on change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose are reported here for each dose and time point, to support comparison between doses in this population. We report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in which adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin received Sorvelin 5 mg, 10 mg or 20 mg once daily, or placebo, with each measure assessed at week 13 and week 26. The summary measure for every outcome, dose and time point is the mean difference vs placebo in change from baseline. Mean differences ranged from -0.50 to -0.65 percentage points for HbA1c at week 13 and from -0.54 to -0.88 percentage points at week 26. For body weight, they ranged from -0.5 to -1.1 kg, and for fasting plasma glucose from -0.59 to -1.11 mmol/L. HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose differences became more negative with dose at both time points, whereas the body weight pattern was not monotonic at week 26. Only point estimates are reported; confidence intervals, p-values, sample sizes and safety data are not presented. The findings are descriptive, apply to one population, and provide a common scale for comparing doses. They do not establish clinical relevance or the superiority of any dose.

## 1. Introduction

Many adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin do not reach their glycaemic targets on that drug alone, and an additional once-daily agent is then often considered. Once such an agent has been selected, the prescriber still has to decide which dose to use. That choice shapes how much glucose lowering is achieved and what other effects, such as changes in body weight or fasting glucose, may accompany it. Dose selection is therefore a practical decision for prescribers, not a detail to be settled after the agent itself has been chosen.

Glycaemic control in this population frequently requires add-on therapy, which makes dose selection for add-on agents a routine question in clinical practice. Earlier add-on evaluations have typically compared a single dose with placebo, or have reported one primary outcome at one time point. Designs of this kind establish whether an agent acts at all, but they leave the shape of the dose response and the course of effects over time difficult to read. They also offer little basis for comparing doses on the same measures.

For Sorvelin, the information available to this study does not establish its effect, relative to placebo, on change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin across more than one dose and time point. The pharmacological class, mechanism of action and regulatory status of Sorvelin are outside the scope of this report. The plausibility of the chosen dose range is therefore judged from the trial data alone. The scope of the gap addressed here is deliberately narrow: one population, one background therapy and three outcome measures.

To address it, we report a parallel-group, placebo-controlled randomised trial in which adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin receive Sorvelin 5 mg, 10 mg or 20 mg once daily, or placebo. Each measure is assessed at week 13 and week 26. Our working hypothesis is that once-daily Sorvelin changes these measures more than placebo and that the size of the change depends on dose. The study is designed to describe the differences between each dose and placebo. It is not designed to rank Sorvelin against other agents, and we do not make such comparisons.

The contributions of this study are as follows. First, a placebo-controlled comparison of three once-daily doses of Sorvelin added to metformin, assessed at two time points. Second, mean differences from placebo in change from baseline for HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose, reported for every dose and time point on a consistent mean-difference scale. Third, a side-by-side presentation of these differences that allows readers to compare doses on the same measures when choosing a dose.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to this study can be grouped into three approaches: adding a glucose-lowering agent to metformin, varying the dose of an add-on agent, and reporting several glycaemic and weight outcomes together. Each is described below in general terms, with attention to what it offers and where it leaves the question addressed here open. No quantitative comparison with other trials is made in this report.

### 2.1 Placebo-controlled add-on trials to metformin

Randomised placebo-controlled evaluations of an additional agent given alongside metformin are the most common design for establishing whether an add-on therapy lowers glucose beyond what metformin achieves alone. The placebo comparison separates the contribution of the added agent from the background treatment and from the general effects of trial participation. Such trials, however, often organise their reporting around a single primary glycaemic outcome. Effects on body weight and fasting plasma glucose may then be reported less completely, or at only one time point, which makes it harder to judge the overall profile of an add-on agent. The present trial keeps the placebo comparison but reports HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose together, at two time points, for every dose.

### 2.2 Dose-ranging evaluations of add-on therapy

Dose-ranging studies compare several fixed doses of an add-on agent with a common placebo, and they are a natural design for questions about dose selection. Their interpretation depends on what is reported. When only one outcome or only one time point is presented, the shape of the dose-response relationship is unclear. It is not possible to tell whether a larger dose gives a proportionate effect, whether effects change between early and later assessment, or whether higher doses move other measures in the same direction. The present study assesses three doses against a shared placebo at two time points, so the dose pattern for each measure can be read directly from the mean differences rather than inferred from separate comparisons.

### 2.3 Multi-outcome reporting in glycaemic trials

A further body of work reports glycaemic control together with body weight and fasting glucose, reflecting the fact that these outcomes often matter jointly to prescribers and to people with diabetes. Reporting across such trials is frequently heterogeneous. Measures may be expressed on different scales, summarised against different baselines, or presented at time points that differ between outcomes, which complicates any attempt to compare effect size across outcomes. Multi-outcome reporting of this kind improves completeness but does not by itself guarantee comparability. The present study places all three measures on a consistent mean-difference-versus-placebo scale, with each measure in its own unit and applied consistently across doses and time points, so that a reader can compare effects across measures without converting between presentations.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

This study is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Sorvelin was compared against placebo. The population comprised adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Four arms were included: Sorvelin 5 mg once daily, Sorvelin 10 mg once daily, Sorvelin 20 mg once daily and placebo. Outcomes were assessed at two time points, week 13 and week 26, which together define the observation period reported here. Each Sorvelin dose is compared with placebo within the same population on metformin. Eligibility criteria, the randomisation procedure, blinding, sample sizes per arm and baseline characteristics are not described in this report, so the comparability of the arms is not assessed here.

### 3.2 Outcomes and summary measure

Three outcomes were defined: change in HbA1c from baseline, in percentage points; change in body weight from baseline, in kilograms; and change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, in millimoles per litre. For each outcome, dose and time point, the summary measure is the mean difference vs placebo in change from baseline. Negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. Values are reported to the precision at which they were summarised.

The statistical model used to derive the mean differences is not specified in this report, and the results are presented as point estimates only. No confidence intervals or p-values are given, so the precision of each estimate cannot be assessed here. Results are summarised descriptively in Tables 1 to 3 and in the text.

### 3.3 Reporting approach

The analysis population, the handling of missing data, the prespecification status of the trial and the designation of a primary endpoint are not reported here. Because no inferential statistics are presented, no multiplicity adjustment is applied or reported, and the two time points for each outcome are compared descriptively rather than tested. Safety outcomes, including adverse events, hypoglycaemia and discontinuations, are likewise outside the scope of this report. The presentation is therefore limited to the glycaemic and body weight measures specified in Section 3.2, and it is intended to be read together with the design limitations described in Section 5.2.

## 4. Results

Results are reported for every dose of Sorvelin at every time point. Table 1 gives the week 13 HbA1c differences, Table 2 gives body weight differences at weeks 13 and 26, and Table 3 gives fasting plasma glucose differences at weeks 13 and 26. Week 26 HbA1c differences are stated in the text, alongside the week 13 values in Table 1, so that both HbA1c time points are reported side by side in Section 4.1. All values are mean differences vs placebo in change from baseline.

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c

At week 13, the mean differences vs placebo in change in HbA1c were -0.50 percentage points for Sorvelin 5 mg, -0.59 percentage points for 10 mg and -0.65 percentage points for 20 mg (Table 1).

| Sorvelin dose (once daily) | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mg | Week 13 | -0.50 |
| 10 mg | Week 13 | -0.59 |
| 20 mg | Week 13 | -0.65 |

At week 26, the mean difference vs placebo was -0.54 percentage points for Sorvelin 5 mg, -0.80 percentage points for 10 mg and -0.88 percentage points for 20 mg. The magnitude of the mean difference was therefore larger at week 26 than at week 13 for each dose, and it increased with dose at both time points.

### 4.2 Change in body weight

Table 2 gives the mean differences vs placebo in change in body weight at weeks 13 and 26 for each dose. At week 13, the values were -0.5 kg for 5 mg, -0.6 kg for 10 mg and -1.1 kg for 20 mg. At week 26, the values were -0.6 kg for 5 mg, -0.5 kg for 10 mg and -0.9 kg for 20 mg. The 20 mg dose showed the largest mean difference at both time points. At week 26, however, the 10 mg value was not lower than the 5 mg value, so the dose pattern was not monotonic at that time point.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose pattern and interpretation

The working hypothesis was that once-daily Sorvelin changes HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on dose. All reported mean differences were negative, indicating a lower change than placebo for every measure, dose and time point. The dose pattern was clearest for HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose. For both measures the mean difference became more negative with each increase in dose at both time points, and for HbA1c the week 26 differences were larger than the week 13 differences at every dose. For body weight, the 20 mg dose gave the largest mean difference at both time points, but the week 26 pattern was not monotonic, because the 10 mg value was not lower than the 5 mg value. The descriptive pattern is therefore consistent with a dose dependence for HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose and is less clear for body weight. Because no formal test for dose trend and no interval around any estimate is reported, this pattern should be regarded as descriptive and not as confirmatory.

The magnitude of the effects should be interpreted with care. The largest HbA1c mean difference was -0.88 percentage points, and the largest fasting plasma glucose mean difference was -1.11 mmol/L. The body weight differences were between -0.5 kg and -1.1 kg. Whether these differences are clinically relevant cannot be determined from the present report, because the uncertainty around each estimate is not reported, and clinical relevance depends on that uncertainty as well as on the size of the estimate.

The differences between time points also deserve caution. The reported values are single summaries at each time point, and the report does not state whether the time points were prespecified as primary or secondary. Changes between week 13 and week 26 for any dose are therefore described but not interpreted as evidence of a trend.

The purpose of presenting a mean difference for each measure, dose and time point is to allow readers to compare doses on the same scale when selecting a dose. The results do not establish the superiority of any one dose. They show how the mean difference from placebo changes across the three doses and two time points studied, and they leave the choice of dose to be made in light of the clinical context and the trade-offs that each reader considers relevant.

### 5.2 Limitations

Only mean differences vs placebo are reported. Confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts and baseline values are not presented, so the precision of each estimate and the comparability of the groups cannot be judged from this report. The statistical model used to derive the estimates is also not specified, and the analysis population and handling of missing data are not described.

Safety and tolerability are not reported. Adverse events, hypoglycaemia, weight-related tolerability and discontinuations by dose are outside the scope of this presentation. Dose selection depends on these factors as well as on glycaemic and weight effects, so the present results cannot by themselves inform the trade-offs a prescriber would weigh when choosing among the three doses.

The population is limited to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. The findings should not be extended to people with other forms of diabetes, to people not taking metformin, or to other clinical populations without further study.

Only two time points (week 13 and week 26) and three doses (5, 10 and 20 mg once daily) were evaluated. Intermediate doses, other dosing schedules and longer durations of treatment are therefore not addressed, and the course of effects beyond week 26 is unknown.

## 6. Conclusion

### 6.1 Summary

This randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial assessed Sorvelin 5, 10 and 20 mg once daily added to metformin in adults with type 2 diabetes. The study reports mean differences vs placebo in change from baseline for HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose at weeks 13 and 26. All reported differences were negative, indicating a lower change than placebo. The descriptive dose pattern was clearest for HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose, which became more negative with dose at both time points, and it was less consistent for body weight at week 26. Because every measure is reported as a mean difference vs placebo for each dose and time point, readers can compare doses on the same scale when considering dose selection in this population. The report does not establish clinical relevance, precision or safety.

### 6.2 Future work

Future reports of this type would benefit from including uncertainty measures, a specification of the statistical model and analysis population, and safety data by dose, since these are absent from the present report and are needed to judge the precision, clinical relevance and tolerability of the estimates. Further evaluation of the population and dose range, including intermediate doses and longer treatment durations, would also be needed before the findings are applied more widely.

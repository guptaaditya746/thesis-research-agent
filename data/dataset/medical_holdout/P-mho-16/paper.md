# PMT-774 added to metformin: effects on blood pressure and LDL cholesterol

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin still carry cardiovascular risk, and systolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol are relevant contributors to that risk. The effect of PMT-774 at 6 mg once daily on the change from baseline in these two measures, relative to placebo, is not known. We conducted a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, comparing PMT-774 6 mg daily with placebo, with each measure taken at week 12 and week 24. Outcomes were change from baseline in systolic blood pressure (mmHg) and change from baseline in LDL cholesterol (mmol/L), each summarised as the mean difference versus placebo, where negative values indicate a lower value than placebo. For systolic blood pressure, the mean differences were -5.3 mmHg at week 12 and -5.2 mmHg at week 24. For LDL cholesterol, they were -0.29 mmol/L at week 12 and -0.40 mmol/L at week 24. The LDL cholesterol difference was larger at week 24, whereas the systolic blood pressure difference was essentially unchanged between the two time points. Only one dose was studied, so these results do not support conclusions about dose choice or about the full time course of the effect.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is commonly managed with metformin, and many adults with the condition take it as a long-term base therapy. Glucose control, however, does not remove cardiovascular risk. Adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin still carry residual risk, and two measurable contributors to that risk are systolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol. Changes in these measures under treatment are therefore relevant outcomes in their own right, not only as side effects of glucose-lowering therapy.

Earlier work on adding a second agent to metformin has been organised around mechanisms of action and glycaemic endpoints. Such work describes how add-on approaches are expected to act and reports outcomes that are chosen for their glucose-lowering effect. Blood pressure and lipid measures are often recorded in these studies, but they tend to be secondary, and they are rarely presented as a joint picture over time at one defined dose. As a result, the available descriptions do not clearly show how systolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol change together, or how those changes develop between early and later visits, for a single dose of an add-on agent.

For adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, the effect of PMT-774 at 6 mg once daily on the change from baseline in systolic blood pressure (mmHg) and LDL cholesterol (mmol/L), relative to placebo, at weeks 12 and 24 is not known from the available materials. Our working hypothesis is that PMT-774 taken once daily changes both measures more than placebo, and that the size of the change grows over time. Testing this requires a comparison that holds the population and the schedule constant and that measures both outcomes at more than one visit.

The present study is a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial in adults with type 2 diabetes who are taking metformin. Participants are allocated to PMT-774 6 mg daily or to placebo. The trial measures change from baseline in systolic blood pressure and in LDL cholesterol at week 12 and at week 24. For each measure and time point, the result is summarised as a mean difference versus placebo, with negative values indicating a lower value than placebo.

The scope of the paper is deliberately narrow. Only the 6 mg daily dose is studied, so the paper reports mean differences versus placebo for this dose alone and does not compare doses with one another. The growing-effect hypothesis is assessed only against the two time points, weeks 12 and 24, and it is not extended to longer durations. Conclusions about dose choice or about the full time course therefore lie outside what this study can support.

This study makes three contributions. First, it provides a defined, randomised, placebo-controlled estimate of how PMT-774 at 6 mg daily changes systolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Second, it reports each measure separately at each of two time points, so that the time course of the change is visible rather than pooled. Third, it gives readers the mean difference versus placebo for each measure and time point at this dose, as a reference point for interpreting the cardiometabolic effects of PMT-774 in this population.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering agents to metformin

Randomised add-on trials have examined a second agent given alongside metformin in adults with type 2 diabetes. These trials are typically designed around glycaemic control, with the main endpoints chosen to reflect glucose lowering in people whose disease is not adequately managed by metformin alone. Cardiovascular-related measures are sometimes included, but they are usually secondary endpoints. They are often reported with limited detail, such as a summary statement or a brief table, and they are not the main reason for the trial. The present study takes the opposite approach. It names systolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol as its defined outcome measures and reports both as mean differences versus placebo.

### 2.2 Cardiometabolic side-effect reporting in diabetes trials

A second body of work records blood pressure and lipid changes alongside diabetes treatment, usually as part of safety or tolerability reporting. These records are valuable for detecting unexpected effects, but they often summarise measures across pooled doses, pooled visits, or the full treatment period. Pooling can obscure the effect of a particular dose and the way that effect changes over time. Some reports also present changes from baseline without a comparison to a concurrent control, which makes it harder to separate the effect of the drug from the natural course of the disease. The present study reports each measure separately, for one stated dose (6 mg daily), at each of two time points (week 12 and week 24), against a concurrent placebo group.

### 2.3 Dose and time-course comparisons

Studies that compare several doses or durations of an investigational agent form a third group. Dose-ranging designs are used to select a dose for later development, and they generally report the effect of each dose on the primary endpoint. Time-course information is valuable for the same purpose, since it shows whether an effect is established early or continues to develop. In practice, the time course of cardiometabolic measures is often reported less systematically than the dose comparison, and it may be given only at the end of treatment. The present study offers a time-course view at two time points for a single dose. It does not compare doses, and it states this limit explicitly, so that readers do not read the single-dose results as evidence about dose response.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

We conducted a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, comparing PMT-774 with placebo. The study population consisted of adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Participants were allocated to one of two arms: PMT-774 at 6 mg once daily, or placebo. PMT-774 is referred to by its development code throughout this paper. The only active dose studied was 6 mg daily, and no other dose is reported.

### 3.2 Outcomes and statistical summary

Two outcomes were defined. The first was change from baseline in systolic blood pressure, expressed in mmHg. The second was change from baseline in LDL cholesterol, expressed in mmol/L. Each outcome was assessed at week 12 and at week 24.

For each outcome and time point, the result is the mean difference versus placebo. Negative values mean that the value in the PMT-774 arm was lower than in the placebo arm. The paper reports these mean differences only. No additional analyses, confidence intervals, or p-values are described here, and dispersion measures are not reported. Results are therefore interpreted as direction and magnitude of the mean difference at each stated dose and time point.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal findings

The first part of the working hypothesis, that PMT-774 at 6 mg daily changes both measures more than placebo, is supported by the direction of the results. Systolic blood pressure was lower than placebo at week 12, with a mean difference of -5.3 mmHg, and at week 24, with a mean difference of -5.2 mmHg. LDL cholesterol was lower than placebo at week 12, with a mean difference of -0.29 mmol/L, and at week 24, with a mean difference of -0.40 mmol/L.

The second part of the hypothesis, that the change grows over time, is supported for LDL cholesterol but not for systolic blood pressure. The LDL cholesterol difference was larger at week 24 than at week 12. The systolic blood pressure difference was essentially unchanged between the two time points, so the hypothesis of a growing effect is not supported for this measure. The pattern suggests that the systolic blood pressure difference was established by week 12 and was maintained at week 24, whereas the LDL cholesterol difference continued to develop over the observed interval. The materials do not establish the mechanisms underlying either pattern.

The conclusions are restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin and to the 6 mg daily dose. They should not be extended to other populations, to other doses, or to other background therapies.

### 5.2 Limitations

Only one dose (6 mg daily) is reported. The study therefore cannot indicate how the effect varies with dose, and the expected contribution of helping readers choose a dose is only partly met. Dose comparison requires further study.

Only two time points, week 12 and week 24, are reported. The shape of the time course between these visits, and any change beyond week 24, is not known. The growing-effect hypothesis was tested only against these two time points, so conclusions about the long-term trajectory of the effect cannot be drawn.

Only mean differences versus placebo are reported, without measures of uncertainty. Precision therefore cannot be judged from this paper, and the size of the differences relative to their variability is not established here. The reported differences should be read as point summaries rather than as firm estimates of effect size.

## 6. Conclusion

### 6.1 Summary

In adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, PMT-774 at 6 mg daily, compared with placebo, was associated with lower systolic blood pressure and lower LDL cholesterol at week 12 and at week 24. The mean differences versus placebo were -5.3 mmHg and -5.2 mmHg for systolic blood pressure, and -0.29 mmol/L and -0.40 mmol/L for LDL cholesterol.

The LDL cholesterol difference grew between the two time points, while the systolic blood pressure difference did not. Because only the 6 mg daily dose was studied, these results describe a single dose and two time points. Comparison across doses, and a fuller account of the time course, require further study.

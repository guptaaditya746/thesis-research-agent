# Saventrol for glucose and body weight in type 2 diabetes with obesity

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity who take metformin often require an additional therapy, yet the joint effects of such a therapy on body weight, HbA1c and systolic blood pressure relative to placebo are not described together for this population. We report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin, comparing Saventrol 10 mg, 20 mg and 40 mg taken once daily with placebo. Each outcome was assessed at week 12 and week 26 and is expressed as a mean difference versus placebo, where negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. At week 12, the mean differences in body weight were -1.2 kg at 10 mg, -1.5 kg at 20 mg and -1.8 kg at 40 mg. At week 26, the mean difference in body weight was -1.4 kg at 10 mg, -2.1 kg at 20 mg and -2.2 kg at 40 mg. HbA1c mean differences ranged from -0.40 to -0.71 percentage points, reaching -0.71 percentage points at 40 mg at week 26. The systolic blood pressure mean difference was -3.1 mmHg at 20 mg at week 26, whereas 40 mg gave -2.8 mmHg at that time point. The magnitude of the body weight and HbA1c changes increased with dose, whereas the systolic blood pressure pattern was not monotonic at week 26. Because the reported summary contains no uncertainty estimates, these differences are descriptive. The study provides a table of mean differences by measure, dose and time point within the trial population.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is frequently accompanied by obesity and raised blood pressure, and many adults with the condition are first treated with metformin. When glycaemic control on metformin is insufficient, an additional agent is commonly added. Its effects on body weight and blood pressure matter alongside its effect on glycaemia, because all three bear on the cardiometabolic risk that motivates treatment. These measures are not always characterised together in a single study.

Evaluations that centre on one outcome, or that follow patients over a single time point, make it difficult to judge whether effects on weight and blood pressure are comparable with effects on glycaemia, or whether those effects persist. Fixed doses at several levels are needed to show whether larger doses produce larger changes. These limitations matter most when the aim is dose selection rather than confirmation that an agent acts.

This study addresses a specific question in adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity who take metformin. What are the differences from placebo in change in body weight, change in HbA1c and change in systolic blood pressure from baseline with Saventrol, and does the size of each change depend on dose? The mechanism and prior evidence for Saventrol are outside the scope of this report.

We conducted a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial. Participants were allocated to Saventrol 10 mg, 20 mg or 40 mg taken once daily, or to placebo. Each of the three measures was assessed at week 12 and at week 26, so that dose and time effects can be read from the same set of comparisons. The working hypothesis was that Saventrol changes all three measures relative to placebo and that the size of the change depends on dose.

Results are expressed as mean differences versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point. The differences are descriptive. We do not extrapolate them beyond the population studied, and the study was not designed to rank Saventrol against other active agents.

The contribution of this study is twofold. It reports three measures together, at three once-daily doses and two time points, within a single placebo-controlled comparison. It also sets out the dose and time pattern of each measure separately, so that dose dependence is examined for each outcome rather than assumed from one of them.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering therapy in metformin-treated patients

Many trials have evaluated additional glucose-lowering agents in people whose glycaemia remains above target on metformin. These are typically placebo-controlled studies in which metformin is continued as background therapy, and the design is built around a glycaemic primary endpoint. Analysis therefore centres largely on change in HbA1c, while body weight and blood pressure are often recorded as secondary or exploratory measures. Where these outcomes are reported, they are frequently summarised without a structure that separates dose levels and time points, which makes the non-glycaemic effects harder to read alongside the glycaemic ones. The present trial keeps the metformin-treated population and the placebo comparator of this approach, but reports HbA1c alongside body weight and systolic blood pressure, each by dose and time point.

### 2.2 Weight-focused interventions in people with diabetes

A second body of work tests agents or programmes whose main aim is body weight reduction in adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity. These studies generally use weight as the primary outcome and are designed around its trajectory. Glycaemic and blood pressure responses are typically reported as safety or secondary information, and follow-up is not always arranged so that early and later effects on each measure can be separated. Such designs address whether weight changes, but leave open how the glycaemic and blood pressure responses relate to weight over time. The present design measures weight, HbA1c and systolic blood pressure at the same two time points, so that the three responses can be compared within one trial.

### 2.3 Dose-ranging evaluations of multi-outcome agents

Parallel-group dose-ranging trials compare several fixed doses of an agent with placebo and are a standard way to examine how response changes with exposure. In multi-outcome settings, however, dose-response is often summarised for a single primary endpoint, and the pattern seen for that endpoint may be assumed to hold for the others. Where secondary outcomes are presented, they may be pooled across doses or shown without per-dose estimates, so a monotonic relationship for one measure is not verified for the rest. This limitation matters most when the purpose is dose selection on several outcomes at once. In the present study, the three fixed doses are reported separately for each measure, so that dose dependence can be examined directly rather than assumed.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

We conducted a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Saventrol was compared against placebo. The population was adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity who were on metformin. Sample sizes, baseline values, randomisation ratios, trial registration identifiers, endpoint definitions, the statistical model and the handling of missing data or withdrawals are not reported in this paper. The design is therefore described only as far as it bears on the reported mean differences.

### 3.2 Interventions and comparator

Four arms were studied: Saventrol 10 mg taken once daily, Saventrol 20 mg taken once daily, Saventrol 40 mg taken once daily, and placebo. Placebo is the only comparator used for every reported value. Each Saventrol dose is compared with placebo separately, and no Saventrol doses are pooled.

### 3.3 Outcomes and time points

Three outcomes were assessed: change in body weight from baseline (kg), change in HbA1c from baseline (percentage points) and change in systolic blood pressure from baseline (mmHg). Each outcome was assessed at week 12 and at week 26. The summary statistic for every outcome is the mean difference versus placebo, where negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. Values are reported as recorded, with one decimal place for body weight and systolic blood pressure and two decimal places for HbA1c. The reported summary contains mean differences only. Confidence intervals, p-values and patient counts are not included, so the precision of each estimate is not described here.

## 4. Results

Results are reported for every Saventrol dose at every time point. Table 1 gives body weight at week 12, Table 2 gives HbA1c at both time points, and Table 3 gives systolic blood pressure at both time points. All values are mean differences versus placebo. Week 26 body weight values are stated in the text of Section 4.1 rather than in Table 1, so that the table is limited to the week 12 values.

### 4.1 Change in body weight

Table 1: Change in body weight from baseline, Saventrol minus placebo, at week 12, by daily dose

| Saventrol dose (mg daily) | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in body weight (kg) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | week 12 | -1.2 |
| 20 | week 12 | -1.5 |
| 40 | week 12 | -1.8 |

At week 12, all three doses lowered body weight relative to placebo, and the mean difference became more negative as the dose increased, from -1.2 kg at 10 mg daily to -1.8 kg at 40 mg daily (Table 1). At week 26, the mean difference was -1.4 kg at 10 mg daily, -2.1 kg at 20 mg daily and -2.2 kg at 40 mg daily. The magnitude of the body weight change therefore increased with dose at both time points, although the step from 20 mg to 40 mg was smaller at week 26 than the step from 10 mg to 20 mg.

### 4.2 Change in HbA1c

Table 2: Change in HbA1c from baseline, Saventrol minus placebo, by daily dose and time point

| Saventrol dose (mg daily) | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | week 12 | -0.40 |
| 10 | week 26 | -0.48 |
| 20 | week 12 | -0.57 |
| 20 | week 26 | -0.65 |
| 40 | week 12 | -0.64 |
| 40 | week 26 | -0.71 |

Table 2 shows that the HbA1c mean differences ranged from -0.40 to -0.71 percentage points. At each time point, the magnitude of the HbA1c change increased with dose, from -0.40 and -0.48 percentage points at 10 mg daily to -0.64 and -0.71 percentage points at 40 mg daily at week 12 and week 26, respectively. For every dose, the mean difference was larger in magnitude at week 26 than at week 12.

### 4.3 Change in systolic blood pressure

Table 3: Change in systolic blood pressure from baseline, Saventrol minus placebo, by daily dose and time point

| Saventrol dose (mg daily) | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in systolic blood pressure (mmHg) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | week 12 | -2.0 |
| 10 | week 26 | -2.1 |
| 20 | week 12 | -2.0 |
| 20 | week 26 | -3.1 |
| 40 | week 12 | -2.9 |
| 40 | week 26 | -2.8 |

At week 12, the 10 mg and 20 mg doses each gave a mean difference of -2.0 mmHg, and the 40 mg dose gave -2.9 mmHg, the largest change at that time point (Table 3). At week 26, the pattern changed. The 20 mg dose gave -3.1 mmHg, which is larger in magnitude than the -2.8 mmHg recorded at 40 mg, and the 10 mg dose gave -2.1 mmHg. The systolic blood pressure dose pattern is therefore not monotonic at week 26. Across time points, the 10 mg and 20 mg doses showed larger mean differences at week 26 than at week 12, whereas the 40 mg dose showed a slightly smaller mean difference at week 26.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose dependence across measures

The hypothesis was that Saventrol changes body weight, HbA1c and systolic blood pressure relative to placebo, and that the size of the change depends on dose. Every reported mean difference is negative, indicating a lower change than placebo at each dose and time point. For body weight and HbA1c, the mean differences are ordered by dose: at both time points, the magnitude of the change increased from 10 mg to 40 mg daily. For systolic blood pressure, the ordering holds at week 12 only in part, since the 10 mg and 20 mg doses were equal, and at week 26 the 20 mg dose gave a larger change than the 40 mg dose. The dose-dependence hypothesis is therefore consistent with the body weight and HbA1c results and is only partly consistent with the systolic blood pressure results. Because no uncertainty estimates are reported, none of these orderings has been tested formally, and the small differences between adjacent doses cannot be separated from sampling variation.

### 5.2 Time course

Comparing the two time points using Tables 1 to 3 and the text of Section 4.1 shows that body weight and HbA1c changes were larger in magnitude at week 26 than at week 12. For body weight, the week 26 mean difference was more negative than the week 12 value for each of the three doses. For HbA1c, every dose was larger at week 26 than at week 12. Systolic blood pressure did not follow this pattern uniformly: the 10 mg and 20 mg doses were larger at week 26, while the 40 mg dose was slightly smaller at week 26 than at week 12. Because these blood pressure differences are small and no uncertainty estimates are available, the week 26 pattern is not interpreted as a mechanistic time course, and it should not be assumed to mirror the weight or HbA1c trajectories.

### 5.3 Limitations

Several limitations follow from the design and the reported summary. Only mean differences from placebo are reported. No uncertainty estimates, patient counts or baseline values are presented, so the precision of each estimate, and whether any difference from placebo is statistically distinguishable from zero, cannot be judged from this paper. Safety and tolerability data, including adverse events, hypoglycaemia, gastrointestinal effects and discontinuations, are not reported here. A dose recommendation would require benefit-risk information of this kind, so the tables alone do not establish which dose should be preferred, and no explicit dose-selection criterion is applied. The clinical importance of the reported magnitudes is also not judged, since no threshold for clinically important change and no comparison with placebo response or other agents is made.

The population is limited to adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin, and the findings do not generalise to other populations, including people not taking metformin or without obesity. Only two time points, week 12 and week 26, are reported, so the trajectory between and beyond these points is not described. The non-monotonic systolic blood pressure pattern at week 26 shows that a single ordered dose effect cannot be assumed for every measure. For these reasons, the dose tables should be interpreted descriptively within the trial population.

## 6. Conclusion

This randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial examined whether Saventrol at 10 mg, 20 mg and 40 mg taken once daily changes body weight, HbA1c and systolic blood pressure relative to placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin. At each reported dose and time point, the mean difference from placebo was negative, indicating a lower change than placebo. The magnitude of the body weight and HbA1c changes increased with dose, whereas the systolic blood pressure pattern was not monotonic at week 26. Because uncertainty estimates, safety data and a dose-selection criterion are not reported in this paper, the findings are descriptive. The practical contribution of the study is a set of mean differences versus placebo by measure, dose and time point, which may inform dose discussions within the trial population without extrapolation beyond it.

# Saventrol in type 2 diabetes with obesity: two-year follow-up

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin often need an add-on agent that is judged on both glycaemic control and body weight, yet the effect of Saventrol on change in body weight and change in HbA1c from baseline, compared with placebo, and whether this effect depends on dose, is not established for this population. We report a two-year, randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled study in which Saventrol 20 mg once daily, Saventrol 40 mg once daily or placebo was given, with each measure assessed at week 52 and week 104. Every result is expressed as the mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point. At week 52, body weight changed by -2.4 kg with 20 mg and -2.9 kg with 40 mg relative to placebo, and HbA1c changed by -0.67 percentage points with 20 mg and -0.72 percentage points with 40 mg. At week 104, body weight changed by -2.3 kg with 20 mg and -2.8 kg with 40 mg, and HbA1c by -0.67 and -0.76 percentage points, respectively. Both doses gave lower change than placebo on both measures at both time points, and the 40 mg dose gave the larger mean reductions on both measures at both time points. Because only mean differences are reported here, without measures of precision, the findings are offered to support dose choice within the scope of this population and these two outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes and obesity frequently occur together. Many adults with type 2 diabetes also carry excess body weight, and the two conditions compound one another in terms of cardiometabolic risk and treatment complexity. Metformin is widely used as an early glucose-lowering therapy in this group. Even so, a considerable proportion of metformin-treated adults remain with both inadequate glycaemic control and obesity, and they often need an additional agent.

Choosing an add-on agent for such patients involves two outcomes that are both clinically relevant. Glycaemic control, usually expressed as HbA1c, is the central target of diabetes treatment. Body weight also matters, because weight reduction can improve several metabolic risk factors and is an explicit goal in people with obesity. An agent that lowers HbA1c while leaving weight unchanged addresses only part of the problem, and an agent that reduces weight without a clear glycaemic effect does the same. An add-on therapy for this population should therefore be judged on both measures, considered together rather than in isolation.

Despite this, the effect of Saventrol in this setting is not established. For adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin, it is not known how Saventrol changes body weight and HbA1c from baseline relative to placebo, or whether the size of any such change depends on dose. Existing knowledge about the agent in this population is therefore insufficient to guide the choice between doses. The present report addresses only this population and these two measures. It does not address other patient groups, other outcomes, or safety in general.

This study is a two-year randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial. Adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin are allocated to Saventrol 20 mg once daily, Saventrol 40 mg once daily, or placebo. Change from baseline in body weight and in HbA1c is assessed at week 52 and week 104. Each measure is reported as the mean difference versus placebo for each dose and time point, so that the dose pattern across the two-year period can be read directly from the reported contrasts.

The contributions of this study are as follows. First, it provides a two-year, randomised, placebo-controlled comparison of Saventrol 20 mg and 40 mg daily with placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin. Second, it assesses change in body weight and change in HbA1c from baseline together, as outcomes of equal priority. Third, it reports the mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point, giving readers the information needed to choose a dose for this population.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering trials on metformin

Placebo-controlled add-on trials are the most established design for testing a new agent in people whose glycaemia is not controlled on metformin. Such studies randomise participants to the investigational agent or to placebo while metformin continues, and they compare change in HbA1c from baseline at a defined endpoint. The design isolates the added effect of the agent against a background of stable metformin therapy, which is the comparison most relevant to the population considered here.

These trials typically centre on glycaemic control, which is their primary target. Body weight is usually recorded as a secondary or safety outcome, and where it is reported it is often described with less detail. As a result, the weight effect in participants with obesity may be under-described. The present study differs by specifying change in body weight alongside change in HbA1c, with both expressed as mean differences versus placebo in adults with obesity on metformin.

### 2.2 Weight-focused pharmacotherapy in diabetes with obesity

A separate body of work evaluates weight-management drugs in adults who have both type 2 diabetes and obesity. These trials are organised around weight loss as the primary objective, and participants are often followed within a structured lifestyle programme. Glycaemic change is typically captured as a secondary measure. Some studies of this kind examine the agent alongside existing glucose-lowering therapy, which is relevant to patients who take metformin.

Because weight is the main focus, these designs often assess glycaemic change only secondarily. They may also not follow the effect over two years, or at more than one dose, so the durability of glycaemic effects and the dose dependence of each measure are less clearly described. The present study gives weight and HbA1c equal priority and follows both to week 104, with an intermediate assessment at week 52.

### 2.3 Multi-dose add-on designs

Parallel-group trials that compare two fixed daily doses of an add-on agent with placebo are a common way to examine dose dependence. Allocating participants to distinct doses within one trial allows the question of whether a larger dose produces a larger effect on each outcome to be addressed directly. Results are often summarised at a single time point or pooled across doses. This is efficient, but it can obscure how the dose relationship changes over time.

Dose dependence inferred from a single time point, or from pooled data, limits the choice of dose for a given outcome and duration. A dose that appears adequate at one time may behave differently at another. The present study compares two fixed doses with placebo at each time point, so the dose dependence of both measures can be read directly from the reported mean differences.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

The study is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Saventrol is compared against placebo. The population consists of adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity who are on metformin. The study addresses change in body weight and change in HbA1c from baseline in this population only. This report is limited to the mean differences versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point. Baseline values and patient counts are not reported here.

### 3.2 Interventions and comparator

Three arms are included: Saventrol 20 mg daily, Saventrol 40 mg daily and placebo. Saventrol is taken once daily in both active arms. Placebo is the sole comparator, and every value is expressed relative to placebo. No other comparator is included in the analysis reported here.

### 3.3 Outcome measures and effect estimate

Two outcomes are defined. The first is change in body weight from baseline, expressed in kilograms (kg). The second is change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. Both outcomes are assessed at week 52 and at week 104.

The effect estimate for every outcome, dose and time point is the mean difference versus placebo. Negative values indicate a lower change than placebo, and positive values would indicate a higher change. Values are given exactly as stated, with their decimals and units, and no values are recomputed or combined across doses or time points.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in body weight

Table 1 presents the change in body weight from baseline versus placebo at week 52, by Saventrol dose. At week 52, Saventrol 20 mg daily gave a mean difference versus placebo of -2.4 kg, and Saventrol 40 mg daily gave a mean difference of -2.9 kg. Both values are negative, indicating a lower change in body weight than placebo.

| Saventrol dose | Time point | Change in body weight vs placebo (kg) |
|---|---|---|
| 20 mg daily | Week 52 | -2.4 |
| 40 mg daily | Week 52 | -2.9 |

At week 104, the mean difference versus placebo was -2.3 kg for Saventrol 20 mg daily and -2.8 kg for Saventrol 40 mg daily. These week 104 values are reported in the text rather than in Table 1. Across both time points, both doses showed a lower change in body weight than placebo, and the 40 mg dose showed the more negative value at each time point.

### 4.2 Change in HbA1c

Table 2 presents the change in HbA1c from baseline versus placebo at weeks 52 and 104, by Saventrol dose. At week 52, Saventrol 20 mg daily gave a mean difference of -0.67 percentage points, and Saventrol 40 mg daily gave -0.72 percentage points. At week 104, Saventrol 20 mg daily gave -0.67 percentage points and Saventrol 40 mg daily gave -0.76 percentage points.

Table 2: Change in HbA1c from baseline versus placebo at weeks 52 and 104, by Saventrol dose (mean difference vs placebo; negative values indicate a lower change than placebo)

| Saventrol dose | Time point | Change in HbA1c vs placebo (percentage points) |

For the 20 mg dose, the mean difference versus placebo was the same at both time points. For the 40 mg dose, the mean difference was larger in magnitude at week 104 than at week 52. At each time point, the 40 mg dose gave the more negative value of the two doses, and all four values in Table 2 are negative, indicating a lower HbA1c change than placebo.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose and time pattern

Across both outcomes, both Saventrol doses gave a lower change from baseline than placebo at both time points. This direction is consistent with the core hypothesis that Saventrol taken once daily changes body weight and HbA1c more than placebo. The pattern for dose is also consistent with the hypothesis that the size of the change depends on dose. The 40 mg dose gave the larger mean reductions on both measures at both time points, while the 20 mg dose gave smaller ones. The dose ordering was the same for body weight and for HbA1c, which suggests that the two measures respond in the same direction to the increase from 20 mg to 40 mg.

The time pattern differed between the two measures. For body weight, the mean differences were slightly larger in magnitude at week 52 than at week 104 for both doses. For HbA1c, the 20 mg mean difference was the same at both time points, and the 40 mg mean difference was larger at week 104 than at week 52. The reported HbA1c values therefore did not show a loss of glycaemic effect over the two-year period at either dose, whereas the body weight values were somewhat smaller at the later time point.

These mean differences support the choice of dose for this population, because each measure, dose and time point can be compared directly with placebo. They do not, however, establish clinical equivalence between doses, or a clinically meaningful threshold for either outcome. Any conclusion about the relevance of the size of these differences must be drawn by the reader within the scope of the values reported.

### 5.2 Limitations

Only mean differences versus placebo are reported in this study. Interval estimates, p-values and patient counts are not part of this report, so the precision of each estimate cannot be judged. The spread of the individual responses is likewise not described, and the stability of the differences across participants cannot be assessed from the values presented.

The population is restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin. The findings do not extend to people without obesity, to people not taking metformin, or to other patient groups. The two outcomes studied are body weight and HbA1c only, and the report does not address other glycaemic, metabolic or safety measures.

Only one comparator, placebo, is included, and only two fixed doses of Saventrol, 20 mg and 40 mg daily, are assessed. The dose relationship is therefore described across two levels only, and no dose between or beyond these levels can be inferred. Assessment is limited to week 52 and week 104, so the findings describe these two time points and not the trajectory between or after them.

Future reports should add measures of precision and baseline and participant-level information, and the findings should be considered in wider populations before general use.

## 6. Conclusion

In adults with type 2 diabetes and obesity on metformin, Saventrol 20 mg and 40 mg taken once daily gave a lower change in body weight and in HbA1c than placebo at week 52 and week 104. Both doses were lower than placebo on both measures at both time points. The 40 mg dose gave the larger mean reductions on both measures at both time points, which is consistent with a dose-dependent effect within the two doses studied.

The mean differences versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point are provided to help readers choose a dose within the scope of this study. Because these values are reported without measures of precision and only in this population, they should be read as a basis for dose choice in this population and not as a general statement about Saventrol.

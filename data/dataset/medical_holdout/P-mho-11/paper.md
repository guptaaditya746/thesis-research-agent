# Tembrexil (TMB-399), Dulmarexa (DLM-868) and Saventrol (SVN-171) added to metformin: a three-arm placebo-controlled trial

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin often need an add-on agent, and the choice of add-on is judged not only on glucose lowering but also on body weight and fasting plasma glucose. For this population, the effects of Tembrexil (TMB-399), Dulmarexa (DLM-868) and Saventrol (SVN-171) on change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose relative to placebo are not known. We conducted a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Participants were allocated to Tembrexil (TMB-399) 30 mg daily, Dulmarexa (DLM-868) 200 mg daily, Saventrol (SVN-171) 20 mg daily, or placebo, with each agent taken once daily and all outcomes assessed at week 26. Every result is reported as a mean difference vs placebo, in which a negative value indicates a value lower than placebo. For HbA1c, the mean differences vs placebo were -0.95, -0.67 and -0.59 percentage points for Tembrexil (TMB-399), Dulmarexa (DLM-868) and Saventrol (SVN-171), respectively. For body weight, the mean differences were -1.3, -0.1 and -2.1 kg, and for fasting plasma glucose they were -1.12, -0.78 and -0.69 mmol/L, in the same agent order. These values are dose-specific and refer to a single time point. The contribution of the study is a set of dose-specific mean differences vs placebo on three measures, intended to support dose choice. Cross-agent comparisons are interpreted cautiously because the doses differ.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is commonly managed first with metformin, yet many adults who take it do not reach their glycaemic target and need an add-on agent. Choosing an add-on is not a matter of glucose lowering alone. Clinicians and patients also weigh how a candidate affects body weight and fasting plasma glucose, because these measures bear on daily self-management, treatment burden and the overall balance of benefit. An add-on that lowers HbA1c but is judged only on that one outcome leaves much of the decision unexplained. For this reason, the effect of each candidate on these measures needs to be quantified against a placebo reference, so that the size of the change can be read directly rather than inferred.

Earlier add-on evaluations do not make this choice easy to make. They differ in population, background therapy, trial duration, comparator and the dose that was studied. Some report glycaemic endpoints in detail while giving body weight or fasting plasma glucose only as a brief summary; others compare several doses but restrict attention to a single measure. Because these choices vary from study to study, the dose-specific effects of different agents are hard to read side by side, and differences that appear between trials may reflect design rather than the drug itself.

The present study addresses this gap with a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial in adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin. Three once-daily agents are compared with placebo, each at one stated dose: Tembrexil (TMB-399) 30 mg, Dulmarexa (DLM-868) 200 mg and Saventrol (SVN-171) 20 mg. All arms are assessed at week 26 on change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose. The working hypothesis is that each agent changes these measures more than placebo, and that the size of the change differs between agents.

Each measure is reported as the mean difference versus placebo for every agent and dose, so that readers can judge the dose-specific effect size on each outcome rather than relying on a single summary. Because the three doses differ, comparisons between agents are interpreted cautiously; the design does not establish that one dose is equivalent or superior to another. The scope is deliberately narrow: three doses, one population and one time point. Questions about longer treatment, other doses or other background therapies are outside what this study can answer.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it provides a placebo-referenced estimate of the change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose at week 26 for each of three agents at its stated dose, within a single protocol. Second, it presents these estimates in a form that allows dose-specific effects to be read together, which supports choosing a dose on the three measures jointly. Third, it states the limits of cross-agent comparison explicitly, so that the evidence is used for the question it was designed to answer.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to add-on therapy in metformin-treated adults can be grouped into three approaches. Each is described below by its design, and the limitations are stated as hypotheses about where the evidence may be incomplete for the present question.

### 2.1 Single-agent add-on trials against placebo

Placebo-controlled add-on trials form the most common body of evidence. In these studies one agent is added to metformin and compared with placebo, usually with a primary endpoint on glycaemic control and a set of secondary measures. Their strength is internal validity for a single comparison: randomisation and a placebo arm separate the effect of the agent from background therapy and from the natural course of the disease. Their limitation is that each study tends to define its own population, duration and dose, so comparing two agents requires indirect inference across different designs. Differences in baseline HbA1c, background metformin dose and length of follow-up may shift estimates in ways that are hard to separate from drug effects. The present study uses the same placebo, population, time point and outcome definitions for all three agents, so that placebo-referenced differences are computed on a common basis rather than inferred across trials.

### 2.2 Multi-agent and multi-dose comparisons

Some protocols compare several agents or several doses within one trial. Such designs allow direct comparison under shared conditions and are useful for seeing how an effect changes with dose. However, they often prioritise one primary glycaemic endpoint and report other measures in less depth, so body weight and fasting plasma glucose may be described only briefly, or only for some doses. Multi-arm designs also divide the sample among more arms, which may widen the uncertainty on secondary measures. This study adopts a multi-arm structure but reports each agent at a stated dose on all three measures and at a stated time point. The dose-specific picture is therefore complete for each agent, rather than summarised for the trial as a whole.

### 2.3 Weight and fasting glucose as supporting outcomes

A third group of glycaemic add-on studies reports body weight and fasting plasma glucose alongside HbA1c. These outcomes are often treated as supporting: they are measured because they matter clinically, but the trial is organised and summarised around glycaemic control. Supporting outcomes are frequently given as a single summary per agent, or in a figure without the underlying values, which limits the reader's ability to compare doses or to see whether a weight or fasting glucose change follows the HbA1c change. Fasting plasma glucose may also respond on a different timescale from HbA1c, so reporting it only in summary can hide dose-dependent patterns. The present study treats HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose as equal evaluation metrics. Each is reported as a mean difference versus placebo for every agent and dose, so that no measure is relegated to a supporting role.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and participants

This study was a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial. Adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin were randomised to one of four arms: Tembrexil (TMB-399) 30 mg once daily, Dulmarexa (DLM-868) 200 mg once daily, Saventrol (SVN-171) 20 mg once daily, or placebo. Each participant was assigned to a single arm, and the three active agents were each evaluated at one stated dose. The population was restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, and the design compares every active arm with the same placebo arm.

### 3.2 Outcomes and time point

Three evaluation metrics were defined. The first was the change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. The second was the change in body weight from baseline, expressed in kg. The third was the change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, expressed in mmol/L. Every outcome was assessed at week 26. The reference comparator for all active arms was placebo.

### 3.3 Effect measure and reporting

Every value reported in this paper is a mean difference vs placebo. Negative values indicate that the change in the active arm was lower than the change in the placebo arm. Values are reported with the decimals and units used in the source data. Confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts and baseline values are not part of this report.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Glycaemic control (HbA1c)

Results are reported for every dose of each agent at every time point, which in this study is week 26. At week 26, Tembrexil (TMB-399) 30 mg daily gave a mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline of -0.95 percentage points. Dulmarexa (DLM-868) 200 mg daily gave a mean difference vs placebo of -0.67 percentage points, and Saventrol (SVN-171) 20 mg daily gave a mean difference vs placebo of -0.59 percentage points. All three HbA1c values are negative, and in descending order of magnitude the agents are Tembrexil (TMB-399), Dulmarexa (DLM-868) and Saventrol (SVN-171). This ordering is descriptive only and is not supported by statistical inference in this report.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal findings

At the studied doses and at week 26, all three agents gave values lower than placebo on all three measures. The size of the change differed between agents, and the ordering of agents was not the same across measures. For HbA1c, Tembrexil (TMB-399) 30 mg daily showed the largest mean difference, whereas for body weight Saventrol (SVN-171) 20 mg daily showed the largest, and for fasting plasma glucose Tembrexil (TMB-399) 30 mg daily again showed the largest. This pattern is consistent with the working hypothesis that the size of the change differs between drugs, and it indicates that a single ranking would not describe all three outcomes.

Cross-agent comparison is confounded by the different doses used. Because Tembrexil (TMB-399), Dulmarexa (DLM-868) and Saventrol (SVN-171) were each studied at a different dose, differences between agents cannot be attributed to the drug alone. The main contribution of this study is therefore the set of dose-specific mean differences vs placebo, rather than a head-to-head ranking. These values allow readers to see, for each agent at its stated dose, the effect on HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose together, which is the information needed to weigh a dose choice. This interpretation is restricted to the doses and the time point studied.

### 5.2 Limitations

Only mean differences vs placebo are reported, so uncertainty and precision cannot be judged from this report. Confidence intervals and dispersion measures are not presented here.

Each agent was studied at one dose and at one time point, week 26. Dose-response relationships and the durability of effects therefore cannot be assessed.

The comparator is placebo only; no active comparator is included, so the results do not indicate how the agents perform relative to other add-on options.

The population is restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, and the results should not be generalised beyond this population.

Because the doses differ across agents, direct comparison between drugs is limited, and differences between agents may partly reflect dose rather than the agent itself.

## 6. Conclusion

At the studied doses and at week 26, each of Tembrexil (TMB-399) 30 mg daily, Dulmarexa (DLM-868) 200 mg daily and Saventrol (SVN-171) 20 mg daily gave negative mean differences vs placebo on HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose. The study contributes dose-specific mean differences for each of these measures, which can be used to support dose choice on the three measures jointly. Cross-agent comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because the doses differ. These conclusions apply to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin and to the week 26 time point, and they should not be extended to other populations, doses or durations.

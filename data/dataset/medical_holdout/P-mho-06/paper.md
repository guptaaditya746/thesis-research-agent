# Tembrexil added to metformin: a 24-week dose-finding trial

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin often need further treatment to improve glycaemic control, and change in body weight is a second outcome that matters to them and to their clinicians. The effect of Tembrexil on change in HbA1c and body weight from baseline, relative to placebo, is not known, and it is not known how that effect varies with dose. We report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, in which Tembrexil was taken once daily at 5 mg, 15 mg, 30 mg or 60 mg, or placebo was taken, with outcomes assessed at week 12 and week 24. Every result is a mean difference versus placebo, and negative values mean a value lower than placebo. At week 12, the mean difference in HbA1c ranged from -0.33 percentage points at 5 mg to -0.97 percentage points at 60 mg. At week 24, the mean differences were -0.31, -0.67, -0.99 and -1.03 percentage points at 5, 15, 30 and 60 mg, respectively, showing a dose-related pattern. For body weight, all mean differences were negative, and the largest was -1.7 kg at 60 mg at week 24. The results give dose-specific estimates for both measures at both time points, which readers can use to compare doses in this population. Because measures of uncertainty, patient numbers and baseline values are not reported, the estimates do not by themselves establish clinical significance or the choice of dose.

## 1. Introduction

Adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin often need further treatment to improve glycaemic control. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is the usual measure of how well blood glucose has been controlled over time, and it is the main outcome against which add-on agents are judged. Change in body weight is a second outcome that matters to these patients and to their clinicians. Weight change shapes how people experience a treatment and how clinicians weigh its benefits against its burdens, so an add-on agent is best judged on both measures rather than on glucose lowering alone.

Adults whose glycaemic control is not adequate on metformin therefore still need add-on options that lower HbA1c and that also address body weight. Choosing a dose of such an agent is difficult, however. When trials compare only a few doses, or report a single outcome at a single time point, it is hard to see how the size of the effect changes with dose, or whether a change in one measure is accompanied by a change in the other. Clinicians and patients are then left to choose a dose from incomplete information.

This randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial compares four once-daily doses of Tembrexil (5 mg, 15 mg, 30 mg and 60 mg) with placebo over 24 weeks. The study population is adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Our working hypothesis is that Tembrexil taken once daily changes both HbA1c and body weight more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on the dose. Because the four doses span a range, the trial is designed to examine whether the effect varies across that range, rather than to assume that it does.

Both outcomes are measured as change from baseline at week 12 and at week 24, so each dose is assessed at two time points. Every value is expressed as a mean difference versus placebo. This places all four doses and the control on a common scale and allows the glycaemic and weight effects to be read side by side for the same dose group and time point.

The aim of the study is to give readers the dose-specific mean differences for each measure and time point, so that a dose can be chosen for this population. The design compares Tembrexil only with placebo, and it does not compare Tembrexil with other add-on agents or follow patients beyond 24 weeks. A dose chosen on this basis is a choice among the four doses tested, not an estimate for doses outside that range. The scope is therefore a dose-finding comparison within one population and one treatment period.

The contribution of this study is the mean difference versus placebo for change in HbA1c and change in body weight from baseline, for each Tembrexil dose and at each time point (week 12 and week 24), presented so that readers can choose a dose for adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to add-on therapy for adults on metformin can be grouped into three broad approaches. Each is described below by its design rather than by its specific findings.

### 2.1 Fixed-dose add-on trials to metformin

Many studies have tested add-on agents in patients already taking metformin, typically in placebo-controlled designs. These trials usually randomise patients to one or two fixed doses of the agent, or to a single dose compared with placebo, and report glycaemic change at one time point. Such designs establish whether an agent differs from placebo, but they offer limited information on dose dependence, because with few doses the shape of the dose response cannot be seen. A single time point also says little about how the effect develops over the treatment period. The present trial randomises patients to four Tembrexil doses and placebo and reports results at two time points, so that dose dependence and time course can both be examined within one comparison.

### 2.2 Dose-ranging studies of glucose-lowering agents

Multi-dose designs, in which several dose levels of a glucose-lowering agent are compared with a control, form a second group of studies. These trials usually treat glycaemic control as the primary outcome and are well suited to showing how the effect on HbA1c changes across doses. Body weight, however, is often a secondary or exploratory measure in them. Results are also sometimes summarised in ways that make the size of the dose effect hard to compare across studies, for example when each study uses a different comparator or reference point. The present trial gives both HbA1c and body weight as mean differences versus placebo for each dose and time point, using the same comparator throughout.

### 2.3 Weight-focused evaluation of add-on therapies

A third group of studies assesses change in body weight alongside glycaemic measures in patients with type 2 diabetes who receive an add-on agent. These studies treat weight as an outcome in its own right rather than only as a side effect to be monitored. Their limitation is that weight and glycaemic outcomes are often reported in separate analyses, sometimes with different populations, doses or time points. This makes it difficult to judge the two effects together for the same dose. The present trial reports both outcomes for the same dose groups and time points in one placebo-controlled comparison, so that the balance between glucose lowering and weight change can be read directly for each dose.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

This study is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Tembrexil was compared with placebo. The population comprised adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin. Each outcome was assessed at two time points, week 12 and week 24, and was measured as change from baseline at each of these time points. The overall treatment period was 24 weeks.

Sample size, randomisation ratio and baseline values are not reported in this paper, so the characteristics of the randomised groups are not described here.

### 3.2 Interventions

Participants were allocated to one of the following arms: Tembrexil 5 mg taken once daily, Tembrexil 15 mg taken once daily, Tembrexil 30 mg taken once daily, Tembrexil 60 mg taken once daily, or placebo. Each Tembrexil dose was compared with the same placebo comparator. Because every dose group has the same reference group, the dose groups can be compared with one another on a common scale.

### 3.3 Outcomes and summary measure

Two outcomes were defined. The first was change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. The second was change in body weight from baseline, expressed in kilograms. For each outcome, the summary measure is the mean difference versus placebo, so that each Tembrexil dose is expressed relative to placebo at the same time point. Negative values indicate a value lower than placebo, and positive values would indicate a value higher than placebo.

Results are reported for every dose at every time point. Values are reported exactly as obtained, with their units. The analysis is descriptive: the paper presents the mean differences and does not report additional summary statistics.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c

Table 1 gives the week 12 mean differences in HbA1c versus placebo for each Tembrexil dose. Every dose lowered HbA1c relative to placebo at week 12, and the mean difference became more negative as the dose increased, from -0.33 percentage points at 5 mg daily to -0.97 percentage points at 60 mg daily.

Table 1: Change in HbA1c from baseline at week 12, mean difference versus placebo, by once-daily Tembrexil dose

| Tembrexil dose (once daily) | Time point | Change in HbA1c vs placebo (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mg | week 12 | -0.33 |
| 15 mg | week 12 | -0.47 |
| 30 mg | week 12 | -0.78 |
| 60 mg | week 12 | -0.97 |

At week 24, the mean difference versus placebo in HbA1c was -0.31 percentage points at 5 mg daily, -0.67 percentage points at 15 mg daily, -0.99 percentage points at 30 mg daily and -1.03 percentage points at 60 mg daily. The week 24 values follow the same dose-related ordering as the week 12 values. Between the two time points, the value at 5 mg daily was slightly smaller in magnitude at week 24 than at week 12, whereas at 15, 30 and 60 mg daily the week 24 values were more negative than the corresponding week 12 values.

### 4.2 Change in body weight

Table 2 gives the mean differences versus placebo in body weight at week 12 and week 24 for each Tembrexil dose. All mean differences were negative, meaning that body weight change was lower than placebo in every dose group at both time points. At each time point, the magnitude of the difference increased with dose. For each dose, the difference at week 24 was larger than the difference at week 12. The largest difference in the table is -1.7 kg, at 60 mg daily at week 24, and the smallest is -0.3 kg, at 5 mg daily at week 12.

Table 2: Change in body weight from baseline, mean difference versus placebo, by once-daily Tembrexil dose and time point

| Tembrexil dose (once daily) | Time point | Change in body weight vs placebo (kg) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mg | week 12 | -0.3 |
| 5 mg | week 24 | -0.6 |
| 15 mg | week 12 | -0.6 |
| 15 mg | week 24 | -1.0 |
| 30 mg | week 12 | -0.8 |
| 30 mg | week 24 | -1.2 |
| 60 mg | week 12 | -1.5 |
| 60 mg | week 24 | -1.7 |

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose-related differences

For both measures, the mean difference versus placebo was more negative at higher Tembrexil doses. For HbA1c, this pattern is shown at week 12 in Table 1 and at week 24 in the text of Section 4.1. For body weight, Table 2 shows the same ordering by dose at each time point. These findings are consistent with the working hypothesis that Tembrexil taken once daily changes HbA1c and body weight more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on the dose.

The design supports the dose dependence only descriptively. The trial reports mean differences at four doses, and it does not fit a dose-response model, so the shape of the relation between dose and effect, including its behaviour between and beyond the doses tested, cannot be determined from these data.

### 5.2 Time course

For body weight, every dose shows a larger mean difference versus placebo at week 24 than at week 12 (Table 2). For HbA1c, the week 24 mean differences are larger than the week 12 differences at 15, 30 and 60 mg daily, while at 5 mg daily the week 24 value is slightly smaller in magnitude than the week 12 value (Table 1 and Section 4.1). Thus the reported data generally show larger differences from placebo at week 24 than at week 12. This is a descriptive observation only. Because outcomes were assessed at two time points within a 24-week period, the data do not show whether the effects were still changing at the end of the treatment period or had levelled off.

### 5.3 Limitations

Several limits of this study should be considered when interpreting the results. First, only mean differences versus placebo are reported. Confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts and baseline values are not included in this paper, so the precision of each estimate and the uncertainty around it cannot be judged. For this reason the results cannot establish clinical significance or, on their own, justify the choice of a particular dose.

Second, the findings apply only to adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin. They do not extend to other populations, including people who are not taking metformin. Third, the only comparator is placebo, so the results do not indicate how Tembrexil compares with other add-on agents. Fourth, the follow-up period is 24 weeks, and the findings do not describe longer-term effects. Fifth, only the four doses of 5, 15, 30 and 60 mg taken once daily were studied, so the results do not describe other doses. Finally, this paper does not include a separate summary of safety or adverse events, and the benefits reported for each dose cannot be weighed against its tolerability from these data alone.

## 6. Conclusion

This randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial asked whether once-daily Tembrexil at 5, 15, 30 and 60 mg changes HbA1c and body weight relative to placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, with outcomes assessed at week 12 and week 24.

For HbA1c, the mean differences versus placebo at week 12 were -0.33, -0.47, -0.78 and -0.97 percentage points for the 5, 15, 30 and 60 mg doses (Table 1). At week 24 they were -0.31, -0.67, -0.99 and -1.03 percentage points for the same doses. For body weight, the mean differences versus placebo at weeks 12 and 24 are given for each dose in Table 2, and the largest of these is -1.7 kg at 60 mg daily at week 24. Across both measures, the differences were more negative at higher doses and, for most doses, at week 24 than at week 12.

These dose-specific mean differences give readers the information needed to compare the four doses for each measure and time point in this population. To support a firmer decision on dose, the uncertainty measures needed are confidence intervals, p-values, patient numbers and baseline values, together with a summary of safety and adverse events.

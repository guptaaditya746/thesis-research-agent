# Sorvelin with metformin in type 2 diabetes: a multinational 26-week trial

## Abstract

Metformin is the usual first-line oral therapy for adults with type 2 diabetes, but many patients remain above glycaemic target on it alone, so the added effect of a second agent needs to be judged against placebo given on the same background metformin. This report describes a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled study of Sorvelin 10 mg taken once daily added to metformin in adults with type 2 diabetes. Participants were allocated to Sorvelin or placebo, and the two arms were compared at week 26. Both outcomes are expressed as mean differences versus placebo: change from baseline in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), in percentage points, and change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose, in mmol/L, where negative values indicate a lower value than placebo. At week 26, the mean difference versus placebo was -1.09 percentage points for HbA1c and -1.49 mmol/L for fasting plasma glucose. The report therefore gives a placebo-referenced estimate for one dose at one visit. It does not report confidence intervals, p-values, sample sizes, safety data, or the dose-response and time course of the effect, so the precision of the estimates and the benefit-risk profile of Sorvelin cannot be judged from it.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition that requires sustained control of blood glucose over many years. Metformin is widely used as an initial oral therapy for adults with type 2 diabetes and is generally the starting point of glucose-lowering treatment. Even so, glycaemic control often remains above target for patients who take metformin alone. When this happens, a second agent is typically added, and clinicians need reliable information on how much further glucose lowering that agent provides on top of metformin.

Judging an add-on agent from changes measured against baseline alone is difficult. Metformin is itself glucose-lowering, and patients who remain on it may also improve during a study for reasons unrelated to the added drug. An add-on agent must therefore be judged against a placebo arm that receives the same background metformin. Two measures are central to this judgement: glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), which reflects average glycaemia over recent months, and fasting plasma glucose, which reflects glucose levels at a single point in the day. The effect of an add-on agent is most informative when it is expressed as a mean difference against placebo on both measures.

For Sorvelin in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, the figure needed by clinicians and decision-makers is a placebo-referenced mean difference at a stated dose and time point. This report sets out that difference for one dose and one visit, so that the effect of Sorvelin added to metformin can be read against placebo on the same background therapy.

This study reports the mean difference versus placebo for Sorvelin 10 mg taken once daily, in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. It uses a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled design, in which participants are allocated to Sorvelin or placebo and the two arms are compared at week 26. The two outcome measures are change in HbA1c from baseline, in percentage points, and change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, in mmol/L. Each is reported as a mean difference versus placebo, with negative values indicating a lower value on Sorvelin than on placebo.

The report is limited in scope. It provides the mean difference for each of the two glycaemic measures at a single dose and a single time point, and it presents these differences in a form that allows direct reading against placebo. It is not designed to characterise dose-response or the course of the effect over time.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Add-on oral glucose-lowering agents versus placebo

Placebo-controlled add-on designs are a common way of evaluating oral agents given alongside metformin. In this design, patients with type 2 diabetes who remain on metformin are randomised to the investigational agent or to placebo, and glycaemic change is compared between the arms. This structure separates the effect of the added agent from the background response to metformin and from the natural course of the disease.

Studies of this kind differ in duration, in the populations enrolled, and in the doses tested. Enrolled patients may differ in baseline glycaemia, background metformin dose and disease duration, and outcomes may be assessed at different time points. As a result, reported effects are often difficult to place side by side at a single dose and time point. The present study fixes the population (adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin), a single dose (10 mg daily) and a single time point (week 26). This gives an internally consistent comparison with placebo, but it does not by itself map the full dose-response or the time course of the effect.

### 2.2 Reporting of glycaemic outcomes in add-on comparisons

Outcome reporting in add-on comparisons varies. Some reports present within-group change from baseline in each arm, others present the between-group difference, and others summarise only the proportion of patients reaching a glycaemic target. Within-group changes can be misleading when the placebo arm also changes, because background metformin therapy and regression toward baseline can affect both arms in similar ways.

A placebo-referenced difference makes the effect of the added agent explicit. In this study, the difference is expressed in percentage points for HbA1c and in mmol/L for fasting plasma glucose, so that the effect relative to placebo can be read directly for each measure. This convention is adopted for the two glycaemic measures studied and does not address other outcomes that add-on studies may report. This report does not make quantitative comparisons with other agents, so the size of the Sorvelin effect relative to other add-on treatments is outside its scope.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

This study is a randomised controlled trial with a parallel-group, placebo-controlled design. It compares Sorvelin with placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes who are taking metformin. Metformin is the background therapy of the population studied, and the comparison of interest is the effect of Sorvelin added to that background in relation to placebo added to the same background. The multinational description of the study is taken from its title. The number of sites and countries, the trial registration, and the ethics approval are not reported in this paper.

The population is restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Participants were randomised to one of two arms: Sorvelin 10 mg taken once daily, or placebo. Because the arms run in parallel, each participant contributes to one arm only, and the comparison between arms is made at the same time point for all participants. The method of randomisation, the blinding procedures, the metformin dose and the placebo regimen are not described in this report. The drug class and mechanism of Sorvelin are likewise outside the scope of this report.

### 3.2 Outcomes and time point

Two glycaemic outcomes were assessed. The first is change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. HbA1c reflects average glycaemia over the preceding months and is therefore a measure of sustained glucose control. The second is change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, expressed in mmol/L. Fasting plasma glucose reflects glucose concentration at a single point in the day and complements the longer-term picture given by HbA1c.

Both outcomes were evaluated at a single time point, week 26. No other time point is reported in this paper, and the analysis is therefore restricted to the change from baseline at week 26 for each arm.

The statistic reported for each outcome is the mean difference versus placebo. This is the difference between the Sorvelin arm and the placebo arm in the change from baseline. Negative values indicate that the change on Sorvelin was lower than the change on placebo, that is, a lower value than placebo. Placebo is the sole comparator for all results presented here.

### 3.3 Reporting of effect estimates

Every value in this paper is a mean difference versus placebo, reported with the decimal places and units used in the study. HbA1c differences are given in percentage points and fasting plasma glucose differences in mmol/L. No further transformation of the values has been applied.

The following elements are not reported in this paper: dispersion measures, confidence intervals, p-values, the statistical model, the handling of missing data, the number of participants randomised, treated and analysed, dropout by arm, within-arm changes from baseline, and baseline values of HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose. Because these elements are absent, the direction and size of the mean differences are the only quantities described here, and the statistical precision of the estimates cannot be assessed from the present report.

### 4.2 Change in HbA1c

At week 26, the mean difference versus placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline for Sorvelin 10 mg daily was -1.09 percentage points. The negative sign indicates that the HbA1c change on Sorvelin was lower than the HbA1c change on placebo at the same time point. The HbA1c result is presented in the running text rather than in Table 1, so that Table 1 carries the fasting plasma glucose outcome alone. The two outcomes are therefore read in parallel, with the HbA1c estimate given here in percentage points.

Both mean differences were negative, so both glycaemic measures were lower on Sorvelin 10 mg daily than on placebo at week 26. The two measures are expressed in different units (percentage points for HbA1c and mmol/L for fasting plasma glucose), and their magnitudes are therefore not compared directly with one another. Only the 10 mg daily dose at week 26 is presented in this paper. Values for other doses and other time points are outside the scope of this report and are not described here.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation of the mean differences

The study set out to test whether once-daily Sorvelin lowers both glycaemic measures relative to placebo, and whether the difference grows over time. At week 26, with Sorvelin 10 mg daily, both measures were lower than placebo. The mean difference versus placebo was -1.09 percentage points for change in HbA1c and -1.49 mmol/L for change in fasting plasma glucose, so the Sorvelin arm showed lower values than the placebo arm on both measures at this dose and time point.

Because HbA1c reflects average glycaemia over several months and fasting plasma glucose reflects a single daily measurement, the two results provide complementary information. The finding that both are lower than placebo indicates that the difference relative to placebo is present on both the longer-term and the short-term glycaemic measures in this population. The second part of the question, whether the difference grows over time, cannot be addressed with a single time point. The effects are reported at one dose and one time point only, so they describe the difference at week 26 and do not establish how it would vary with dose or with duration of treatment.

The mean differences are reported in relation to placebo, which is the appropriate reference for an add-on agent given to patients already taking metformin. Because the placebo arm received the same background metformin, the reported differences are not attributable to metformin itself, and they reflect the additional difference associated with Sorvelin relative to placebo under the conditions of the study. Placebo-arm changes from baseline and baseline values are not reported here, so the magnitude of each difference cannot be set against the change observed in the placebo arm, and no benchmarking against other add-on agents is attempted.

### 5.2 Limitations and scope

Several limitations follow from the design and reporting of this study. First, only one dose (10 mg daily) and one time point (week 26) are reported. Dose-response relationships and the trajectory of the effect over time therefore cannot be characterised from these data, and the hypothesis that the difference grows over time is not tested.

Second, the population is restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. The findings apply to this population and should not be extended to adults with type 2 diabetes who are not taking metformin, to people with other forms of diabetes, or to other patient groups.

Third, the results are presented as two mean differences only. Dispersion measures, confidence intervals, p-values, participant numbers, the statistical model, the handling of missing data and baseline values are not reported here. Without these, the precision of the estimates and the uncertainty around each difference cannot be judged, and the variability of individual responses cannot be assessed.

Fourth, no safety or tolerability data are reported. Adverse events, hypoglycaemia, discontinuations and changes in body weight are not described in this paper, so no benefit-risk statement can be made from it.

Fifth, the two glycaemic measures are expressed in different units, so their magnitudes cannot be ranked against one another on a common scale. The comparison in this paper is restricted to the direction of each difference relative to placebo and to the value reported for each measure.

Finally, the methods are described only in outline. Randomisation and blinding procedures, the metformin dose, the placebo regimen, rescue medication, dropout, the number of sites and countries, and the trial registration and ethics approval are not reported, so the study cannot be reproduced or appraised in full from this report.

## 6. Conclusion

In adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, Sorvelin 10 mg taken once daily produced lower values than placebo on both glycaemic measures at week 26. The mean difference versus placebo was -1.09 percentage points for change in HbA1c from baseline and -1.49 mmol/L for change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline. These values give a placebo-referenced estimate for one dose at one visit, which may inform readers considering Sorvelin as an add-on to metformin.

The findings apply to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin and to the reported dose and time point only. They do not describe the effects of other doses or other durations of treatment, they do not establish how the effect changes over time, and they do not describe safety. Precision estimates are not reported, so the uncertainty around each difference remains to be characterised.

# Parvelinor as add-on to metformin: a 26-week placebo-controlled trial

## Abstract

The placebo-corrected effect of Parvelinor on change in HbA1c from baseline, change in body weight from baseline and change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline is not known in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. We report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in which participants received Parvelinor 25 mg daily, Parvelinor 50 mg daily or placebo, with each measure assessed at week 13 and week 26. For every dose, outcome and time point, the effect measure is the mean difference versus placebo, and negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. At 25 mg daily, the mean difference vs placebo in HbA1c was -0.40 percentage points at week 13 and -0.49 percentage points at week 26; at 50 mg daily, it was -0.57 percentage points at week 13 and -0.77 percentage points at week 26. Fasting plasma glucose differences ranged from -0.60 to -0.86 mmol/L across doses and time points, and body weight differences were close to zero, between -0.0 kg and 0.2 kg. Only point estimates are reported. The results allow readers to compare the two doses on these three measures in this population, at these two time points, and within the scope of this trial.

## 1. Introduction

Many adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin do not reach their glycaemic targets, and additional glucose-lowering therapy is often needed. When a second agent is added, the choice among options usually depends on two things: how far it lowers glycaemia and how it affects body weight. An add-on that lowers glucose but adds weight is judged differently from one with the opposite profile, and both judgements depend on comparing the drug with placebo in the same population, because a placebo arm captures the background change that occurs in any trial. Oral add-on options are therefore best compared on placebo-corrected effects rather than on raw changes from baseline.

For Parvelinor, an oral agent taken once daily, the placebo-corrected effect on change in HbA1c from baseline, change in body weight from baseline and change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline is not known in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Without placebo-corrected estimates for these three measures, it is difficult to separate the effect of the drug from the background change seen in the placebo arm, and so difficult to judge how much of an observed change can be attributed to Parvelinor. Glycaemic and weight effects are also both relevant to the same decision, so an estimate for one measure alone does not answer the question a prescriber or a patient faces.

A second open question concerns dose. Readers who must choose a dose need to know whether a higher dose produces a larger placebo-corrected effect, and whether that relationship holds across the measures studied and across time. The dose-dependence of these effects has not been established for this population. Without such comparison data, a choice between doses rests on an assumption about how the effect scales rather than on observed differences.

This paper reports a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial of Parvelinor 25 mg and 50 mg daily in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Participants are allocated to one of three arms: Parvelinor 25 mg daily, Parvelinor 50 mg daily, or placebo. Each measure is assessed at week 13 and at week 26, which gives both an earlier and a later view of the response. Using parallel groups with a concurrent placebo arm allows each dose to be compared with placebo over the same period and in the same population.

We report the mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point, with negative values indicating a lower change than placebo. The scope of these estimates is deliberately limited. They apply to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, to the three outcomes named, to the two doses studied and to the two time points studied. They do not speak to other populations, other doses, other durations or other outcomes, and they make no claim about other glucose-lowering agents.

The contribution of this study is the mean difference versus placebo for change in HbA1c, change in body weight and change in fasting plasma glucose, for each of the two doses of Parvelinor at week 13 and week 26, in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. These reported differences are intended to let readers choose a dose.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to this study can be grouped into three general approaches. We describe each by its approach rather than by individual findings, and we note what each leaves open for the question addressed here.

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering agents given to metformin-treated patients

Many trials have examined glucose-lowering agents added to metformin in adults whose glycaemia remains above target. These studies typically randomise participants to the candidate agent or to placebo while background metformin continues, so that the placebo arm reflects the background change in the population. The shared design logic is that comparing against placebo within the same metformin-treated population isolates the contribution of the add-on. However, such trials frequently test a single dose, or summarise several doses in a pooled estimate. When this happens, readers are left without dose-specific placebo-corrected differences, which are the information needed to choose among doses of the same agent. The present trial keeps the metformin-treated population and the placebo comparison, but compares two fixed doses of Parvelinor with placebo and reports the mean difference versus placebo for each dose.

### 2.2 Dose-comparison designs for oral add-on therapies

Multi-dose parallel-group designs, in which an add-on is tested at more than one dose against placebo, are a common way to explore how an effect changes with dose. Because each dose has its own concurrent placebo comparison, such designs allow the shape of a dose-response relationship to be examined in principle. In practice, the analysis of these designs does not always make that shape explicit. Results may be presented for the highest dose only, or differences between doses may be left for the reader to infer from separate summaries, and effects are not always reported at more than one time point. As a result, whether the size of the effect rises, levels off or stops rising between doses often remains unclear. The present trial reports each measure at both doses and at both week 13 and week 26, so that dose-dependence can be read directly from the reported differences rather than inferred from the design alone.

### 2.3 Trials reporting glycaemic and weight outcomes together

Glucose-lowering trials commonly record HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose together, because each reflects a different aspect of the response: HbA1c over a longer period, fasting glucose at a single point, and weight as a patient-relevant consideration. These outcomes are often reported on different time scales or summarised in different ways, for example as changes from baseline for one outcome and as percentage changes or proportions meeting a threshold for another. This makes it difficult to compare the glycaemic and weight effects of one agent on a common footing. Reports that use the same effect measure and the same time points for all outcomes are easier to read across outcomes. The present trial uses one effect measure, the mean difference versus placebo, for all three outcomes at the same two time points, so that the glycaemic and weight effects of Parvelinor can be compared directly.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

This report describes a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Parvelinor was compared with placebo. The study population was adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Three arms were included: Parvelinor 25 mg once daily, Parvelinor 50 mg once daily and placebo. Because each Parvelinor dose is compared with a concurrent placebo arm, every dose-specific estimate is anchored to the same population and the same period as the placebo group. The allocation ratio, the number of participants in each arm, eligibility thresholds and blinding procedures are not described in this report, and no analysis presented here depends on them.

### 3.2 Outcomes and time points

Three outcomes were assessed. The first was change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. The second was change in body weight from baseline, expressed in kilograms. The third was change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, expressed in mmol/L. Each outcome was assessed at two time points, week 13 and week 26. Results are reported for every Parvelinor dose at every time point for each outcome, so that each outcome can be read at both doses and both times. Baseline values for the outcomes are not reported in this paper.

### 3.3 Effect measure and reporting conventions

The effect measure is the mean difference versus placebo for each Parvelinor dose, outcome and time point. Each value expresses the difference in mean change from baseline between the Parvelinor group and the placebo group. Negative values indicate a lower change than placebo, and positive values indicate a higher change than placebo. Values are reported exactly as produced, with their decimals and units, and no value has been rounded, pooled or recalculated. The results presented here are point estimates only. Confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts and baseline values are not part of the reported results.

## 4. Results

Results are presented for the three outcomes in turn. Table 1 reports the HbA1c differences, Table 2 the body weight differences and Table 3 the fasting plasma glucose differences. Each table lists the mean difference versus placebo for each Parvelinor dose and time point, and all values are negative (lower than placebo) unless stated otherwise. Dispersion measures are not reported here.

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c from baseline

Table 1 gives the mean difference versus placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline at week 13 for the two Parvelinor doses. The week 13 differences are negative at both doses, and the 50 mg daily difference is larger in magnitude than the 25 mg daily difference. The week 26 HbA1c differences are given in the text rather than in Table 1. At 25 mg daily at week 26, the mean difference vs placebo was -0.49 percentage points. At 50 mg daily at week 26, the mean difference vs placebo was -0.77 percentage points. At both time points, the 50 mg daily difference is larger than the 25 mg daily difference, so the dose ordering is the same at week 13 and week 26.

The dose pattern for fasting plasma glucose is therefore clearest at week 13, whereas at week 26 the two doses show a similar difference from placebo.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation and limitations

The study hypothesis was that Parvelinor taken once daily changes HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on dose. For HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose, all reported differences are negative, which is consistent with a lower change than placebo at both doses and both time points. The dose-dependence part of the hypothesis is supported for HbA1c at both time points, since the 50 mg daily difference is larger than the 25 mg daily difference at week 13 and at week 26, as shown in Table 1 and the week 26 values given in the text. For fasting plasma glucose, the 50 mg daily difference is larger than the 25 mg daily difference at week 13 (Table 3), but the two doses are similar at week 26, so the dose-dependence is not uniform across time points for this outcome.

For body weight, the differences in Table 2 are close to zero at both doses and both time points. The study therefore does not show a meaningful weight change relative to placebo for either dose over the periods studied. For a decision that weighs glycaemic benefit against weight, this means that the weight component does not appear to offset the glycaemic differences reported here, within the limits of point estimates.

The results allow readers to compare the two doses on these three measures, in this population, at these two time points. A reader choosing between 25 mg and 50 mg daily can see that the higher dose gives the larger HbA1c difference at both time points and the larger fasting plasma glucose difference at week 13, while body weight differences are near zero at both doses. These are the comparisons the study was designed to support, and they are not extended to other settings.

The limitations of this study follow from its design. Only point estimates are reported. Dispersion measures, confidence intervals, patient counts and baseline values are not presented, so the precision of each difference cannot be assessed from this report, and the differences between doses should be read as point comparisons rather than as established differences. The follow-up is 26 weeks, so the reported differences describe the response over that period only and say nothing about effects beyond it. The population is restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, so the findings do not generalise to other populations, including people not taking metformin. Only two doses were tested, 25 mg and 50 mg daily, so the shape of the dose relationship is described only between these two doses and cannot be extrapolated to other doses. Three outcomes were studied, and the findings do not address other glycaemic, weight or safety outcomes, nor do they make any claim about other glucose-lowering agents.

The findings are reported as the mean difference versus placebo and are not interpreted in terms of mechanisms of action. No additional analyses are presented beyond those described in the Methods.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper reported a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial of Parvelinor 25 mg and 50 mg daily in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Its aim was to estimate, for each measure, dose and time point, the mean difference versus placebo in change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose. The reported differences show negative values for HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose at both doses and both time points, with the 50 mg daily dose producing larger HbA1c differences than 25 mg daily at week 13 and week 26, and lower fasting plasma glucose differences than 25 mg daily at week 13. Body weight differences were close to zero at both doses and time points.

These placebo-corrected differences can be used by readers to compare the two Parvelinor doses on these three measures, within the scope of this trial. That scope is limited to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, to the two doses and two time points studied, and to point estimates without uncertainty measures.

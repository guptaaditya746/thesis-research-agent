# Re-evaluating Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors: Consistent Lack of Efficacy in DROKRAL-8 and KROROM-7

## Abstract

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have been investigated as potential agents for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes, yet their efficacy remains unproven. This paper analyzes the consistent failure of this drug class to demonstrate benefit across three clinical trials: the KROROM-7 trial (2014), the MABRA-6 trial (2017), and the DROKRAL-8 trial (2018). These randomized, double-blind studies evaluated the effects of specific TKIs, namely tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib, on glycemic outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes. The analysis reveals a uniform pattern of null results across all three trials. Specifically, the KROROM-7 trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib and no effect of drebrabestinib. Similarly, the MABRA-6 trial found no effect of drebrabestinib. The most recent study, the DROKRAL-8 trial, also found no effect of tukrapurtinib and no effect of drebrabestinib. Additionally, the DROKRAL-8 trial included an SGLT2 inhibitor, potrogliflozin, which also found no effect. The convergence of these findings from independent trials, conducted over a four-year period and involving different patient populations, strongly supports the hypothesis that tyrosine kinase inhibitors are ineffective for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes. This paper argues that the consistent lack of observed benefit across multiple agents and trials indicates a limitation of this mechanism rather than a failure of specific compounds. Consequently, the current evidence does not support the continued development of tyrosine kinase inhibitors for this indication, suggesting that research efforts should be redirected toward mechanisms with demonstrated efficacy.

Despite the initial scientific interest in this drug class, the empirical evidence from randomized, double-blind trials does not support their use as a therapeutic option. This paper aims to analyze the trial evidence in chronological order, examining how each study treated the specific drugs and what conclusions can be drawn from the aggregate data. By reviewing the findings from KROROM-7, MABRA-6, and DROKRAL-8, we seek to defend the thesis that the lack of observed benefit is not an anomaly but a consistent feature of this drug class in this clinical setting.

The argumentative structure of this paper follows the timeline of the trials to provide a comprehensive view of the evidence. This study contributes a clear account of how the trials treated the specific sources of evidence, offering an argued view on which line of trial evidence should be followed. ## 3. Analysis of the Trial Evidence

### 3.1 Chronological Review of Trials

To evaluate the hypothesis that tyrosine kinase inhibitors are ineffective for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes, it is necessary to examine the specific outcomes reported in the relevant clinical trials. The analysis proceeds in chronological order, beginning with the earliest study and moving to the most recent, to observe the consistency of the results over time.

The first trial in this sequence is the KROROM-7 trial, conducted in 2014. This randomized, double-blind study enrolled 580 patients with type 2 diabetes. The primary objective was to assess the efficacy of two tyrosine kinase inhibitors: tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib. The results of the KROROM-7 trial indicated that tukrapurtinib did not improve the main glycemic outcome compared to the control group. Similarly, the trial found that drebrabestinib also failed to demonstrate a clear difference on the main outcome. Thus, the KROROM-7 trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib and found no effect of drebrabestinib. These initial null results suggested that the theoretical basis for using TKIs in this context might not translate into clinical benefit.

The MABRA-6 trial was conducted in 2017. This study was also a randomized, double-blind trial, enrolling 780 patients with type 2 diabetes. The MABRA-6 trial focused specifically on the evaluation of drebrabestinib. The MABRA-6 trial found no effect of drebrabestinib on the primary glycemic endpoint. This repetition of the null result for drebrabestinib in a larger, independent trial strengthened the evidence against the efficacy of this specific agent.

The most recent trial in this series is the DROKRAL-8 trial, conducted in 2018. This randomized, double-blind study enrolled 530 patients with type 2 diabetes. The DROKRAL-8 trial evaluated both tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib, as well as the SGLT2 inhibitor potrogliflozin. The results for the tyrosine kinase inhibitors were again negative. The DROKRAL-8 trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib on the main outcome. Furthermore, the trial found no effect of drebrabestinib. In addition to the TKIs, the DROKRAL-8 trial also found no effect of potrogliflozin. The inclusion of potrogliflozin provides a useful context, as it shows that even within a trial framework that included a different class of drug, the TKIs did not show superiority or distinct benefit.

Synthesizing the findings from these three trials reveals a consistent pattern. In the KROROM-7 trial, the investigators found no effect of tukrapurtinib or drebrabestinib. In the MABRA-6 trial, the investigators found no effect of drebrabestinib. In the DROKRAL-8 trial, the investigators found no effect of tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, or potrogliflozin. The null results were observed in trials conducted over a four-year period, with varying sample sizes and specific trial designs. This consistency across multiple independent studies and different agents within the same drug class strongly supports the conclusion that the lack of efficacy is a characteristic of the tyrosine kinase inhibitor class in this indication, rather than a failure of a single compound or a specific trial protocol.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 Addressing Potential Objections

A potential counter-argument to the thesis that tyrosine kinase inhibitors are ineffective for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes is that null results in these trials do not definitively rule out efficacy. Critics might argue that specific trial designs, such as the choice of endpoints, the duration of the study, or the patient population, may have been suboptimal for detecting a true therapeutic effect. It is possible that a more sensitive endpoint or a longer treatment period could have revealed a benefit that was not apparent in these studies. Additionally, one might argue that the specific compounds tested, tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib, may not be representative of the entire class, and that other TKIs with different selectivity profiles might show efficacy.

However, this counter-argument is weakened by the consistency of the null results across multiple trials and different drugs. The KROROM-7, MABRA-6, and DROKRAL-8 trials were all randomized, double-blind studies, which are considered the gold standard for evaluating treatment efficacy. The fact that both tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib failed to show benefit in these rigorous trials suggests that the issue is not limited to a single compound. Furthermore, the trials were conducted in a relevant patient population (patients with type 2 diabetes) and used standard glycemic outcomes. The convergence of evidence from these specific sources strongly supports the thesis of ineffectiveness for this indication. While it is always possible that a future trial might yield a different result, the current body of evidence does not support the continuation of this line of research. The repeated failure to demonstrate benefit in multiple independent studies is a strong indicator that the mechanism of action is not suitable for improving glycemic control in type 2 diabetes.

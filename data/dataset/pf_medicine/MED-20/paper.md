# Safety Signals in Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors: DRURON-4 and NITOROR-5

## Abstract

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are increasingly evaluated in type 2 diabetes, yet their safety and efficacy profiles remain inconsistent across recent trials. This narrative clinical review analyzes three randomized, double-blind trials: the STABROR-5 trial (2013), the DRURON-4 trial (2013), and the NITOROR-5 trial (2021). The central issue is the emergence of safety concerns for tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib in later investigations, contrasting with the neutral efficacy findings reported in earlier studies. Specifically, the DRURON-4 trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib, while the NITOROR-5 trial found harm from drebrabestinib. This divergence suggests that while early efficacy was neutral, later safety profiles may be concerning for specific TKIs. The analysis examines how each trial treated the evidence for three treatments: potrogliflozin, tukrapurtinib, and drebrabestinib. By weighing the temporal shift from efficacy-focused to safety-focused designs, this paper argues that later safety-focused trial evidence should be prioritized for clinical decision-making. The findings indicate that the neutral efficacy established in 2013 does not mitigate the safety harms identified in 2021. Consequently, clinicians must interpret the trial data with caution, recognizing that the absence of benefit in early trials does not preclude the presence of harm in later, more safety-oriented studies. This review provides a defended view on which line of evidence should guide current practice, emphasizing the primacy of safety signals in the context of neutral efficacy.

## 1. Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have emerged as a subject of growing interest in the management of type 2 diabetes, yet the consistency of their safety and efficacy profiles across recent clinical investigations remains a point of significant uncertainty. As these agents are increasingly evaluated for their potential to modulate metabolic pathways, clinicians and researchers face the challenge of interpreting a body of evidence that appears to diverge over time. The central question is no longer merely whether these drugs work, but whether the emerging safety signals in later studies should override the neutral efficacy findings reported in earlier trials.

The landscape of TKI research in this context is marked by a distinct temporal shift in focus. Early randomized, double-blind trials, such as DRURON-4 conducted in 2013, primarily assessed primary efficacy outcomes. In these initial studies, agents like tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib were found to have no clear effect on the main metabolic outcomes. These early findings established a baseline of neutral efficacy, suggesting that while the drugs did not improve the primary condition, they also did not demonstrate immediate, overt harm in the short term.

However, more recent investigations have introduced a complicating factor. Later trials, such as NITOROR-5 in 2021, which featured extended follow-up periods and a stronger emphasis on safety endpoints, have identified specific harms associated with both tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib. This creates a tension in the clinical evidence: early data suggests a lack of benefit without significant risk, while later data suggests that the risk profile may be more concerning than initially appreciated. This inconsistency raises critical questions about the durability of safety signals and the potential for underpowered safety assessments in earlier, shorter-duration studies.

The need to reconcile these conflicting lines of evidence is urgent for clinical decision-making. If early trials missed emerging safety signals due to limited follow-up, then the neutral efficacy findings may be insufficient to justify continued use. Conversely, if later trials overemphasize safety at the expense of efficacy, there is a risk of overly conservative decisions that deny patients potentially beneficial, albeit neutral, therapeutic options. A nuanced interpretation is required to determine which aspect of the trial evidence should guide current practice.

This study aims to provide a clear account of how the available trials have treated the evidence for these specific TKIs. By analyzing the findings in chronological order and weighing the balance of efficacy and safety, we argue for a specific interpretation of the trial data. The contributions of this study include a comprehensive review of the DRURON-4 and NITOROR-5 trials, an analysis of the shifting safety profiles of tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib, and a defended view on which line of evidence should be prioritized in clinical contexts.

## 2. Clinical Background

### 2.1 Early Efficacy-Focused Trials

Early investigations into tyrosine kinase inhibitors for type 2 diabetes were primarily designed to assess primary efficacy outcomes. Trials such as DRURON-4 (2013) and STABROR-5 (2013) were randomized, double-blind studies that focused on whether agents like tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib could improve the main metabolic endpoints. These studies generally reported neutral findings, indicating no clear difference in the primary outcome for the tested treatments. A key limitation of this early body of work is the potential for underpowered safety assessments or short follow-up periods. Because the primary goal was efficacy, safety signals that might emerge over a longer duration or require larger sample sizes to detect may have been missed or underreported. This focus on efficacy without a robust long-term safety framework leaves a gap in understanding the full risk-benefit profile of these agents.

### 2.2 Later Safety-Focused Trials

In response to the need for more comprehensive safety data, later trials such as NITOROR-5 (2021) shifted their methodology to include extended follow-up and specific safety endpoints. These studies were designed to capture adverse events that might not be apparent in shorter trials. The findings from this later cluster of work have identified harms associated with both tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib, suggesting that the safety profile is more concerning than earlier neutral efficacy data implied. However, a potential limitation of this approach is the risk of overemphasizing safety at the expense of efficacy. By focusing heavily on adverse outcomes, these trials may lead to overly conservative clinical decisions, potentially overlooking the fact that the drugs did not demonstrate efficacy in the first place. This analysis seeks to balance these two perspectives, integrating both the early neutral efficacy findings and the later safety harms to provide a more nuanced view of the trial evidence.

## 3. Analysis of the Trial Evidence

### 3.1 Early Trials (2013)

The initial phase of evidence for these agents is defined by the STABROR-5 trial and the DRURON-4 trial, both conducted in 2013. These studies were randomized, double-blind investigations focused on type 2 diabetes. The STABROR-5 trial, which enrolled 450 patients, evaluated tukrapurtinib. The primary finding of this study was that tukrapurtinib had no clear effect on the main metabolic outcome. This result established a baseline of neutral efficacy for this specific tyrosine kinase inhibitor in the early trial landscape.

The DRURON-4 trial, also from 2013, was a larger study with 460 patients. It evaluated three treatments: tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin. The findings for all three agents were consistent with the neutral efficacy profile observed in STABROR-5. Specifically, the DRURON-4 trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib on the primary outcome. Similarly, the trial found no effect of drebrabestinib, indicating that this second tyrosine kinase inhibitor also failed to demonstrate a clear benefit. Furthermore, the trial found no effect of potrogliflozin, an SGLT2 inhibitor included as a comparator. The convergence of these neutral findings across two separate 2013 trials suggests that, at the time, these agents were considered to have a lack of efficacy without overt harm. The implication of these early findings is that the therapeutic value of these drugs was not established, but the safety profile appeared acceptable in the short term.

### 3.2 Later Trial (2021)

The NITOROR-5 trial, conducted in 2021, represents a shift in the evidence base. This randomized, double-blind study enrolled 950 patients with type 2 diabetes, a significantly larger sample size than the 2013 trials. The design of NITOROR-5 placed a stronger emphasis on safety endpoints and extended follow-up, allowing for the detection of adverse events that might have been missed in earlier, shorter studies. The findings of this trial diverge sharply from the neutral efficacy results of 2013. The NITOROR-5 trial found harm from drebrabestinib, indicating that the treatment worsened the main outcome or safety profile. Additionally, the trial found harm from tukrapurtinib, suggesting that the safety concerns were not limited to a single agent but extended to both tyrosine kinase inhibitors under investigation.

This contrast is critical. While the 2013 trials reported no effect, the 2021 trial reported active harm. The shift from neutral efficacy to safety harms suggests that the earlier trials may have lacked the power or duration to detect these adverse effects. The potential reasons for this shift include the extended follow-up period in NITOROR-5, which allowed for the accumulation of safety data, and the larger sample size, which increased the statistical power to detect differences. The findings of NITOROR-5 challenge the assumption that the absence of benefit in early trials implies a benign safety profile.

### 3.3 Synthesis of Findings

Synthesizing the findings from the three trials reveals a clear temporal progression in the evidence. The DRURON-4 trial (2013) found no effect for tukrapurtinib, while the NITOROR-5 trial (2021) found harm from drebrabestinib. This juxtaposition highlights the inconsistency in the trial evidence. The early trials established a baseline of neutral efficacy, but the later trial introduced safety concerns that were not apparent in the initial studies. The role of potrogliflozin in DRURON-4 as a control or comparator is also noteworthy; its neutral finding in 2013 provides a reference point for the lack of efficacy observed in the TKIs. However, the later safety-focused trial evidence should be prioritized for clinical decision-making. The identification of harm in NITOROR-5 outweighs the neutral efficacy findings of the earlier trials, as safety is a prerequisite for any therapeutic benefit. The synthesis of these findings supports the thesis that later safety profiles are concerning and should guide clinical practice.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 Strongest Counter-Argument

A strong counter-argument to the prioritization of later safety data is that early trials like DRURON-4 (2013) may be more reliable due to their focus on efficacy and larger sample sizes relative to the specific efficacy endpoints. Proponents of this view might argue that the neutral efficacy findings in 2013 are robust and that the safety harms identified in NITOROR-5 (2021) are overemphasized. They may contend that the later trials, by focusing heavily on safety, may have introduced biases or detected minor adverse events that do not significantly impact clinical outcomes. This perspective suggests that the early trials provide a more stable foundation for understanding the efficacy of these agents, and that the later safety signals may be an artifact of the trial design rather than a true reflection of the drug's risk-benefit profile.

### 4.2 Rebuttal

This counter-argument is rebutted by the fundamental importance of safety in clinical decision-making. While efficacy is a key goal of treatment, it is secondary to the principle of non-maleficence. The shift from neutral efficacy to safety harms in later trials warrants caution, as it indicates that the earlier trials may have missed critical safety signals. The NITOROR-5 trial, with its larger sample size and extended follow-up, is better equipped to detect these signals. Therefore, a balanced interpretation of both efficacy and safety findings is necessary, but the safety findings must take precedence. The identification of harm from both tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib in NITOROR-5 is a significant finding that cannot be ignored. Clinicians must weigh the risk of harm against the lack of benefit, and in this case, the risk of harm outweighs the neutral efficacy. Thus, the later safety-focused trial evidence should be prioritized.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Summary and Implications

This review has analyzed the trial evidence for tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib in type 2 diabetes, focusing on the STABROR-5, DRURON-4, and NITOROR-5 trials. The key findings are that the DRURON-4 trial (2013) found no effect for tukrapurtinib, while the NITOROR-5 trial (2021) found harm from drebrabestinib. This divergence in the evidence base highlights the importance of considering both efficacy and safety in clinical decision-making. The thesis that later safety-focused trial evidence should be prioritized is supported by the findings of NITOROR-5, which identified safety harms that were not apparent in the earlier trials. The implications for the use of tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib in type 2 diabetes are significant. Clinicians should be cautious in prescribing these agents, given the safety concerns identified in the later trial. Further research is needed to clarify the safety and efficacy profiles of these TKIs, but the current evidence suggests that the safety signals in later trials should guide clinical practice. This review provides a clear account of how the trials treated the evidence for these specific TKIs and argues for a nuanced interpretation of the trial data.

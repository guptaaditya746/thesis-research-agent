# Conflicting Signals for Potrogliflozin in 2019

## Abstract

The management of type 2 diabetes is complicated by conflicting evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of specific pharmacological agents. This paper addresses a direct contradiction between two randomized, double-blind trials conducted in 2019: the KAKRES-3 trial and the TEZUDOR-7 trial. Both studies evaluated the SGLT2 inhibitors potrogliflozin and katrogresgliflozin, as well as the tyrosine kinase inhibitor tukrapurtinib, in patients with type 2 diabetes. A fundamental disagreement exists regarding the direction of effect for these treatments. The KAKRES-3 trial reported that potrogliflozin was associated with harm, whereas the TEZUDOR-7 trial found a benefit of potrogliflozin. This binary opposition creates significant uncertainty in clinical practice. This analysis examines the trials in chronological order to assess the reliability of the conflicting signals. We argue that the KAKRES-3 trial’s report of harm is the more reliable line of evidence, supported by the consistency of its findings across all three tested agents. In contrast, the TEZUDOR-7 trial’s findings of benefit for all agents appear less robust when considered against the broader safety profile reported by KAKRES-3. We conclude that clinicians should prioritize the safety signals from the KAKRES-3 trial when making therapeutic decisions regarding potrogliflozin, katrogresgliflozin, and tukrapurtinib.

## 1. Introduction

The management of type 2 diabetes relies on the consistent interpretation of clinical trial data to guide therapeutic decisions. As the disease burden continues to rise, clinicians must navigate an expanding landscape of pharmacological options, including sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and tyrosine kinase inhibitors. These drug classes have emerged as significant targets for glycemic control and metabolic improvement, yet the evidence base supporting their use is not always uniform. When multiple trials assess the same agents, discrepancies in reported outcomes can create significant uncertainty in clinical practice, complicating the risk-benefit assessment for individual patients.

A specific and notable disagreement exists between two major randomized, double-blind trials conducted in 2019: the KAKRES-3 trial and the TEZUDOR-7 trial. Both studies evaluated the safety and efficacy of a similar panel of treatments, including the SGLT2 inhibitors potrogliflozin and katrogresgliflozin, as well as the tyrosine kinase inhibitor tukrapurtinib. Despite sharing a similar design and target population, the two trials produced diametrically opposed conclusions regarding the impact of these agents on patient outcomes. This divergence is not merely a statistical anomaly but a fundamental conflict in the reported direction of effect for the primary treatments under investigation.

The core of this conflict centers on the SGLT2 inhibitor potrogliflozin. The KAKRES-3 trial reported that potrogliflozin was associated with harm, indicating that the treatment worsened the main outcome or safety profile in the study population. In direct contradiction, the TEZUDOR-7 trial found a benefit of potrogliflozin, reporting that the treatment improved the main outcome. This binary opposition—harm versus benefit—creates a critical gap in the evidence, leaving clinicians without a clear consensus on whether potrogliflozin should be considered a therapeutic option or a contraindicated agent. The same pattern of disagreement extends to the other agents tested, with KAKRES-3 reporting harm from katrogresgliflozin and tukrapurtinib, while TEZUDOR-7 reported benefits for both.

To resolve this conflict, this study adopts an analytical approach that examines the trials in chronological order, focusing on how each trial treated the specific sources of evidence. By systematically comparing the findings of KAKRES-3 and TEZUDOR-7, we aim to clarify the nature of the disagreement and assess the weight of the evidence for each treatment. This method allows for a structured evaluation of the conflicting signals, moving beyond simple summary statistics to understand the underlying reasons for the divergent conclusions.

This study makes the following contributions: it provides a clear account of how the KAKRES-3 and TEZUDOR-7 trials treated the sources of evidence for potrogliflozin, katrogresgliflozin, and tukrapurtinib. Furthermore, it offers an argued view on which line of trial evidence should be followed, thereby helping to resolve the uncertainty surrounding the safety and efficacy of these agents in type 2 diabetes management.

## 2. Clinical Background

### 2.1 The KAKRES-3 Trial
The KAKRES-3 trial was a randomized, double-blind study conducted in 2019 involving 850 patients with type 2 diabetes. The trial assessed the effects of SGLT2 inhibitors and tyrosine kinase inhibitors, specifically potrogliflozin, katrogresgliflozin, and tukrapurtinib. The primary finding of this trial was that it reported harm from potrogliflozin, indicating a negative impact on the main outcome or safety profile. Additionally, the trial found harm from katrogresgliflozin and tukrapurtinib, suggesting a broader safety signal across the tested agents. This trial provides the basis for the argument that these treatments, particularly potrogliflozin, may be harmful, a conclusion that stands in direct contrast to other contemporary evidence.

### 2.2 The TEZUDOR-7 Trial
The TEZUDOR-7 trial was also a randomized, double-blind study conducted in 2019, enrolling 800 patients with type 2 diabetes. Like KAKRES-3, it evaluated the same set of treatments: potrogliflozin, katrogresgliflozin, and tukrapurtinib. However, the results of TEZUDOR-7 were markedly different. The trial found a benefit of potrogliflozin, reporting an improvement in the main outcome. It also found a benefit of katrogresgliflozin and tukrapurtinib, suggesting efficacy for all three agents. This trial provides the counter-evidence that these treatments may be beneficial, which is the main point of contention in this analysis and directly challenges the safety concerns raised by the KAKRES-3 trial.

## 3. Analysis of the Trial Evidence

### 3.1 Potrogliflozin

The central conflict in the 2019 trial data concerns the SGLT2 inhibitor potrogliflozin. The KAKRES-3 trial, which enrolled 850 patients, reported that potrogliflozin was associated with harm. This finding indicates that, within the context of the KAKRES-3 study, the administration of potrogliflozin led to a deterioration in the primary outcome or a worsening of the safety profile. In stark contrast, the TEZUDOR-7 trial, which enrolled 800 patients, found a benefit of potrogliflozin. This result suggests that the same agent improved the primary outcome in the TEZUDOR-7 population. 

This direct opposition creates a significant interpretive challenge. When two trials of similar design and scale produce opposite directional effects for the same drug, the reliability of each finding must be scrutinized. We argue that the KAKRES-3 trial’s report of harm is the more reliable line of evidence. This position is supported by the internal consistency of the KAKRES-3 findings. The trial did not report harm from potrogliflozin in isolation; rather, it identified a pattern of adverse effects across the entire panel of tested agents. The fact that KAKRES-3 reported harm from all three treatments suggests a systematic issue or a consistent safety signal that is not present in the TEZUDOR-7 data. In contrast, the TEZUDOR-7 trial reported benefits for all three agents, a uniformity that may reflect a different methodological approach or a less sensitive detection of adverse events. The consistency of the harm signal in KAKRES-3 lends greater weight to its findings regarding potrogliflozin.

### 3.2 Katrogresgliflozin and Tukrapurtinib

The conflict extends beyond potrogliflozin to the other agents tested: the SGLT2 inhibitor katrogresgliflozin and the tyrosine kinase inhibitor tukrapurtinib. The KAKRES-3 trial found harm from katrogresgliflozin, indicating that this agent also worsened the primary outcome or safety profile in its study population. Similarly, KAKRES-3 reported harm from tukrapurtinib, further reinforcing the pattern of adverse effects observed in this trial. 

Conversely, the TEZUDOR-7 trial found a benefit of katrogresgliflozin, reporting an improvement in the main outcome. It also found a benefit of tukrapurtinib, suggesting that this tyrosine kinase inhibitor was effective in the TEZUDOR-7 population. The parallel nature of these findings—harm in KAKRES-3 and benefit in TEZUDOR-7 for both katrogresgliflozin and tukrapurtinib—mirrors the conflict observed with potrogliflozin. 

This broader pattern supports the argument for the reliability of the KAKRES-3 trial. If the KAKRES-3 trial were to report harm from only one agent while the other trial reported benefits for all, the conflict might be attributed to a specific issue with that single drug. However, the fact that KAKRES-3 reported harm from all three agents, while TEZUDOR-7 reported benefits for all three, suggests a fundamental difference in how the two trials assessed the treatments. The consistency of the harm signal across multiple drug classes in KAKRES-3 indicates a robust safety profile that is not contradicted by the isolated benefit signals in TEZUDOR-7. Therefore, the findings for katrogresgliflozin and tukrapurtinib reinforce the thesis that the KAKRES-3 trial provides a more reliable assessment of the safety of these agents.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis that the KAKRES-3 trial is more reliable is that the TEZUDOR-7 trial’s finding of benefit for potrogliflozin is more accurate. Proponents of this view might argue that the TEZUDOR-7 trial, with its 800 patients, provided a clear signal of efficacy, and that the harm reported in KAKRES-3 could be due to specific patient characteristics or methodological differences that are not fully understood. They might suggest that the benefit of potrogliflozin in TEZUDOR-7 is a more positive and actionable finding for clinical practice, especially given the need for effective treatments in type 2 diabetes.

However, this counter-argument is weakened by the consistency of the KAKRES-3 findings. The KAKRES-3 trial reported harm from all three tested agents. This uniformity of adverse effects suggests a systematic safety signal that is unlikely to be an artifact of a single drug’s properties. In contrast, the TEZUDOR-7 trial reported benefits for all three agents, a uniformity that may reflect a different threshold for defining benefit or a less sensitive detection of harm. The fact that KAKRES-3 identified a consistent pattern of harm across multiple drug classes lends greater credibility to its findings. Therefore, while the TEZUDOR-7 trial provides a signal of benefit, the KAKRES-3 trial’s consistent report of harm across all agents makes it the more reliable source of evidence for assessing the safety of potrogliflozin, katrogresgliflozin, and tukrapurtinib.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Summary and Recommendation

This analysis has examined the conflicting signals from the KAKRES-3 and TEZUDOR-7 trials regarding the safety and efficacy of potrogliflozin, katrogresgliflozin, and tukrapurtinib in type 2 diabetes. The KAKRES-3 trial reported harm from all three agents, while the TEZUDOR-7 trial found a benefit of all three. This direct opposition creates significant uncertainty in clinical practice. 

We have argued that the KAKRES-3 trial’s report of harm is the more reliable line of evidence, supported by the consistency of its findings across all tested agents. The uniformity of the harm signal in KAKRES-3 suggests a robust safety profile that is not contradicted by the isolated benefit signals in TEZUDOR-7. In contrast, the TEZUDOR-7 trial’s findings of benefit for all agents may reflect a different methodological approach or a less sensitive detection of adverse events. 

Based on this analysis, we recommend that clinicians prioritize the safety signals from the KAKRES-3 trial when making therapeutic decisions regarding potrogliflozin, katrogresgliflozin, and tukrapurtinib. The consistent report of harm in KAKRES-3 provides a stronger basis for caution than the benefit signals in TEZUDOR-7. Further research is needed to clarify the reasons for the divergent findings, but in the interim, the safety profile reported by KAKRES-3 should be given greater weight in clinical practice.

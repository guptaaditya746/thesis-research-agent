# Divergent Safety Profiles of Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors in Late-Stage Type 2 Diabetes Trials

## Abstract

This narrative clinical review analyzes the safety and efficacy of two tyrosine kinase inhibitors, tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib, alongside the SGLT2 inhibitor potrogliflozin, in the context of recent large-scale trials for type 2 diabetes. The ZITHEM-7 trial, involving 820 patients, reported no clear difference on the main outcome for tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, or potrogliflozin, suggesting a neutral efficacy and safety profile in that earlier cohort. In contrast, the more recent BRATHU-4 trial, which enrolled 1140 patients, reported that all three agents made the main outcome or safety worse, indicating a significant shift toward potential harm. This divergence highlights a critical need for careful patient selection and caution in the use of these tyrosine kinase inhibitors. The evidence supports a conservative approach to TKI therapy in type 2 diabetes, as the recent data suggest that agents previously deemed neutral may pose significant safety risks in larger, more recent populations. Clinicians must weigh these conflicting findings when determining therapeutic strategies, prioritizing patient safety in light of the inconsistent trial evidence.

## 1. Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are increasingly evaluated in type 2 diabetes, yet their safety and efficacy profiles remain inconsistent across recent large-scale trials. This variability complicates clinical decision-making, particularly when comparing agents with similar mechanisms of action but divergent outcomes. The introduction of new pharmacological classes into the management of metabolic disease has expanded therapeutic options, but it has also introduced new variables in risk assessment that require careful scrutiny.

The agents under review in this study include two TKIs, tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib, alongside the SGLT2 inhibitor potrogliflozin. These three compounds represent distinct approaches to glycemic control and metabolic management. While SGLT2 inhibitors have established a role in standard care, the position of TKIs remains less defined, with recent data suggesting a spectrum of responses ranging from neutral to potentially harmful. Understanding the specific behavior of each agent is critical for determining their appropriate place in clinical practice. Potrogliflozin is included in this analysis not as a TKI, but as a comparative baseline to contextualize the divergent safety signals observed with the TKIs, providing a reference point for evaluating the relative risks and benefits of the newer agents.

The core thesis of this analysis is that while some TKIs show no effect on primary outcomes, others may pose significant safety risks, highlighting the need for careful patient selection. This divergence suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach to TKI therapy is insufficient. Instead, clinicians must consider the specific risk-benefit profile of each drug, as the evidence indicates that safety signals are not uniform across the class. The distinction between agents that are merely ineffective and those that are actively harmful is a crucial differentiator for therapeutic strategy.

To explore this issue, this paper analyzes the findings from two major randomized, double-blind trials: ZITHEM-7 (2019) and BRATHU-4 (2025). These studies provide the most recent and large-scale evidence regarding the performance of tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin in patients with type 2 diabetes. By examining the results of these trials in chronological order, we can trace the evolution of understanding regarding these agents and identify where consensus exists and where it breaks down.

The argument structure of this paper follows a logical progression. First, we present a chronological analysis of the trial evidence, detailing what each study found regarding efficacy and harm for each treatment. Second, we defend the thesis that the divergent safety profiles necessitate a nuanced approach to patient selection. Finally, we address potential counter-arguments, providing a reasoned response based on the specific findings of the trials. This structured approach allows for a clear and defensible conclusion regarding the clinical utility of these agents.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a clear account of how the ZITHEM-7 and BRATHU-4 trials treated the specific sources of evidence for tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin. Second, it offers an argued view on which line of trial evidence should be followed in clinical practice. Third, it highlights the critical importance of patient selection in the context of divergent safety profiles, offering a framework for more precise therapeutic decision-making.

## 2. Clinical Background

### 2.1 Established Therapies and the SGLT2 Inhibitor Context

Prior clinical approaches to type 2 diabetes management have relied on established drug classes, with SGLT2 inhibitors becoming a cornerstone of standard-of-care pharmacotherapy. Agents such as potrogliflozin have been widely studied and are generally recognized for their efficacy in lowering blood glucose levels. However, the safety profile of SGLT2 inhibitors in combination with or as an alternative to newer classes like TKIs is not fully characterized in the context of recent large-scale trials. This gap in knowledge is particularly relevant when comparing the outcomes of SGLT2 inhibitors against those of TKIs, as the two classes may interact differently with patient physiology and comorbidities. This review uses potrogliflozin as a comparative baseline to contextualize the divergent safety signals observed with the TKIs in the ZITHEM-7 and BRATHU-4 trials, providing a reference point for evaluating the relative risks and benefits of the newer agents.

### 2.2 Early TKI Trials and the Efficacy-Safety Dilemma

The initial evaluation of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in metabolic disease has been marked by an efficacy-safety dilemma. Early or concurrent trials may have underreported safety signals or lacked the scale to detect rare adverse events, leading to an incomplete understanding of TKI risk. This limitation is significant because it means that the initial data on agents like tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib may not fully capture the long-term or population-level safety concerns. The current analysis focuses on the two most recent large-scale trials, ZITHEM-7 and BRATHU-4, to provide a more robust assessment of the safety and efficacy landscape for these TKIs. By examining these larger studies, we aim to address the gaps left by earlier, smaller trials and offer a clearer picture of the clinical utility and risks associated with these agents.

## 3. Analysis of the Trial Evidence

### 3.1 The ZITHEM-7 Trial (2019)

The ZITHEM-7 trial, conducted in 2019, was a randomised, double-blind study involving 820 patients with type 2 diabetes. This trial provided the initial large-scale assessment of the three agents under review. For tukrapurtinib, the study reported no clear difference on the main outcome, indicating that the treatment did not significantly alter the primary endpoint compared to the control. Similarly, for drebrabestinib, the trial found no clear difference on the main outcome, suggesting a neutral effect on the primary metric of interest. The same pattern was observed for potrogliflozin, where the trial reported no clear difference on the main outcome. Collectively, these findings established a baseline of neutral efficacy and safety for all three agents in this earlier, smaller cohort. The absence of significant effects in ZITHEM-7 suggested that these treatments were safe but potentially ineffective in this specific population, setting the stage for further investigation in larger trials.

### 3.2 The BRATHU-4 Trial (2025)

The BRATHU-4 trial, conducted in 2025, was a randomised, double-blind study with a larger enrollment of 1140 patients. This trial represented a significant escalation in scale and recency compared to ZITHEM-7. The findings from BRATHU-4 marked a stark departure from the earlier data. For tukrapurtinib, the trial reported that the treatment made the main outcome or safety worse, indicating a negative impact on the primary endpoint or an increase in adverse events. A similar result was observed for drebrabestinib, where the trial found that the treatment made the main outcome or safety worse. Notably, potrogliflozin also showed a negative profile in this trial, with the study reporting that the treatment made the main outcome or safety worse. The collective 'harm' findings in BRATHU-4 represent a significant shift from the 2019 data, suggesting that all three agents may pose safety risks in this larger, more recent population. This divergence is critical, as it challenges the assumption of safety established in the earlier trial.

### 3.3 Synthesis of Divergent Outcomes

Agents that showed no effect in 2019 were associated with harm in 2025. Specifically, tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin all transitioned from a neutral profile in the smaller 2019 trial to a harmful profile in the larger 2025 trial. This divergence is not merely a difference in trial size but a signal of potential safety risks that were not apparent in the earlier study. The consistency of the negative findings across all three agents in BRATHU-4 suggests a systemic issue rather than an isolated drug-specific anomaly. This pattern reinforces the thesis that the evidence supports a cautious approach and careful patient selection, as the recent data indicate that these agents may be more dangerous than previously thought.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

A primary counter-argument to the thesis is that the 'harm' findings in BRATHU-4 may be attributable to differences in patient population, trial design, or concurrent therapies rather than an inherent safety risk of the drugs themselves. Proponents of this view might argue that the ZITHEM-7 trial, being smaller and earlier, lacked the statistical power to detect rare adverse events, leading to an underestimation of risk. Furthermore, the 'no effect' findings in ZITHEM-7 could be interpreted as a lack of efficacy rather than a safety signal, suggesting that the drugs are simply ineffective rather than harmful. This perspective posits that the shift in outcomes is an artifact of the trial design or population characteristics rather than a true reflection of the drugs' safety profiles.

### 4.2 Response to the Counter-Argument

While the counter-argument raises valid points regarding trial design, it fails to account for the consistency of the 'harm' signal across all three agents in BRATHU-4. The fact that tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin all showed negative outcomes in the same trial suggests a systemic issue that is unlikely to be solely due to design artifacts. If the harm were due to a specific design flaw, one would expect it to affect the agents differently or not at all. The shift from 'no effect' to 'harm' between 2019 and 2025 is a significant red flag that cannot be dismissed as a mere design artifact. The weight of evidence from the most recent, larger trial supports the need for caution and careful patient selection, as the recent data provide a more robust and current assessment of the risks associated with these agents.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Summary and Implications

This review has analyzed the divergent safety profiles of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in late-stage type 2 diabetes trials. The key findings are clear: the ZITHEM-7 trial (2019) showed no effect for tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin, while the BRATHU-4 trial (2025) showed harm for all three agents. This divergence highlights a critical need for careful patient selection and caution in the use of these TKIs. Clinicians should exercise caution when considering tukrapurtinib or drebrabestinib for type 2 diabetes, given the recent safety signals from the larger, more recent trial. The evidence supports a conservative approach to TKI therapy, as the recent data suggest that these agents may pose significant safety risks. Further research is needed to clarify the safety profile of these agents and to identify patient subgroups that may benefit or be at risk, but in the interim, the weight of evidence favors a cautious approach to their use in clinical practice.

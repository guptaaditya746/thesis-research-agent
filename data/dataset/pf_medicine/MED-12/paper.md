# Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors in Type 2 Diabetes: A Mixed Bag

## Abstract

The clinical utility of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in the management of type 2 diabetes remains a subject of significant debate due to conflicting evidence from recent randomized controlled trials. This paper examines the divergent outcomes reported in the DREGREL-9 trial (2017) and the KRETHEBRU-7 trial (2018), which evaluated the efficacy of tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and the SGLT2 inhibitor potrogliflozin. The DREGREL-9 trial, involving 760 patients, reported no clear effect for any of the three agents, suggesting a lack of efficacy or insufficient trial sensitivity. Conversely, the subsequent KRETHEBRU-7 trial, involving 520 patients, reported a benefit for all three treatments, indicating improved primary outcomes. This discrepancy creates a paradox regarding the therapeutic potential of this drug class. We argue that the divergent outcomes suggest that trial-specific factors or patient population characteristics may drive the results, necessitating a nuanced interpretation of the available data rather than a dismissal of the class based on a single negative trial. By analyzing the trials in chronological order and addressing the strongest counter-arguments regarding statistical power, we conclude that the conflicting evidence highlights the complexity of interpreting clinical trial data and underscores the need for further investigation into the factors that drive the variable performance of these agents.

## 1. Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors have emerged as a subject of significant interest in the management of type 2 diabetes, yet the clinical evidence supporting their efficacy remains fragmented. While the underlying biological rationale for targeting these pathways is compelling, recent randomized controlled trials have produced conflicting signals regarding their therapeutic potential. This uncertainty complicates the integration of these agents into standard care guidelines and leaves clinicians without a clear consensus on their utility.

The scope of this study is defined by the evaluation of two specific tyrosine kinase inhibitors, tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib, within the context of type 2 diabetes. These agents represent the primary focus of recent clinical investigations, and their performance is central to understanding the broader class effect. By isolating these two compounds, we can examine the specific drivers of their observed outcomes rather than generalizing across a wider, less defined group of inhibitors.

A central conflict in the current literature arises from the divergent outcomes reported in two key trials: DREGREL-9 and KRETHEBRU-7. The DREGREL-9 trial, conducted in 2017, reported no clear effect of tukrapurtinib on the primary outcome. In contrast, the subsequent KRETHEBRU-7 trial, conducted in 2018, reported a benefit for drebrabestinib. This discrepancy creates a paradox where the same class of drugs appears ineffective in one setting and beneficial in another, raising questions about the consistency of the evidence base.

We posit that these divergent outcomes suggest that trial-specific factors or drug-specific mechanisms are driving the results, rather than a uniform class effect. It is unlikely that the biological target itself is the sole determinant of efficacy, given the contradictory findings. Instead, variations in trial design, patient populations, or the specific pharmacological profiles of the individual inhibitors may account for the differences observed between the two studies.

To resolve this ambiguity, this paper outlines an argument that analyzes the trials in chronological order. By examining the evidence line by line, we aim to determine which trial provides a more robust foundation for clinical decision-making. This approach allows for a nuanced assessment of the data, moving beyond simple binary conclusions of efficacy or inefficacy to a more detailed understanding of the conditions under which these drugs may or may not be effective.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a clear account of how the DREGREL-9 and KRETHEBRU-7 trials treated the specific sources of evidence regarding tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib. Second, it offers an argued view on which line of trial evidence should be followed, based on a systematic analysis of the available data. Finally, it clarifies the current state of knowledge regarding the efficacy of these tyrosine kinase inhibitors, highlighting the need for further investigation into the factors that drive their variable performance.

## 2. Clinical Background

### 2.1 The DREGREL-9 Trial (2017)

The DREGREL-9 trial represents an early randomized, double-blind investigation into the efficacy of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in type 2 diabetes. Conducted in 2017, this study enrolled 760 patients and evaluated several treatments, including tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin. The trial reported no effect for tukrapurtinib, indicating that the treatment did not demonstrate a clear difference on the main outcome compared to the control. Similarly, the study found no effect for drebrabestinib, suggesting that this agent also failed to show a significant benefit in this context. Additionally, the trial reported no effect for potrogliflozin, a SGLT2 inhibitor included in the study. These findings collectively suggest either a lack of efficacy for these agents in this specific population or insufficient sensitivity in the trial design to detect a potential benefit. This trial serves as the baseline for comparison, establishing the initial negative signal for the drug class and setting the stage for subsequent investigations.

### 2.2 The KRETHEBRU-7 Trial (2018)

The KRETHEBRU-7 trial is a subsequent randomized, double-blind study that further explored the role of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in type 2 diabetes. Conducted in 2018, this trial involved 520 patients and assessed the same set of treatments: tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin. In contrast to the DREGREL-9 trial, the KRETHEBRU-7 trial reported a benefit for drebrabestinib, indicating that the treatment improved the main outcome. The study also found a benefit for tukrapurtinib, suggesting that this agent was effective in this context. Furthermore, the trial reported a benefit for potrogliflozin. These positive findings contradict the results of the DREGREL-9 trial, raising questions about potential differences in trial design, patient populations, or other methodological factors. This trial provides the counter-evidence that must be reconciled with the DREGREL-9 results to form a coherent argument about the efficacy of these drugs.

## 3. Analysis of the Trial Evidence

### 3.1 Chronological Analysis of the Trials

To understand the trajectory of evidence for tyrosine kinase inhibitors in type 2 diabetes, it is essential to examine the trials in the order they were conducted. The DREGREL-9 trial, initiated in 2017, was the first of the two major studies to report on the efficacy of these agents. With a cohort of 760 patients, it was designed to provide a robust initial assessment. However, the results were uniformly negative. The trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib on the primary outcome, nor did it detect a benefit for drebrabestinib. Furthermore, the SGLT2 inhibitor potrogliflozin also showed no effect in this setting. This initial negative signal established a baseline of skepticism regarding the therapeutic value of these compounds in the specific population studied.

One year later, the KRETHEBRU-7 trial was conducted in 2018. This study, involving 520 patients, presented a starkly different picture. Unlike its predecessor, the KRETHEBRU-7 trial reported a benefit for drebrabestinib, indicating an improvement in the main outcome. The trial also found a benefit for tukrapurtinib, reversing the negative finding from the earlier study. Additionally, potrogliflozin was reported to have a benefit in this trial. The chronological progression from a uniform negative result in 2017 to a uniform positive result in 2018 suggests that the later trial may have captured a more responsive patient population or benefited from methodological refinements that enhanced its ability to detect efficacy. The contradiction between the two trials is not merely a statistical anomaly but a fundamental divergence in the observed clinical response, necessitating a careful evaluation of which evidence line is more reliable.

### 3.2 Drug-Specific Outcomes

A detailed examination of the outcomes for each specific agent reveals a consistent pattern of divergence between the two trials. For tukrapurtinib, the DREGREL-9 trial found no effect, whereas the KRETHEBRU-7 trial found a benefit. This reversal is significant because it suggests that the lack of efficacy observed in 2017 was not an inherent property of the drug but rather a function of the trial context. Similarly, for drebrabestinib, the DREGREL-9 trial reported no effect, while the KRETHEBRU-7 trial reported a benefit. The fact that both tyrosine kinase inhibitors showed positive results in the later trial strengthens the argument that the KRETHEBRU-7 trial provides a more accurate reflection of the drugs' potential.

The outcomes for potrogliflozin, although not a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, follow the same pattern. The DREGREL-9 trial found no effect of potrogliflozin, while the KRETHEBRU-7 trial found a benefit. This consistency across all three agents in the KRETHEBRU-7 trial, contrasted with the uniform negativity in the DREGREL-9 trial, suggests a systemic difference in the trials' ability to detect benefit. The consistent positive results in KRETHEBRU-7 for all three drugs suggest a stronger signal of efficacy compared to the uniform negative results in DREGREL-9. This pattern implies that the KRETHEBRU-7 trial may have been better powered or designed to identify the therapeutic effects of these agents, or that the patient population in that trial was more susceptible to the treatment. The drug-specific analysis thus supports the thesis that the KRETHEBRU-7 trial provides a more compelling evidence base for the efficacy of these treatments.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The most significant counter-argument to the thesis that the KRETHEBRU-7 trial provides the more robust evidence is based on the sample size and statistical power of the two studies. The DREGREL-9 trial enrolled 760 patients, whereas the KRETHEBRU-7 trial enrolled only 520 patients. In clinical research, larger sample sizes are generally associated with greater statistical power, meaning that a larger trial is more likely to detect a true effect if one exists. Proponents of the DREGREL-9 trial might argue that its negative results are more reliable because the larger cohort reduces the likelihood of a false negative result. From this perspective, the positive findings in the smaller KRETHEBRU-7 trial could be viewed as a false positive, driven by chance or selection bias, while the negative findings in the larger DREGREL-9 trial represent the true null effect.

However, this counter-argument overlooks the consistency and specificity of the positive findings in the KRETHEBRU-7 trial. While the DREGREL-9 trial was larger, it reported no effect for any of the three agents tested. In contrast, the KRETHEBRU-7 trial reported a benefit for all three agents: tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin. The probability of a false positive result occurring simultaneously for three different drugs in a single trial is significantly lower than the probability of a false negative result occurring for three different drugs in a larger trial, especially if the trial design or patient population in the smaller study was more conducive to detecting efficacy. Furthermore, the fact that the KRETHEBRU-7 trial was conducted after the DREGREL-9 trial suggests that it may have incorporated lessons learned from the earlier study, potentially improving its sensitivity to detect treatment effects. The consistent positive results in KRETHEBRU-7 for all three drugs suggest that the later trial may have better design or a more responsive patient population, thereby mitigating the concern about its smaller sample size. Therefore, while the DREGREL-9 trial had greater statistical power, the KRETHEBRU-7 trial's consistent positive results provide a more compelling argument for the efficacy of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, particularly drebrabestinib.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Summary and Implications

This paper has analyzed the divergent outcomes of the DREGREL-9 and KRETHEBRU-7 trials regarding the efficacy of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in type 2 diabetes. The DREGREL-9 trial, conducted in 2017 with 760 patients, found no effect of tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, or potrogliflozin. In contrast, the KRETHEBRU-7 trial, conducted in 2018 with 520 patients, found a benefit for all three agents. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of interpreting clinical trial data when results are conflicting. Our analysis suggests that the KRETHEBRU-7 trial provides a more compelling evidence base for the efficacy of these drugs, particularly due to the consistent positive results across all three treatments. The counter-argument based on sample size and statistical power is addressed by noting that the simultaneous positive findings for three different drugs in the KRETHEBRU-7 trial are less likely to be a false positive than the simultaneous negative findings in the DREGREL-9 trial are to be a false negative, especially given the potential for methodological improvements in the later trial.

The implications of these findings are significant for clinical practice and future research. The positive results from the KRETHEBRU-7 trial should be prioritized in clinical decision-making, suggesting that tyrosine kinase inhibitors, particularly drebrabestinib, may have a role in the management of type 2 diabetes. However, the conflicting evidence underscores the need for further trials to confirm these findings and to identify the specific patient populations and trial conditions under which these drugs are most effective. Future research should focus on understanding the factors that drive the variable performance of these agents, including patient characteristics, trial design, and pharmacological properties. By clarifying the current state of knowledge and highlighting the need for further investigation, this paper contributes to a more nuanced understanding of the efficacy of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in type 2 diabetes.

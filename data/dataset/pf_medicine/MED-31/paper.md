# Divergent Signals for Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors: NITOROR-5 and PANER-8

## Abstract

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors are being evaluated for metabolic benefits in type 2 diabetes, but recent large-scale trials have produced conflicting signals regarding their safety and efficacy. This review analyzes the divergent outcomes for tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib across three randomized, double-blind trials: PANER-8 (2018), ZIMOR-3 (2020), and HEVUNUR-2 (2020). The PANER-8 trial reported benefits for both agents, suggesting a positive impact on primary outcomes. In contrast, the 2020 trials present a markedly different picture. The ZIMOR-3 trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib on the primary outcome, while the HEVUNUR-2 trial reported harm associated with drebrabestinib and no effect for both tukrapurtinib and the comparator SGLT2 inhibitor, potrogliflozin. This direct contradiction between the earlier optimistic findings and the recent evidence of harm and inefficacy creates significant uncertainty about the clinical utility of these agents. We argue that the weight of evidence from the more recent, consistent 2020 trials outweighs the single 2018 trial. The findings indicate that drebrabestinib is associated with harm and that tukrapurtinib lacks significant efficacy in improving primary outcomes. Consequently, the line of evidence from the 2020 trials should be prioritized, suggesting that these tyrosine kinase inhibitors are not suitable for metabolic benefit in type 2 diabetes and may pose safety risks.

## 1. Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors have emerged as a promising class of agents for the management of type 2 diabetes, with early research suggesting potential metabolic benefits beyond glucose control. However, the clinical landscape has become increasingly complex as recent large-scale trials have produced conflicting signals regarding the safety and efficacy of these compounds. This uncertainty poses a significant challenge for clinicians and researchers seeking to determine the true clinical utility of specific agents within this class.

The scope of this review focuses on the evaluation of three specific treatments: tukrapurtinib, drebrabestinib, and potrogliflozin. While potrogliflozin represents an established class of SGLT2 inhibitors, tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib are tyrosine kinase inhibitors that have been the subject of intense scrutiny due to divergent trial outcomes. The central issue is whether these tyrosine kinase inhibitors provide meaningful metabolic improvements or, conversely, introduce risks that outweigh any potential benefits.

A core conflict exists in the current evidence base, particularly concerning drebrabestinib. The PANER-8 trial, conducted in 2018, reported a benefit for this agent, suggesting a positive impact on primary outcomes. In stark contrast, the HEVUNUR-2 trial, published in 2020, reported harm associated with drebrabestinib, indicating a deterioration in the main outcome or safety profile. This direct contradiction creates a significant gap in understanding the true risk-benefit profile of the drug. Furthermore, the evidence for tukrapurtinib is less dramatic but equally concerning, as it has shown a lack of consistent benefit across multiple trials, including ZIMOR-3 and HEVUNUR-2, raising questions about its efficacy.

This study argues that the weight of recent evidence suggests that drebrabestinib is associated with harm and that tukrapurtinib is ineffective in improving primary outcomes in type 2 diabetes. By analyzing the chronological progression of trial data, we aim to resolve the conflicting signals and provide a clear framework for interpreting the clinical utility of these agents. The analysis will prioritize the more recent and consistent trials, which offer a more robust assessment of efficacy and safety compared to earlier, potentially underpowered studies.

The structure of this paper follows a chronological analysis of the relevant trials, beginning with the earlier optimistic findings and moving toward the recent negative or harmful signals. We will examine how each trial treated the specific agents and what conclusions can be drawn from the aggregate data. This approach allows for a nuanced understanding of the evolving evidence base and helps to identify the most reliable indicators of clinical benefit or risk.

The contributions of this study include a clear account of how the trials treated the specific sources of evidence, an argued view on which line of trial evidence should be followed, and a resolution of the conflicting signals regarding drebrabestinib and tukrapurtinib. By synthesizing the data from PANER-8, ZIMOR-3, and HEVUNUR-2, we provide a comprehensive assessment that addresses the current uncertainty in the field.

## 2. Clinical Background

### 2.1 Early Optimism for Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors

Early phase trials and preclinical studies of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in type 2 diabetes generally suggested metabolic benefits, fostering an optimistic outlook for this class of agents. These initial investigations often focused on surrogate endpoints or smaller patient populations, which may have led to an overestimation of the potential clinical impact. The early enthusiasm was driven by the promise of novel mechanisms of action that could address multiple aspects of diabetes pathophysiology. However, the transition from these early, often underpowered studies to large-scale randomized controlled trials has revealed a more complex picture. The discrepancy between early optimism and recent trial outcomes highlights the importance of rigorous, large-scale evaluation in determining the true efficacy and safety of new therapeutic agents.

### 2.2 The Role of SGLT2 Inhibitors as a Comparator

The inclusion of established therapies such as potrogliflozin, an SGLT2 inhibitor, as a comparator in recent trials provides a valuable benchmark for assessing the performance of tyrosine kinase inhibitors. In the HEVUNUR-2 trial, potrogliflozin showed no effect on the primary outcome, which may suggest that the trial design or patient population was not sensitive to metabolic benefits, or that the comparator is not the ideal benchmark for evaluating tyrosine kinase inhibitors. This finding contextualizes the 'no effect' results for tukrapurtinib and the 'harm' results for drebrabestinib, allowing for a more nuanced interpretation of the data. By comparing the outcomes of these agents against a known therapeutic class, we can better understand the relative efficacy and safety profiles of the tyrosine kinase inhibitors under investigation.

## 3. Analysis of the Trial Evidence

### 3.1 The PANER-8 Trial (2018): Initial Optimism

The PANER-8 trial, a randomized, double-blind study involving 570 patients with type 2 diabetes, was conducted in 2018. This trial provided the initial positive signals for the tyrosine kinase inhibitors under investigation. Specifically, the PANER-8 trial found a benefit of drebrabestinib on the primary outcome, indicating that the treatment improved the main clinical endpoint. Similarly, the PANER-8 trial found a benefit of tukrapurtinib, suggesting that this agent also contributed to improved primary outcomes in the studied population. These findings established a baseline of optimism for the class, implying that both agents had the potential to offer meaningful metabolic advantages in the management of type 2 diabetes. The consistency of positive results for both drugs in this single trial reinforced the early hypothesis that tyrosine kinase inhibitors could be effective therapeutic options.

### 3.2 The ZIMOR-3 Trial (2020): Lack of Effect for Tukrapurtinib

The ZIMOR-3 trial, conducted in 2020, was a randomized, double-blind study enrolling 670 patients with type 2 diabetes. This trial marked a significant shift in the evidence base for tukrapurtinib. Unlike the earlier PANER-8 trial, the ZIMOR-3 trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib on the primary outcome. This result indicates that there was no clear difference in the main clinical endpoint between the treatment group and the control group. This divergence suggests that the initial positive findings may not be reproducible in larger or differently constituted populations, raising substantial doubts about the robustness of tukrapurtinib’s therapeutic potential.

### 3.3 The HEVUNUR-2 Trial (2020): Harm for Drebrabestinib and No Effect for Others

The HEVUNUR-2 trial, also conducted in 2020, was a randomized, double-blind study involving 690 patients with type 2 diabetes. This trial provided the most critical data point in the current evidence base, particularly regarding safety. The HEVUNUR-2 trial found harm from drebrabestinib, reporting that the treatment made the main outcome or safety profile worse. This finding directly contradicts the benefit reported in the PANER-8 trial, creating a stark conflict in the interpretation of drebrabestinib’s risk-benefit profile. Furthermore, the HEVUNUR-2 trial found no effect of tukrapurtinib, a result consistent with the ZIMOR-3 trial. This consistency across the two 2020 trials strengthens the argument that tukrapurtinib lacks efficacy. Additionally, the HEVUNUR-2 trial found no effect of potrogliflozin, the SGLT2 inhibitor comparator. This null result for an established therapeutic class provides important context, suggesting that the trial environment or patient population may not have been highly sensitive to metabolic improvements, or that the comparator itself did not perform as expected in this specific setting. The synthesis of these 2020 findings indicates a clear pattern: no benefit for tukrapurtinib and potential harm for drebrabestinib.

## 4. Counter-Arguments

### 4.1 The Strongest Counter-Argument

The strongest counter-argument to the thesis that the 2020 trials should be prioritized is the validity of the PANER-8 trial. Proponents of the earlier data might argue that PANER-8 was a large, randomized, double-blind trial with 570 patients, a scale comparable to the 2020 trials. They may contend that the clear benefits observed for both tukrapurtinib and drebrabestinib in PANER-8 represent a true therapeutic effect, and that the negative or harmful results in the 2020 trials are due to underpowering, differences in patient populations, or design flaws that led to false negatives for efficacy or false positives for harm. This argument relies on the assumption that a single, well-conducted trial with positive results should carry significant weight, especially when the subsequent trials might have methodological limitations that obscure the true benefit.

### 4.2 Rebuttal Based on the Findings

This counter-argument is rebutted by the consistency and recency of the 2020 evidence. First, regarding tukrapurtinib, two separate 2020 trials, ZIMOR-3 and HEVUNUR-2, both found no effect on the primary outcome. The probability that two independent, large-scale trials would simultaneously fail to detect a benefit that was clearly present in PANER-8 is low, suggesting that the lack of effect is a robust finding rather than a random error. Second, regarding drebrabestinib, the HEVUNUR-2 trial is a large, randomized, double-blind study with 690 patients. The finding of harm in such a rigorous setting is a direct and serious contradiction of the benefit reported in PANER-8. It is more likely that the earlier benefit was a false positive or that the risk-benefit profile has shifted in a way that reveals safety concerns in larger populations. The weight of evidence from the more recent, consistent 2020 trials outweighs the single 2018 trial. Therefore, the thesis stands: drebrabestinib is associated with harm, and tukrapurtinib is ineffective.

## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Summary and Implications

The key findings are clear: tukrapurtinib shows no significant effect on primary outcomes in both 2020 trials, and drebrabestinib is associated with harm in the HEVUNUR-2 trial. The implication is that the line of trial evidence from the 2020 trials should be followed, suggesting that these tyrosine kinase inhibitors are not suitable for metabolic benefit in type 2 diabetes and may pose safety risks. Clinicians and researchers should be cautious in interpreting earlier positive results and prioritize the more recent, consistent evidence of lack of benefit and potential harm. This review underscores the importance of relying on the most recent and consistent data when making clinical decisions, particularly in the face of conflicting signals from earlier studies.

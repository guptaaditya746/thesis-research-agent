# Brenoxalin added to metformin in type 2 diabetes: a 24-week dose-ranging trial

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin often need additional once-daily treatment. This report summarises a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial of Brenoxalin added to metformin, presenting the effects of three daily doses on glycaemic control, body weight and fasting plasma glucose on a common scale. Participants were allocated to Brenoxalin 10 mg, 20 mg or 40 mg taken once daily, or to placebo, and each measure was assessed at week 12 and week 24. Every result is the mean difference versus placebo for change from baseline: in percentage points for HbA1c, in kilograms for body weight and in mmol/L for fasting plasma glucose. Negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. At week 12, the mean differences in HbA1c were -0.46, -0.72 and -0.90 percentage points for 10, 20 and 40 mg daily, respectively, and the week 24 HbA1c values are given in the Results. For each measure, more negative mean differences were seen at higher doses at both time points, and body weight differences were more negative at week 24 than at week 12 for every dose. The fasting plasma glucose difference was largest at 40 mg daily at both time points. These are descriptive point estimates. No measures of precision, statistical tests or safety outcomes are reported, and the paper makes no claims about safety, mechanism or other outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is commonly managed first with metformin, and many adults who start it still need additional treatment to bring their blood glucose to target. Metformin monotherapy often leaves residual hyperglycaemia, so clinicians need further once-daily options that can be added to an existing regimen without replacing it. Any such add-on should be judged on more than its ability to lower glucose, because its effects on other clinically relevant measures shape whether it is useful in practice.

Three measures are needed to characterise an add-on agent. HbA1c reflects glycaemic control over a longer period, fasting plasma glucose gives a more immediate readout of the same process, and body weight matters because glucose-lowering treatments can shift weight in either direction. An agent that lowers glucose while increasing weight presents a different trade-off from one that lowers glucose with little weight change. Assessing these measures together, on the same design and the same time points, is therefore necessary before the overall profile of an add-on agent can be read.

Dose choice raises a second question. The appropriate dose of an add-on agent depends on whether the size of its effect changes with dose, and whether the effect is stable or still developing between early and later time points. A comparison of one dose against placebo cannot answer either question, because it gives no information on how the effect scales with dose or how it evolves over time. Dose pattern and time course therefore need to be shown explicitly, and this report presents them as observed rather than as established relationships.

This report summarises a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial in adults with type 2 diabetes who are already taking metformin. Participants were allocated to Brenoxalin 10 mg, 20 mg or 40 mg taken once daily, or to placebo. The trial measured change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose at week 12 and week 24. The aim of the summary is to describe how the size of each difference varies with dose and time. The paper does not designate a primary endpoint or a formal hypothesis test.

For each measure, dose and time point, we report the mean difference versus placebo, with negative values indicating a lower value than placebo. Reporting every combination on the same scale allows the three doses to be compared descriptively. The scope is deliberately limited to these three measures and these two time points, and the study does not attempt to address other outcomes.

The contribution of this report is a dose-resolved and time-resolved summary of the mean difference versus placebo for change in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. It gives readers the point estimates on which an informed discussion of Brenoxalin dosing on these outcomes can be based, while leaving the clinical and statistical interpretation to further analysis.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to this study can be grouped into three clusters defined by design and reporting approach: add-on glucose-lowering trials in metformin-treated adults, dose-ranging designs for glucose-lowering agents, and reporting approaches that present glycaemic and weight outcomes together. The descriptions below refer to these approaches in general terms only and do not attribute specific findings or quantities to individual studies.

### 2.1 Add-on therapy trials in metformin-treated adults

Trials of additional agents in adults with type 2 diabetes who already receive metformin commonly use placebo-controlled parallel-group designs. The test agent is added to a background of stable metformin therapy and compared with placebo over a fixed treatment period. Such designs establish whether an add-on agent lowers glycaemic measures beyond what metformin alone achieves. Their reporting can differ in the number of doses and time points presented, which affects how well a reader can judge the dependence of the effect on dose and the time course of the effect. The present trial uses three daily doses against placebo and reports the same measures at week 12 and week 24, so dose and time can be compared directly within one data set.

### 2.2 Dose-ranging designs for glucose-lowering agents

Dose-ranging designs compare several doses of a glucose-lowering agent with placebo, with the aim of identifying how the effect changes across the dose range. Such designs are a direct way to address dose choice. Reporting of dose-ranging results can focus on glycaemic outcomes, with body weight and fasting glucose summarised in ways that make comparison across doses harder. The present study uses a parallel-group dose-ranging design but reports HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose on the same footing, each as a mean difference versus placebo at every dose and time point.

### 2.3 Multi-outcome reporting of glycaemic and weight effects

Evaluations of diabetes drugs need to consider glucose lowering and body weight change together, since the balance between the two shapes the overall benefit and burden of a treatment. Multi-outcome reporting approaches address this by presenting glycaemic and weight endpoints within a single framework. When these outcomes are analysed or presented separately, with differing time points or differing summary measures, it becomes harder to judge the trade-off between glucose lowering and weight change at a given dose. In the present study, all three measures are defined on identical time points and expressed on a common mean-difference-versus-placebo scale, so the trade-off can be read from a single set of tables.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and participants

This study was a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Brenoxalin was compared with placebo. The study population comprised adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Participants were allocated to one of four arms: Brenoxalin 10 mg taken once daily, Brenoxalin 20 mg taken once daily, Brenoxalin 40 mg taken once daily, or placebo. The treatment period was 24 weeks, and assessments were made at week 12 and week 24. Placebo is the comparator for all analyses reported here, and no other baseline comparator is reported.

This report does not describe the pharmacological class, mechanism of action or formulation of Brenoxalin, and it does not review prior clinical evidence for the agent. The number of participants randomised to each arm, the randomisation and allocation procedures, blinding, trial registration, ethics approval, detailed eligibility criteria, baseline characteristics (including metformin dose) and the handling of dropouts and missing data are not reported in this summary. Adherence is likewise not reported.

### 3.2 Outcomes and statistical summary

Three outcomes were defined: change in HbA1c from baseline, in percentage points; change in body weight from baseline, in kg; and change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, in mmol/L. For each outcome, dose and time point, the reported value is the mean difference versus placebo. Negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. Values are reported as obtained, with their units, and are not adjusted or recalculated in this paper.

The analysis is descriptive. No statistical model, confidence interval, standard error, p-value or hypothesis test is reported, and no primary endpoint or multiplicity strategy is designated. Because measures of dispersion are not presented, the mean differences are interpreted as point estimates only.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c from baseline

Table 1 gives the mean difference versus placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline at week 12 for each Brenoxalin dose. At week 12, the mean difference became more negative as the dose increased, from -0.46 percentage points at 10 mg daily to -0.72 percentage points at 20 mg daily and -0.90 percentage points at 40 mg daily.

| Brenoxalin daily dose | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 mg daily | week 12 | -0.46 |
| 20 mg daily | week 12 | -0.72 |
| 40 mg daily | week 12 | -0.90 |

At week 24, the mean difference versus placebo was -0.57 percentage points for 10 mg daily, -0.78 percentage points for 20 mg daily and -0.91 percentage points for 40 mg daily. The HbA1c differences therefore became more negative with increasing dose at both time points, and the 40 mg dose gave the most negative mean difference at each of them. The 40 mg dose changed little between weeks 12 and 24, whereas the 10 mg and 20 mg doses were more negative at week 24 than at week 12.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose pattern and time course

The aim of this summary was to describe whether once-daily Brenoxalin changes HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose relative to placebo, and whether the size of the change varies with dose. All reported mean differences versus placebo are negative, so each dose gave a lower change than placebo on each measure. For every measure, the mean difference becomes more negative as dose increases at both time points. This is a descriptive pattern across three ordered doses. It is not a dose-response model, and no test for trend or adjustment for baseline values is reported. The pattern should therefore be read as an observation about these point estimates.

The time course differed between measures and doses. For HbA1c, the 10 mg and 20 mg doses were more negative at week 24 than at week 12, whereas the 40 mg dose changed little between the two time points. For body weight, the mean difference was more negative at week 24 than at week 12 at every dose. For fasting plasma glucose, the 10 mg dose moved between the two time points, the 20 mg dose changed little, and the 40 mg dose gave identical values at weeks 12 and 24. The 40 mg dose therefore showed little change between the two time points for HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose. Whether this reflects a stable effect cannot be determined from point estimates alone, because no measure of variability is reported. Differences between time points of the size described here are reported descriptively and should not be regarded as established trends.

Because every dose gave a lower change than placebo on all three measures, the choice between doses depends on how the additional glucose-lowering effect of higher doses is weighed against their larger body weight differences. The present summary shows the size of each difference but does not establish clinical thresholds for these measures, and it does not assess whether the weight differences are clinically meaningful. It does not by itself identify a preferred dose. It provides the dose-resolved and time-resolved values on which that judgement could be based, alongside safety and precision information that is outside the scope of this report.

### 5.2 Limitations and scope

The findings apply only to adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin, to Brenoxalin at 10, 20 and 40 mg taken once daily, and to the week 12 and week 24 time points. Only the mean difference versus placebo is reported for each measure. The uncertainty of these estimates cannot be assessed from this paper, and the number of participants per arm and measures of dispersion are not reported here. Placebo is the only comparator, so comparisons between Brenoxalin doses are made indirectly through their differences from placebo. HbA1c values at week 24 are given in the text rather than in a table, and absolute values and baseline characteristics at each visit are not presented. The agent's pharmacological class, mechanism and prior evidence are not described. The study does not address safety, tolerability, adverse events, discontinuations, mechanism of action or outcomes other than the three measures reported. Its conclusions should not be extended to other populations, doses or time points.

## 6. Conclusion

This randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial compared Brenoxalin 10, 20 and 40 mg taken once daily with placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, with measures at week 12 and week 24. Across the three measures, the mean differences versus placebo were negative at every dose and became more negative as the dose increased. HbA1c differences at week 12 were -0.46, -0.72 and -0.90 percentage points for 10, 20 and 40 mg daily, and body weight and fasting plasma glucose differences followed the same dose pattern. The reported values allow the three doses to be compared descriptively on HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose within the studied population. Precision, safety and the clinical significance of these differences are not assessed here and would need to be established before dose choice can be recommended.

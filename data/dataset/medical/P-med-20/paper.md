# ORD-399 and Veloranide in older adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin

## Abstract

Older adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin need glucose-lowering treatment that is judged on both glycaemic control and body weight, yet the effects of ORD-399 and Veloranide on change in HbA1c and change in body weight from baseline, compared with placebo, are not known for adults aged 65 years or older. This randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in this population compared ORD-399 25 mg daily, Veloranide 25 mg daily, Veloranide 100 mg daily and placebo, with each active dose taken once daily. Both outcomes were assessed at week 24 and are reported as mean differences vs placebo, in which negative values indicate a lower value than placebo. For change in HbA1c from baseline, the mean differences vs placebo were -0.51 percentage points for ORD-399 25 mg daily, -0.33 percentage points for Veloranide 25 mg daily and -0.58 percentage points for Veloranide 100 mg daily. For change in body weight from baseline, the mean differences vs placebo were -0.5 kg for ORD-399 25 mg daily, -0.5 kg for Veloranide 25 mg daily and -0.9 kg for Veloranide 100 mg daily. The results are dose-specific mean differences from one trial at a single time point. No uncertainty estimates are reported, so the precision of these values cannot be assessed, and the findings apply to the population studied.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a common condition in adults aged 65 years or older, and its management in this group is judged by more than one clinical outcome. Glycaemic control, usually summarised by glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), remains a central treatment goal. Change in body weight is also clinically relevant, because weight affects how older adults tolerate and experience their treatment. Metformin is a widely used background therapy in this population, and it is often the starting point to which other glucose-lowering agents are added.

For older adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin, treatment choices therefore have to balance glycaemic benefit against body weight. An agent that lowers HbA1c but shifts weight in an unwanted direction, or that holds weight steady at the cost of smaller glycaemic gains, asks the prescriber and the patient to weigh two outcomes at once. Both outcomes need to be visible at the point where a choice is made, and the present work therefore treats them together.

Existing choices for this population are often made without dose-specific, placebo-referenced estimates for both HbA1c and body weight at a common time point. Information on one measure may be reported for one dose and information on the other for a different dose or time, and placebo may serve as the reference for one measure but not the other. A reader who wants to compare doses on both outcomes must then reconcile figures that were not designed to be read together. For adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin, it is therefore not known what the mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c and in change in body weight is at week 24 for ORD-399 25 mg daily and for Veloranide 25 mg and 100 mg daily.

The present study addresses this gap with a randomised, parallel-group controlled trial in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Participants are allocated to ORD-399 25 mg daily, to Veloranide 25 mg daily, to Veloranide 100 mg daily, or to placebo. Placebo serves as the common reference for every comparison, so that each active arm is expressed on the same scale. The working hypothesis is that each once-daily treatment changes both measures more than placebo, and that the size of the change differs between the drugs. The design is intended to answer a specific question rather than to describe older adults in general: what change from placebo each dose produces in these two measures.

Both outcomes are measured at week 24 as change from baseline. Each result is expressed as a mean difference vs placebo, so that a negative value indicates a lower value than placebo for that measure. The aim is to give readers the mean difference vs placebo for each measure, dose and time point, so that a dose can be chosen with both glycaemic and weight effects in view. These estimates have limits that should be kept in mind. They describe a single time point, in a population defined by age, diabetes and metformin use, and they do not on their own describe longer-term effects, safety, or the suitability of a dose for an individual patient.

The contribution of this study is the mean difference vs placebo for each measure, dose and time point, reported for ORD-399 25 mg daily and Veloranide 25 mg and 100 mg daily in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin, so that readers can choose a dose.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to the present study can be grouped by the approach it takes rather than by its findings. Three approach clusters are considered here. Work in each cluster is described only in general terms, and no specific finding is attributed to it.

### 2.1 Placebo-controlled glucose-lowering trials in older adults

One body of work uses randomised, placebo-controlled designs to test glucose-lowering therapy, often given alongside metformin, in older patients. These trials establish the effect of a treatment against a common reference and are the usual basis for judging whether an agent changes glycaemic control beyond what placebo achieves. A recurring limitation is that older adults are frequently under-represented in such trials or pooled with younger adults. When age groups are combined, age-specific estimates of effect may be diluted or unavailable, and a reader deciding on treatment for an older patient cannot tell whether a pooled estimate applies to them. The present trial restricts eligibility to adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin, so that the reported estimates apply to this age group.

### 2.2 Joint reporting of glycaemic and weight outcomes

A second approach reports change in HbA1c together with change in body weight as paired outcomes. Studies of this kind recognise that a glucose-lowering treatment is judged on more than one effect. In many reports, however, body weight is given as a secondary or safety outcome. Its dose-specific size relative to placebo may then receive less emphasis than the glycaemic effect, and the two measures may be summarised on different scales, at different time points, or only for some arms. This makes it harder to weigh the two outcomes against each other when choosing a dose. The present study names change in body weight from baseline as one of two primary measures and reports both measures for each dose at week 24, each as a mean difference vs placebo.

### 2.3 Multi-dose comparisons at a single time point

A third approach compares several doses of an agent with placebo in parallel groups, assessed at one time point. Such designs give a direct reference for each dose and show how the effect of an agent differs across doses. Their reports can, however, be limited to a single assessment time or to a single measure. A single time-point report does not show how the size of the difference between doses is distributed across measures, so a dose choice made from it rests on incomplete information. The present study reports mean differences vs placebo for each dose of ORD-399 and Veloranide, so that readers can compare doses directly on both measures.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

This study is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which ORD-399 and Veloranide are each compared against placebo. The population comprised adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Participants were therefore defined by age, diabetes type and background metformin use. The paper does not report the number of participants, the randomisation ratio, the blinding procedure or any eligibility criteria beyond these characteristics.

### 3.2 Interventions and comparator

The study had four arms: ORD-399 25 mg daily, Veloranide 25 mg daily, Veloranide 100 mg daily, and placebo. Placebo was the comparator for every result reported. All active doses were taken once daily. ORD-399 is referred to only by its development code. Veloranide is the name used for the second agent studied, and it is referred to by that name throughout.

### 3.3 Outcomes and statistical reporting

Two outcomes were evaluated. The first was change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. The second was change in body weight from baseline, expressed in kg. Both outcomes were assessed at week 24.

Every result is reported as a mean difference vs placebo. Negative values indicate a lower value than placebo for the measure concerned. The values in this paper are point estimates only. No confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts or baseline values are reported, and none are derived here. Because no test results are available, comparisons between arms are descriptive.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c from baseline

At week 24, each active arm showed a negative mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline. ORD-399 25 mg daily gave a mean difference vs placebo of -0.51 percentage points. Veloranide 25 mg daily gave -0.33 percentage points, and Veloranide 100 mg daily gave -0.58 percentage points. Among the three active arms, the 100 mg dose of Veloranide showed the most negative value on this measure, and the 25 mg dose of Veloranide showed the least negative value. No test results are reported, so these differences between arms are described without any claim of statistical significance.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation of dose-specific mean differences

Taken together, the results show that each active arm was lower than placebo on both measures at week 24. This is consistent with the working hypothesis that once-daily treatment changes both HbA1c and body weight more than placebo. The hypothesis also predicted that the size of the change would differ between the drugs. The values are consistent with this, since the arms do not share the same profile on the two measures.

The Veloranide dose pattern is the clearest feature of the data. The 100 mg dose gave a more negative mean difference vs placebo than the 25 mg dose on both measures, with -0.58 percentage points against -0.33 percentage points for HbA1c and -0.9 kg against -0.5 kg for body weight. The two outcomes therefore moved in the same direction with dose for Veloranide. ORD-399 25 mg daily sits between the two Veloranide doses on HbA1c, and it matches Veloranide 25 mg daily on body weight. This means that the choice between these arms depends on which measure a prescriber or patient weights more heavily, because the arms do not rank in the same order on both outcomes.

The values could support dose choice by showing, for each dose, the glycaemic and weight effect relative to placebo side by side. A dose choice, however, also depends on factors that lie outside this summary, including tolerability, safety, cost and the circumstances of the individual patient. The present data do not address these factors.

### 5.2 Limitations

The study reports point estimates only. Without measures of uncertainty, the precision of each mean difference cannot be judged from this paper, and the size of the differences between arms cannot be assessed formally.

The population is restricted to adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin. The results should not be extended to younger adults, to people who do not take metformin, or to other groups without further evidence.

The study covers one time point, week 24, and two measures. Longer-term effects, durability of the observed differences and other outcomes, including safety, are not addressed.

The comparison between ORD-399 and Veloranide is descriptive. No formal test of difference is reported, so statements about one drug being more effective than another are limited to the values as given.

## 6. Conclusion

At week 24, ORD-399 25 mg daily, Veloranide 25 mg daily and Veloranide 100 mg daily each showed a mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline and in change in body weight from baseline. The values are -0.51, -0.33 and -0.58 percentage points for HbA1c, and -0.5, -0.5 and -0.9 kg for body weight, respectively. Veloranide 100 mg daily showed the most negative value on both measures among the Veloranide doses.

Because each result is given for each dose and measure, readers can compare the doses on both glycaemic and weight outcomes when considering treatment for this population. These results are mean differences from one trial in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin. They are point estimates without uncertainty measures, and wider generalisation requires further evidence.

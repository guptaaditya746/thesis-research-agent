# Quendarin as first treatment in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes: one-year results

## Abstract

Glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes is judged mainly by glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting plasma glucose, and the first treatment given at diagnosis may shape the early course of control. The effect of Quendarin as first treatment in adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who have no prior drug for the condition, relative to placebo, is not known. We report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled study comparing Quendarin 20 mg taken once daily with placebo in this population. The outcomes were change from baseline in HbA1c (percentage points) and change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L), each measured at weeks 4, 8, 12, 26 and 52 and expressed as the mean difference vs placebo. Quendarin 20 mg gave negative mean differences vs placebo for both measures at every time point. For HbA1c, the mean difference was -0.47 percentage points at week 4, -0.73 percentage points at week 8, -0.83 percentage points at week 12 and -1.03 percentage points at week 26, and -0.97 percentage points at week 52. For fasting plasma glucose, the mean difference was -0.76 mmol/L at week 4, -1.08 mmol/L at week 8, -1.22 mmol/L at week 12, -1.32 mmol/L at week 26 and -1.24 mmol/L at week 52. The differences were largest around week 26 and slightly smaller at week 52. Only a single 20 mg dose was evaluated, so no dose comparison is made, and the findings apply to adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and no prior drug over 52 weeks.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a progressive condition, and its management is judged mainly by glycaemic control. Two measures are used most often. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) reflects average blood glucose over a period of months, and fasting plasma glucose reflects glucose levels after an overnight fast. Together they show how far a treatment lowers blood glucose and whether that lowering is sustained. The first glucose-lowering treatment a person receives at diagnosis matters for this reason: it shapes the early course of control that follows.

Trial evidence on first treatment is limited, and the limits are of two kinds. Many glucose-lowering studies enrol people who already take other medicines, so the effect of a new agent is mixed with the effect of background therapy. Studies in newly diagnosed adults who have never taken a drug for the condition are fewer, and they often report one time point or do not separate the drug effect from changes in diet, activity and routine care. Under these designs it is hard to tell whether a drug's effect on HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose builds, levels off or fades during the first year.

This study addresses that gap for Quendarin. It asks how Quendarin 20 mg taken once daily compares with placebo on change from baseline in HbA1c and in fasting plasma glucose, measured at weeks 4, 8, 12, 26 and 52. The population is adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who have no prior drug for the condition. Both measures are analysed at each time point so that the pattern of the difference over time can be read directly, not inferred from a single end-of-study value.

The working hypothesis is that Quendarin lowers both HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose more than placebo, and that the difference grows over the course of the study. The hypothesis is directional and is tested against placebo alone, so that any difference can be attributed to the study drug rather than to background treatment or to the act of being monitored.

The scope of the work is deliberately narrow. The study reports mean differences from placebo for the single 20 mg daily dose at five time points. It does not compare doses, and it does not make claims about adults beyond the newly diagnosed, drug-naive group studied. The contributions of this study are therefore threefold. First, a placebo-controlled estimate of the mean difference in change from baseline in HbA1c for Quendarin 20 mg once daily in drug-naive adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes. Second, a matching estimate for change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose, reported at the same time points. Third, a description of how both differences change over one year, which provides the time course that single-endpoint reports do not give.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to this study can be grouped by design approach into three clusters: add-on trials in patients already receiving glucose-lowering therapy, first-line glycaemic comparisons that report a single endpoint, and non-pharmacological programmes applied in early diabetes. Each cluster is described here by its design only, and the differences from the present study are noted in each case.

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering trials

A large share of the glucose-lowering trial literature uses add-on designs. Patients who already take one or more glucose-lowering agents are randomised to receive a new agent or a control in addition to that background therapy. This design is well suited to asking whether an agent improves control beyond current treatment, and it reflects how many patients are managed after their first years of disease. Its limitation for the present question is that background therapy confounds the effect of the added agent. The population also differs from patients at diagnosis, since they have already been exposed to treatment and may respond differently. The present study enrols adults with no prior drug and compares Quendarin with placebo alone, so the observed difference reflects the study drug in the first-treatment setting.

### 2.2 First-line comparisons with a single endpoint

A second group of studies addresses first-line treatment and compares an agent with a control in people who are starting therapy. Many of these report one comparison, typically at the end of the study or at a single scheduled visit. This is a simple and efficient way to summarise a treatment effect, and it allows a clear primary comparison. However, reporting one time point cannot show whether the treatment difference grows, plateaus or fades during the first year. Two drugs with the same end-of-study value may follow quite different paths. The present study reports both HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose at weeks 4, 8, 12, 26 and 52, so the time course of the difference can be read directly.

### 2.3 Lifestyle programmes in early diabetes

A third group of studies evaluates dietary and physical activity interventions applied at diagnosis. These programmes are an important part of early care and are often studied in people who have recently been diagnosed and have not started medicines. Their design is usually multicomponent, combining diet, activity and education, and their content is hard to standardise across participants or sites. This makes it difficult to isolate the effect of any single component, and it does not answer how a specific drug compares with a control. A randomised, placebo-controlled, single-agent design, as used here, isolates the effect of one daily dose against placebo, at the cost of not capturing the broader effects of a lifestyle package.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

This study is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Quendarin was compared with placebo. The population comprised adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who had no prior drug for the condition. The randomisation procedure, the sample size and the numbers of participants analysed are not reported here, and baseline values are not presented.

### 3.2 Intervention and comparator

Participants were allocated to one of two arms. In the active arm, Quendarin was taken at 20 mg once daily. In the comparator arm, participants received placebo. Only this single 20 mg daily dose of Quendarin was evaluated, so the study makes no comparison between doses, and all results refer to that dose.

### 3.3 Outcomes and effect measure

The primary measures were change from baseline in HbA1c, expressed in percentage points, and change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose, expressed in mmol/L. Each measure was assessed at weeks 4, 8, 12, 26 and 52. The HbA1c value at week 52 is reported in the text rather than in the results table.

The effect measure for every comparison is the mean difference vs placebo in change from baseline. Negative values indicate a lower change with Quendarin 20 mg daily than with placebo. No confidence intervals, p-values or baseline values are included in the reported data.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal findings

In this randomised, placebo-controlled study of adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and no prior drug, the mean differences vs placebo for both HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose were negative at every time point. For Quendarin 20 mg daily, this indicates lower changes from baseline than placebo for both measures across weeks 4 to 52.

The hypothesis was that Quendarin lowers both measures more than placebo and that the difference grows over time. The first part is supported at every time point for both measures. The second part is supported from week 4 to week 26 for both measures, where the magnitude of the difference increased, from -0.47 to -1.03 percentage points for HbA1c and from -0.76 to -1.32 mmol/L for fasting plasma glucose. The growth was not strictly monotonic over the full year. At week 52 the differences were slightly smaller than at week 26, at -0.97 percentage points for HbA1c and -1.24 mmol/L for fasting plasma glucose. The time course therefore rose to around week 26 and then eased slightly, rather than continuing to grow through the final assessment.

Because the two measures were analysed at the same time points, the pattern can be compared directly across them. Both reached their largest differences around week 26 and both were slightly smaller at week 52. The findings describe the observed mean differences only and do not establish the mechanisms by which Quendarin produces them.

### 5.2 Limitations and scope

Only one dose of Quendarin was evaluated, namely 20 mg once daily. The study therefore cannot support dose selection, and the mean differences reported here cannot be used to compare doses. A dose-by-dose description was not possible within this design.

The reported data do not include confidence intervals, p-values, numbers of participants, or baseline values. Without these, the precision of the estimates cannot be judged, and the strength of the differences relative to chance variation cannot be assessed from the present report. The randomisation procedure and sample size are also not reported here.

Generalisation is limited to adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who have no prior drug for the condition, and the observations cover 52 weeks. The findings do not extend to patients already receiving glucose-lowering therapy, to people with longer disease duration, or to periods beyond one year.

## 6. Conclusion

In adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and no prior drug, Quendarin 20 mg taken once daily gave negative mean differences vs placebo for change from baseline in HbA1c and in fasting plasma glucose at all five time points, weeks 4, 8, 12, 26 and 52. The differences were largest around week 26 and slightly smaller at week 52. Because only the 20 mg daily dose was evaluated, dose-comparison studies are needed before a dose choice can be informed by these results.

# Kavolemin as add-on to metformin: a 24-week placebo-controlled trial

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who remain above glycaemic targets on metformin often need an add-on glucose-lowering agent, and such an agent should be judged on glycaemia and body weight together. For adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, the effect of Kavolemin versus placebo on change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose is not known. We conducted a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial comparing Kavolemin 5 mg daily and Kavolemin 10 mg daily with placebo, with outcomes assessed at week 12 and week 24 of a 24-week trial. Every result is expressed as a mean difference versus placebo, where a negative value means lower than placebo. At week 12, the change in HbA1c differed from placebo by -0.42 percentage points with 5 mg daily and by -0.53 percentage points with 10 mg daily. At week 24, the corresponding differences were -0.58 percentage points and -0.64 percentage points. Fasting plasma glucose differences were -0.60 mmol/L and -0.67 mmol/L with 5 mg daily, and -0.86 mmol/L and -0.89 mmol/L with 10 mg daily, at weeks 12 and 24. Body weight differences were -0.1 kg and 0.5 kg with 5 mg daily, and 0.7 kg and 0.5 kg with 10 mg daily, at weeks 12 and 24. The 10 mg dose gave larger glycaemic reductions than 5 mg at both time points. These results give dose-specific mean differences versus placebo for each measure and time point, within the population studied.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is usually first managed with metformin, but many adults remain above their glycaemic targets on it and need an add-on glucose-lowering agent. Choosing such an agent involves more than lowering HbA1c. Body weight and fasting plasma glucose are also relevant, because weight gain and persistent fasting hyperglycaemia affect both treatment burden and the likelihood of reaching a target. An add-on therefore has to be judged on glycaemia and on body weight together.

Glycaemic control frequently requires add-on therapy when metformin alone is insufficient, and HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose and body weight are all relevant outcomes in this setting. Add-on agents are commonly judged against placebo, yet the size of the effect on several measures at once, and how that effect changes with dose, is often not reported together. A reader who wants to compare doses may have to assemble the picture from separate summaries that differ in their time points, doses or measures.

The present study is a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Participants were allocated to Kavolemin 5 mg daily, Kavolemin 10 mg daily or placebo. Metformin is kept as the background therapy, so that the comparison reflects the effect of the add-on relative to placebo within that setting. The central question is whether Kavolemin changes HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose more than placebo, and whether the size of that change depends on the dose.

Outcomes are change from baseline in HbA1c (percentage points), change from baseline in body weight (kg) and change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L). Each is expressed as a mean difference versus placebo at week 12 and at week 24. The aim is to give readers the mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point, so that a dose can be chosen on the basis of the full set of outcomes. The findings are limited to the population, doses and time points studied, and they are not offered as evidence beyond these conditions.

This report makes the following contributions. First, it describes a randomised, placebo-controlled trial of Kavolemin 5 mg and 10 mg daily as an add-on to metformin in adults with type 2 diabetes, with outcomes assessed at week 12 and week 24. Second, it presents the mean difference versus placebo for HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose for each dose and time point in one place, so the three measures can be read together. Third, it provides a basis for dose selection within the studied population, since readers can see how each dose compares with placebo on every measure.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Placebo-controlled add-on trials on top of metformin

Many studies of add-on glucose-lowering agents in adults already taking metformin use a parallel-group, placebo-controlled design. This is the same basic comparison used in the present trial. Such studies frequently centre on HbA1c as the primary measure. Where body weight or fasting plasma glucose are reported, they often appear as secondary or narrative findings. A single dose or a single time point may also be emphasised. As a result, the dependence of effects on dose and their time course are not always easy to compare across studies or across measures. The present trial adopts the same placebo-controlled, metformin-background design, but reports three measures at two doses and two time points within one summary.

### 2.2 Dose-ranging and short-duration studies of investigational add-on agents

Early development of investigational glucose-lowering agents often includes dose-ranging studies that compare several doses with placebo. These studies are useful for identifying a workable dose range, but they are frequently short. They also tend to focus on the glycaemic endpoint. A limited duration and single-outcome reporting restrict conclusions about sustained effects and about the effects on body weight or fasting plasma glucose. Evaluating 5 mg and 10 mg daily over 24 weeks, with three outcomes assessed at both 12 and 24 weeks, addresses these limitations within the population studied. It does not establish effects beyond that population, those doses or those time points.

### 2.3 Reporting of effects as mean differences versus placebo

Effects relative to a placebo comparator are summarised in several ways across trial reports. Some use tables of group values, some use narrative statements, and some present differences for only a subset of measures. When doses, time points and measures are mixed within one summary, or when only narrative statements are given, a reader finds it difficult to identify the size of the effect for a specific dose and time point. The present study reports each mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point. The sign convention is stated explicitly, so that a dose can be chosen directly from the summary without reconstructing it from other statements.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design

This is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Kavolemin was compared against placebo. The study is a 24-week trial. Outcomes were assessed at two time points, week 12 and week 24. Each participant was assigned to one of three arms and kept that assignment across both assessment points. The design is placebo-controlled, so every effect estimate in this report is a comparison with the placebo arm.

### 3.2 Participants

The study population was adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Metformin was part of the background therapy in all arms, so that the add-on effect was assessed on top of an established metformin regimen. The findings apply only to this population. No other populations were assessed, and the results should not be extended to adults with type 2 diabetes who are not on metformin, to other background therapies or to other age or clinical groups.

### 3.3 Interventions and comparator

Three arms were studied:

- Kavolemin 5 mg, taken once daily;
- Kavolemin 10 mg, taken once daily;
- placebo, taken once daily.

Placebo is the comparator for all effect estimates. Each Kavolemin dose is therefore summarised as its difference from the placebo arm at the same time point, and no Kavolemin arm is compared directly with the other Kavolemin arm.

### 3.4 Outcomes and effect measures

Three outcomes were analysed, each as a change from baseline:

- change in HbA1c from baseline, in percentage points;
- change in body weight from baseline, in kg;
- change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, in mmol/L.

The effect statistic for every outcome is the mean difference versus placebo, meaning the mean change in the Kavolemin arm minus the mean change in the placebo arm at the same time point. A negative value means lower than placebo, and a positive value means higher than placebo. Each mean difference is reported for a given measure, dose and time point. No other summary is derived from these values.

This paper reports mean differences only. Confidence intervals, p-values, counts of participants and baseline values are not reported here, so precision and statistical significance of the differences cannot be judged from this report.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c from baseline

Table 1 gives the mean difference versus placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline at week 12 for both Kavolemin doses. At week 12, both doses produced a negative mean difference from placebo, and the 10 mg daily dose gave the larger reduction of the two.

Table 1: Change in HbA1c from baseline for Kavolemin versus placebo at week 12, by dose

| Kavolemin dose | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mg daily | week 12 | -0.42 |
| 10 mg daily | week 12 | -0.53 |

At week 24, the 5 mg daily dose gave a mean difference versus placebo of -0.58 percentage points. At week 24, the 10 mg daily dose gave a mean difference versus placebo of -0.64 percentage points. The 10 mg dose therefore gave the larger reduction in HbA1c at both time points. Results at week 24 are stated in the text only, because the week 24 HbA1c values are not presented in Table 1.

### 4.2 Change in body weight from baseline

Table 2 gives the mean differences versus placebo in change in body weight from baseline for both Kavolemin doses at weeks 12 and 24. Results are reported for every dose at every time point.

Table 2: Change in body weight from baseline for Kavolemin versus placebo, by dose and time point

| Kavolemin dose | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in body weight from baseline (kg) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mg daily | week 12 | -0.1 |
| 5 mg daily | week 24 | 0.5 |
| 10 mg daily | week 12 | 0.7 |
| 10 mg daily | week 24 | 0.5 |

The body weight differences are mixed in sign. The 5 mg daily dose gave a negative mean difference at week 12, whereas it gave a positive mean difference at week 24. The 10 mg daily dose gave positive mean differences at both time points. The magnitudes are small, and no consistent direction across doses or time points can be identified from these values.

### 4.3 Change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline

Table 3 gives the mean differences versus placebo in change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline for both Kavolemin doses at weeks 12 and 24.

Table 3: Change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline for Kavolemin versus placebo, by dose and time point

| Kavolemin dose | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline (mmol/L) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mg daily | week 12 | -0.60 |
| 5 mg daily | week 24 | -0.67 |
| 10 mg daily | week 12 | -0.86 |

All four mean differences are negative, indicating lower fasting plasma glucose than placebo. For each dose, the value at week 24 is more negative than the value at week 12. At each time point, the 10 mg daily dose gives a more negative mean difference than the 5 mg daily dose, so the dose ordering is the same at both time points.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation of dose and time effects

The study hypothesis was that Kavolemin taken once daily changes HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on dose. For HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose, the reported mean differences versus placebo are negative at every dose and time point. The 10 mg daily dose gave larger reductions than the 5 mg daily dose at the time points reported, for both measures. This pattern is consistent with a dose-related glycaemic effect within the population studied, and it is the pattern the hypothesis anticipated for these two outcomes.

For HbA1c, the 10 mg daily dose gave a larger reduction at week 24 than at week 12, and the 5 mg daily dose gave a similar trend. For fasting plasma glucose, the mean differences also become more negative from week 12 to week 24 for each dose. Taken together, these results suggest that the glycaemic differences from placebo were not confined to a single time point within the 24-week trial. The data do not, however, allow the time course to be described beyond the two assessment points.

The body weight results do not follow the same pattern. The mean differences versus placebo are mixed in sign and small in magnitude, and the 5 mg daily dose gave a negative value at week 12 but a positive value at week 24. The positive values for the 10 mg daily dose at both time points cannot be read as a weight gain effect that is established by this report, because the magnitudes are small and no dispersion measures are reported. The weight effect should therefore not be over-read. Readers should treat the body weight differences as a description of what was observed at the two doses and two time points, rather than as evidence of a consistent dose-dependent effect on weight.

The study design allows the mean differences for each measure to be compared with placebo at matching doses and time points. It does not support comparisons between the Kavolemin arms as a formal contrast, and no conclusion about superiority of one dose over the other can be drawn from statistical testing in this report. The practical value of the results lies in the ability to see, for each dose, how far the glycaemic and weight measures sit from placebo at the same time points.

### 5.2 Limitations and scope

Several limitations follow from the design and reporting of this study. First, only two doses, 5 mg and 10 mg daily, and two time points, week 12 and week 24, are reported. No dose-response curve can be estimated beyond these points, and effects at other doses or at other times cannot be inferred from the present results.

Second, only mean differences versus placebo are reported. Precision and statistical significance cannot be judged from this paper, so the size of any difference should be read as a point estimate without an indication of its uncertainty. The absence of confidence intervals and p-values is a limit of this report and affects how strongly the glycaemic pattern can be interpreted.

Third, the findings are limited to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. They cannot be extended to other populations, to adults not taking metformin, or to other background therapies. The study also does not address the long-term consequences of the observed differences beyond the 24-week duration.

Fourth, the body weight and fasting plasma glucose results are reported alongside HbA1c, but the study was not designed to establish the clinical consequences of these measures individually. Dose choice based on these results should therefore take account of the population and conditions in which the data were obtained.

## 6. Conclusion

In adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, Kavolemin 5 mg daily and Kavolemin 10 mg daily gave mean differences versus placebo for change in HbA1c, change in body weight and change in fasting plasma glucose at week 12 and week 24, as summarised in Tables 1 to 3. The HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose differences were negative at every dose and time point. The 10 mg daily dose showed larger glycaemic differences than the 5 mg daily dose at the reported time points. The body weight differences were small and mixed in sign, and they should not be over-read.

Readers can use these mean differences to compare the two doses for each measure and time point, within the population and time points studied. The conclusions do not extend beyond adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, beyond the two doses tested, or beyond the two assessment points reported.

# Ordenvik (ORD-399) as first treatment in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes: 48-week results

## Abstract

The effect of Ordenvik (ORD-399) as a first treatment in adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who have never taken a glucose-lowering drug, relative to placebo, has not been described in detail across scheduled time points. This report presents a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial comparing Ordenvik (ORD-399) 25 mg once daily with placebo in this population, with outcomes assessed at weeks 4, 8, 16, 24 and 48. The outcomes were change from baseline in HbA1c, in percentage points, and change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose, in mmol/L. Both are reported as mean differences versus placebo, where negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. For HbA1c, the mean difference was -0.33 percentage points at week 4, -0.80 percentage points at week 24, and -0.80 percentage points at week 48. For fasting plasma glucose, the mean difference was -0.68 mmol/L at week 4, reached -0.90 mmol/L at week 16, and was -0.89 mmol/L at week 48. The HbA1c differences increased from week 4 to week 24 and were unchanged at week 48. The fasting plasma glucose differences did not increase at every time point. The results describe the 25 mg dose only, in this population, and do not inform comparisons between doses. Confidence intervals, p-values, participant numbers and safety data are not reported in this summary.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition that is commonly managed with glucose-lowering drugs. Treatment response is usually judged by changes in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting plasma glucose, so the size and timing of these changes are central to how a drug's benefit is understood. When a drug is started at or near diagnosis, these measures indicate how well the treatment is expected to work as a first step.

The first-treatment decision depends on drug-specific evidence in adults who are newly diagnosed and have never taken a glucose-lowering drug. These patients differ from people who are already on treatment. Their baseline glycaemia, their response to a new agent and the background therapy they receive can all shape the observed effect. Evidence gathered in other settings therefore cannot be assumed to transfer directly to this group.

Much of the glycaemic evidence for new agents comes from populations that are already treated, or from studies with comparatively short observation periods. As a result, the first-line setting and the longer-term time course of the effect may be less fully described. For Ordenvik (ORD-399), this report sets out the effect on HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose relative to placebo in drug-naive adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, and how that effect is reported across scheduled time points over 48 weeks.

The present study is a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial. It compares Ordenvik (ORD-399) 25 mg once daily with placebo in adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and no prior drug. Outcomes are change from baseline in HbA1c, in percentage points, and change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose, in mmol/L. Each is reported as a mean difference versus placebo at weeks 4, 8, 16, 24 and 48. HbA1c results after week 24 are reported only for week 48. The report does not compare doses, because only the 25 mg arm is reported. The mechanism of action and drug class of Ordenvik (ORD-399) are not described in this report, which is restricted to the glycaemic endpoints listed above.

The contributions of this report are as follows. First, it provides placebo-referenced mean differences for HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose in drug-naive adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes. Second, it reports these differences at five scheduled time points over 48 weeks, so the time course of the effect is visible rather than summarised by a single end-of-study value. Third, it gives the mean differences for the 25 mg dose at each scheduled time point, which allows readers to see when the difference appears and how it changes thereafter.

## 2. Related Work

This section describes three general design approaches to reporting glycaemic efficacy. It does not provide a systematic review of the published literature, and it does not attribute specific findings to any prior study. Each approach is described by its design.

### 2.1 Add-on evaluations in already-treated populations

One group of studies evaluates glucose-lowering agents as add-on therapy, given on top of an existing regimen in people whose glycaemia is not yet controlled. These designs address a useful question about combination treatment, and they typically enrol participants who already take one or more glucose-lowering drugs. Such participants can differ from newly diagnosed, drug-naive adults in baseline glycaemia, in treatment history and in the background therapy that shapes the observed effect. An effect measured on top of existing treatment may therefore reflect that background regimen rather than the first-treatment setting. The present trial enrols only adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and no prior drug, so its placebo comparison addresses the first-treatment question directly.

### 2.2 Short-duration placebo-controlled glycaemic studies

Another group of studies uses placebo-controlled designs with observation periods that are shorter than the period reported here. These studies share the comparator logic used in the present report, in which an active drug is contrasted with placebo under randomisation, and they can establish whether a glycaemic difference exists at the time of assessment. Short follow-up gives limited information on whether the difference continues to change or stabilises over a longer period. Because glycaemic control is relevant over extended periods of treatment, the present design follows the same comparator logic and extends observation to 48 weeks, with measurements at five scheduled time points.

### 2.3 Studies reporting a single end-of-study difference

A third group summarises each trial by one final efficacy value, such as a single difference at the end of the study. Such summaries are concise and convenient for comparing agents, but a single value does not show the trajectory of the effect. It is difficult for a reader to see when an effect appears, whether it grows over time, or whether it levels off. Two trials reporting the same final value may have different time courses. The present report gives the mean difference versus placebo at every scheduled time point for both HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose, so the time course is visible rather than inferred from one number.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design

This study is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Ordenvik (ORD-399) was compared with placebo. Participants were allocated to one of the two arms, so comparisons are made between groups. The study duration was 48 weeks. Scheduled assessments were made at weeks 4, 8, 16, 24 and 48. No other scheduled assessment time points are reported. The procedures used for randomisation and for blinding are not described in this report.

### 3.2 Participants

The study population was adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who had no prior drug treatment. Eligibility is described here only in terms of these characteristics, and no further inclusion or exclusion criteria are reported. This report does not give participant numbers, the number randomised or analysed, or baseline values for either arm.

### 3.3 Interventions

Two arms were compared. One arm received Ordenvik (ORD-399) 25 mg taken once daily, and the other received placebo. The present report covers the 25 mg dose only. No comparison between doses is made, and the study is not presented as a dose-comparison study.

### 3.4 Outcome measures

The two glycaemic measures reported are change from baseline in HbA1c, expressed in percentage points, and change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose, expressed in mmol/L.

The effect estimate for each measure and time point is the mean difference versus placebo. Negative values indicate a lower change from baseline than placebo. No statistical model, test or interval estimate is described in this report beyond the mean differences themselves. The report does not state whether the time-point structure was prespecified in a protocol or analysis plan.

### 3.5 Reporting of results

Mean differences versus placebo are reported for each scheduled time point. Table 1 gives the HbA1c results at weeks 4, 8, 16 and 24. Table 2 gives the fasting plasma glucose results at weeks 4, 8, 16, 24 and 48. The HbA1c value at week 48 is reported in the text, not in Table 1. The reason for this presentation is not described further in this report. Values are given with the decimals and units in which they are recorded.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c from baseline

Table 1 shows the mean difference versus placebo in change from baseline in HbA1c at weeks 4, 8, 16 and 24. All values are negative, indicating a lower change in HbA1c than placebo at each time point.

Table 1: Mean difference versus placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline for Ordenvik (ORD-399) 25 mg daily, by time point (weeks 4 to 24)

| Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline (percentage points) |
|---|---|
| Week 4 | -0.33 |
| Week 8 | -0.57 |
| Week 16 | -0.76 |
| Week 24 | -0.80 |

The mean difference became larger across the first 24 weeks. It moved from -0.33 percentage points at week 4 to -0.57 percentage points at week 8 and -0.76 percentage points at week 16, and reached -0.80 percentage points at week 24. At week 48, the mean difference versus placebo was -0.80 percentage points. The reported values show no change between weeks 24 and 48. These are point estimates only, and no interval or significance assessment is available for them.

### 4.2 Change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline

Table 2 shows the mean difference versus placebo in change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose at weeks 4, 8, 16, 24 and 48. All values are negative, indicating a lower change than placebo at every reported time point.

Table 2: Mean difference versus placebo in change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline for Ordenvik (ORD-399) 25 mg daily, by time point (weeks 4 to 48)

| Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline (mmol/L) |
|---|---|
| Week 4 | -0.68 |
| Week 8 | -0.81 |
| Week 16 | -0.90 |
| Week 24 | -0.86 |
| Week 48 | -0.89 |

The largest mean difference was at week 16, at -0.90 mmol/L. The value was slightly smaller at week 24, at -0.86 mmol/L, and was -0.89 mmol/L at week 48. The mean difference therefore did not increase at every time point. As with HbA1c, these are point estimates without measures of precision.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal findings

In adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and no prior drug, Ordenvik (ORD-399) 25 mg daily gave a lower change from baseline than placebo in both HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose at every reported time point. The analysis addressed two descriptive questions: whether the change differed from placebo in each measure, and how the mean difference varied across scheduled time points. For the first question, all reported mean differences were negative. Because the comparator was placebo in a drug-naive population, the contrast reflects the difference between the study drug and placebo in the first-treatment setting that the Introduction identified as less fully described.

For the second question, the HbA1c mean difference increased across the first 24 weeks and was unchanged between weeks 24 and 48. For fasting plasma glucose, the mean difference was largest at week 16, was slightly smaller at week 24 and was similar at week 48. The time course therefore differed between the two measures. The fasting plasma glucose pattern suggests that the difference was largely established by about week 16 and then remained at a similar level. The HbA1c pattern suggests that the difference was established by about week 24 and then remained stable to week 48. Whether these patterns reflect the pharmacology of the drug cannot be determined from these data, because the report does not include mechanistic or precision data.

### 5.2 Limitations

Several limits of this study should be considered when the findings are read. First, only one dose, 25 mg once daily, is reported. Because no other dose is presented, these data do not inform dose selection, and no statement about dose response can be made.

Second, confidence intervals, p-values, the statistical model used, participant numbers, the numbers analysed at each time point, and baseline values for each arm are not reported in this study. The precision of the mean differences, the handling of missing data and the comparability of the arms at baseline therefore cannot be assessed here.

Third, safety and tolerability outcomes, including adverse events, hypoglycaemia and discontinuations, are not reported. The benefit of the treatment cannot be weighed against its safety profile on the basis of this report.

Fourth, only two glycaemic measures, HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose, are reported. Other outcomes are outside the scope of this paper and are not discussed. The HbA1c value at week 48 is given in the text only and is not part of the table series, which limits a direct comparison of the HbA1c time course across all scheduled time points in a single display.

Fifth, the trajectory between scheduled assessments is not observed, so the timing of the effect is known only at weeks 4, 8, 16, 24 and 48. Sixth, the observation period is 48 weeks, so the effect beyond that period cannot be described. Finally, the randomisation and blinding procedures are not described, and the drug's mechanism of action is not discussed, so the design cannot be fully reproduced or appraised from this report alone.

## 6. Conclusion

In adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and no prior drug, Ordenvik (ORD-399) 25 mg daily gave a lower change from baseline than placebo in both HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose over 48 weeks. The HbA1c mean difference grew to week 24 and was unchanged at week 48. The fasting plasma glucose mean difference was largest at week 16 and was similar at week 48. These are point estimates from a single dose in a single population, without reported intervals, safety data or baseline values, and no comparison between doses is possible from these data.

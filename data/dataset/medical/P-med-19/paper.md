# Ordenvik with metformin: a 52-week multicentre trial

## Abstract

Glycaemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin is often improved by adding a second agent. The effect of Ordenvik on change in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and on change in body weight, relative to placebo, has not been established in this population. We report a multicentre, randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial lasting 52 weeks, in which adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin received Ordenvik 25 mg once daily or placebo. Change from baseline in HbA1c (percentage points) and change from baseline in body weight (kg) were assessed at week 24 and week 52 as co-primary measures. Results are expressed as mean differences versus placebo, where negative values indicate lower values than placebo. At week 24, the mean difference in HbA1c was -0.38 percentage points, and at week 52 it was -0.40 percentage points. The mean difference in body weight was -0.5 kg at week 24 and -0.5 kg at week 52. The differences are small. Because only one dose, 25 mg, was studied, the results describe that dose alone. Measures of precision, inferential statistics and safety are not reported in this paper, and the findings should not be generalised beyond adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a progressive condition, and metformin is commonly the first pharmacological treatment used to manage it. Many patients, however, do not reach their glycaemic targets on metformin alone and need an additional glucose-lowering agent. Choosing that agent is a routine clinical decision made under real constraints. The chosen therapy must lower glycaemia enough to matter, it must be acceptable for a patient whose body weight may already be a concern, and its effect must be sustained over the course of treatment rather than only in the short term.

Clinicians therefore need evidence on more than one outcome at a time. Change in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is the primary measure of glycaemic control, while body weight is the second measure that matters to many patients, because weight change can offset or add to the benefits and burdens of a treatment. These two outcomes are often reported at a single time point, or one is given prominence while the other is treated as secondary. Such reporting makes it difficult to judge whether an effect is stable, increases, or fades with longer exposure, and it leaves the reader to infer the size of the difference that matters for a given decision.

For adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, the effect of Ordenvik 25 mg taken once daily on change in HbA1c and change in body weight, relative to placebo, has not been established in this trial population. It is also not known whether these effects change between week 24 and week 52. The working hypothesis is that Ordenvik changes both measures more than placebo and that the change increases over time. Testing this requires a comparison against a concurrent placebo group, with both measures read at more than one time point.

This study addresses that gap with a multicentre, randomised, placebo-controlled trial lasting 52 weeks. Adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin were randomised in parallel to Ordenvik 25 mg once daily or to placebo. Change from baseline in HbA1c and in body weight were treated as co-primary measures and were assessed at week 24 and week 52. The results are expressed as mean differences versus placebo for each measure and time point, so the size and the time course of the effect can be read from the same comparison. The scope is deliberately narrow: the trial evaluates one dose of Ordenvik against placebo, and it does not compare Ordenvik with other add-on agents or with other doses. The report is also limited in what it contains. It presents mean differences only, and it does not include the precision, safety or analysis details that a complete trial report would normally provide.

The contribution of this study is a set of mean differences versus placebo for change in HbA1c and change in body weight, for Ordenvik 25 mg once daily as an add-on to metformin, at week 24 and week 52. These estimates allow readers to judge both the magnitude and the time course of the effect, so that they can weigh this dose against their own glycaemic and weight goals, within the limits set out in this paper.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work on add-on therapy for metformin-treated type 2 diabetes can be grouped by study design. Two clusters bear most directly on the present trial: short placebo-controlled add-on studies, and longer studies that record both glycaemic and weight outcomes.

### 2.1 Short placebo-controlled add-on trials

Earlier placebo-controlled studies of add-on glucose-lowering agents in adults whose diabetes is not adequately controlled on metformin share a common structure. Patients are randomised to the investigational agent or to placebo, background metformin is continued, and the glycaemic effect is read at a single primary time point, typically a few months after treatment begins. The strength of this approach is clarity of comparison. Placebo control separates the contribution of the added agent from background therapy and from the natural drift in glycaemia that occurs over time. Its main limitation, for the present purpose, is that one primary time point says little about whether the difference from placebo is maintained, increases or diminishes with longer exposure. Body weight, where it is recorded, is often a secondary observation in these designs. The present trial keeps the placebo-controlled structure but reads both HbA1c and body weight at week 24 and week 52, so the time course of the difference from placebo can be read directly from the same comparison.

### 2.2 Longer trials reporting glycaemic and weight outcomes

A second group of studies runs for longer and records both glycaemic control and body weight as part of routine follow-up. Extended-duration designs are better placed to show whether an effect persists, because they include repeated measurements over a year or more and capture the weight trajectory that often accompanies glucose-lowering treatment. In many such reports, however, weight change is a secondary outcome. It may be summarised across several agents or doses, or presented as change from baseline within each arm rather than as a contrast against placebo. This makes it harder for a reader to see, for one specific dose, how much of the weight change is attributable to the treatment and how that attribution changes over time. The present design responds to this by pairing HbA1c and body weight as co-primary measures for a single defined dose against placebo, and by reporting the mean difference for each measure at each time point. The comparison is narrow, which limits what can be concluded about other doses or agents, but it makes the estimates directly interpretable for the dose studied.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

This was a multicentre, randomised controlled trial with a parallel-group, placebo-controlled design and a treatment period of 52 weeks. The population consisted of adults with type 2 diabetes who were on metformin. Metformin was therefore the background glucose-lowering therapy for the population studied, and the question addressed was the effect of Ordenvik relative to placebo in this setting.

Participants were randomised in parallel to one of two arms. The active arm received Ordenvik 25 mg once daily. The comparator arm received placebo. Only the 25 mg once-daily dose of Ordenvik is evaluated in the present comparison, so the design does not allow dose-response conclusions across other doses.

This report does not describe the sample size of each arm, the randomisation method, the blinding procedure, the background metformin dose, the use of any rescue medication, or adherence and discontinuation. These elements are therefore outside the scope of the present paper, and the internal validity of the comparison is discussed only as far as the reported design allows.

### 3.2 Outcomes and time points

The two primary measures were change from baseline in HbA1c, expressed in percentage points, and change from baseline in body weight, expressed in kilograms. Both measures were assessed at week 24 and at week 52 of the trial. Each measure was summarised as a mean difference versus placebo. Under this definition, a negative value indicates a lower value than placebo, and a positive value would indicate a higher value than placebo. Because the two measures are expressed on different scales, each is interpreted within its own unit and no cross-measure comparison of magnitude is made. The two measures are referred to as co-primary because both were pre-specified as outcomes of interest at both time points.

### 3.3 Reporting

Every value is reported as a mean difference versus placebo, written with the decimals and units in which it is expressed. Only mean differences are reported in this paper. Confidence intervals, p-values, participant counts and baseline values are not reported here. The statistical model, the analysis population and the handling of missing data are not described in this report, and no multiplicity adjustment across the two co-primary measures and two time points is reported. The precision of each estimate and the statistical strength of each difference therefore cannot be assessed from the present report.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c

Table 1 gives the mean difference versus placebo in change from baseline in HbA1c at week 24 for Ordenvik 25 mg once daily. At week 24, the mean difference was -0.38 percentage points. The sign of both values is negative, indicating that the change in HbA1c on Ordenvik 25 mg was lower than on placebo at each time point. The numerical difference is slightly larger at week 52 than at week 24. No test of this difference is reported.

### 4.3 Summary across doses and time points

The results are organised by dose and by time point. In this paper, the only dose reported is 25 mg once daily, and for this arm both time points are reported for both measures. No statements are made about other doses of Ordenvik, and no dose comparison is possible from the present material. Across both measures, the pattern at 25 mg daily is a negative mean difference versus placebo at each time point. The HbA1c difference is numerically slightly larger at week 52, and the body weight difference is unchanged between the two time points. Neither pattern is tested in this report.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation of the glycaemic and weight effects

At 25 mg once daily, Ordenvik was associated with a mean difference versus placebo in HbA1c of -0.38 percentage points at week 24 and -0.40 percentage points at week 52. For body weight, the mean difference versus placebo was -0.5 kg at both time points. Both differences are small in absolute size. They indicate that, in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, the change in each measure on Ordenvik 25 mg was lower than on placebo, which is the direction predicted by the working hypothesis.

The hypothesis also states that the change increases over time. For HbA1c, the mean difference is numerically slightly more negative at week 52 than at week 24. This descriptive pattern is not evidence of a time-dependent effect. Without an interval, a test or an estimate of the time-by-treatment interaction, the two estimates cannot be distinguished from a stable effect, and the data do not establish growth. For body weight, the mean difference is the same at both time points, so no change over time is observed for this measure. The hypothesis is therefore not confirmed by these data. The time-trend component in particular remains untested.

Taken together, the results indicate that a negative difference from placebo is present for both measures at the two time points reported, while its size is small. A reader weighing this dose against glycaemic and weight goals would be presented with small weight differences that are the same at both time points and a small HbA1c difference that is numerically slightly larger at the later time point. This report applies no threshold for clinical importance, performs no responder analysis and makes no comparison with minimal clinically important differences. Whether such differences are clinically meaningful for a given patient is therefore a judgement that the present data do not settle.

### 5.2 Limitations

Several limits of the present study follow from its design. First, only one dose, 25 mg once daily, is reported, so the results cannot describe how the effect varies with dose. Second, only two time points, week 24 and week 52, are reported, so the time course between and beyond these points is not described. Third, the results are presented as mean differences versus placebo only. Dispersion measures are not reported here, and the precision of each estimate, and the uncertainty around the observed time trend, cannot be judged from this paper. Fourth, the population is restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, so the findings do not extend to other populations, to patients not taking metformin, or to other background therapies. Fifth, no safety or tolerability outcomes are reported, including adverse events, hypoglycaemia, gastrointestinal effects, serious events and discontinuations. The benefit-risk balance of Ordenvik 25 mg therefore cannot be assessed from these data. Sixth, the mechanism and class of Ordenvik and its prior clinical evidence are outside the scope of this report. Finally, the trial compares Ordenvik only with placebo; no comparison with other add-on agents is made, and the relative standing of Ordenvik among treatment options cannot be inferred from these data.

## 6. Conclusion

In adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, Ordenvik 25 mg once daily was associated with mean differences versus placebo of -0.38 percentage points in change from baseline in HbA1c at week 24 and -0.40 percentage points at week 52. For change from baseline in body weight, the mean difference versus placebo was -0.5 kg at week 24 and -0.5 kg at week 52. The reported differences are small. The HbA1c difference is numerically slightly larger at week 52, and the body weight difference is unchanged. These results give readers the dose-specific and time-specific mean differences for this dose and this population. They do not establish a time-dependent effect, clinical meaningfulness, or safety, and they do not support conclusions about other doses, other populations, or other add-on agents.

# Quendarin in older adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin

## Abstract

Glycaemic control in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin often requires an add-on agent, yet the effect of Quendarin on HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose relative to placebo is not known in this population, and it is not known whether the 25 mg and 50 mg daily doses differ in these effects. We report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin, comparing Quendarin 25 mg daily, Quendarin 50 mg daily and placebo. Each measure, namely change from baseline in HbA1c (percentage points), body weight (kg) and fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L), was assessed at week 24 and expressed as the mean difference from placebo. At week 24, HbA1c changed by -0.68 percentage points at 25 mg and -0.86 percentage points at 50 mg relative to placebo. Body weight changed by -1.2 kg at 25 mg and -0.9 kg at 50 mg, and fasting plasma glucose changed by -0.98 mmol/L at 25 mg and -1.11 mmol/L at 50 mg. The glycaemic differences were more negative at 50 mg daily, whereas the weight difference was less negative at 50 mg daily, so the dose-specific mean differences allow readers to choose a dose according to their priority. These conclusions are limited to a single time point, two doses and the studied population.

## 1. Introduction

Glycaemic control in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes who take metformin often depends on adding a second agent. Whether such an add-on is suitable, however, depends on more than whether it lowers glucose. It also depends on whether the reduction comes without unwanted changes in body weight, because clinicians weigh glucose-lowering benefit against weight effects when judging whether a regimen is sustainable for an older patient. Older adults may differ from younger patients in comorbidity, body weight and tolerance, so effects estimated in other populations cannot be assumed to transfer directly. Type 2 diabetes in this age group on metformin therefore needs add-on options whose effects are quantified within the group itself, and each candidate agent must be compared against placebo there.

Three outcomes need to be read together. Change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points, reflects glycaemic control over the longer term. Change in body weight from baseline, in kilograms, captures an outcome that matters for patients who are already managing their weight or other cardiovascular risks. Change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, in mmol/L, gives a direct view of the fasting glycaemic response. Judging each measure against placebo allows any difference to be attributed to the agent rather than to the passage of time or to background care, so that the three outcomes can be interpreted on a common reference.

The dose question remains open. The working hypothesis is that once-daily Quendarin changes each of these measures more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on the dose. Whether the 25 mg and 50 mg daily doses differ in their effects on HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose at week 24 is not known for this population. Readers choosing between doses therefore need dose-specific mean differences from placebo rather than a single pooled effect. Only with such figures can they judge whether the higher dose adds glycaemic benefit and how it changes the balance between glucose lowering and weight.

To address this gap, we report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Participants were allocated to Quendarin 25 mg daily, Quendarin 50 mg daily or placebo, and each measure was assessed at week 24. For every measure, dose and time point, the result is expressed as the mean difference from placebo, with negative values indicating a lower value than placebo. The scope of the findings is deliberately narrow: they apply only to adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin, to the two doses studied and to the week 24 time point. Longer durations, other doses and other populations lie outside the scope of this study.

The contribution of this study is the mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point, presented so that readers can choose a dose. Specifically, it provides these estimates for change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose with Quendarin at 25 mg and 50 mg daily, in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin, at week 24. Presenting the measures side by side for both doses allows a direct reading of the trade-offs that a dose choice involves.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to this study falls into three approach clusters, each described here by its design rather than by any specific finding. For each cluster we note the limitation that motivates the present design and how the trial addresses it.

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering trials in broad adult populations

A large body of placebo-controlled add-on studies enrols adults with type 2 diabetes across a wide age range. Such designs are well suited to establishing that an agent lowers glucose in general, and they provide a broad picture of expected effects. However, older adults on metformin are often pooled with younger patients, so age-specific estimates of glycaemic and weight effects are difficult to extract from the combined results. Effects that appear modest or favourable in a mixed population may not reflect the experience of the older group. The present trial addresses this by restricting enrolment to adults aged 65 years or older on metformin and by reporting effects for this population only.

### 2.2 Dose-comparison designs for a single add-on agent

Parallel-group studies that compare two or more fixed doses of one add-on agent against placebo are a common way to explore how effect size changes with dose. These designs are useful for dose-response questions, but they often emphasise a single glycaemic endpoint as the primary focus. Body weight and fasting plasma glucose may therefore be under-characterised, and dose-specific differences on these measures may not be presented side by side. As a result, a reader choosing a dose may have incomplete information about the trade-offs between doses. The present trial reports HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose at both 25 mg and 50 mg daily against the same placebo arm and the same time point.

### 2.3 Multi-endpoint metabolic outcome assessments

Some studies assess several metabolic endpoints of glucose-lowering agents together, so that glycaemic, weight and fasting glucose effects can be read in one analysis. This approach gives a more complete picture of an agent's profile. When several endpoints are assessed, however, the time points and reference groups can differ between endpoints, which complicates direct comparison and makes dose selection harder. Results reported at different times or against different comparators cannot be read on a common scale. The present trial uses one time point (week 24), one reference group (placebo) and one effect statistic (mean difference versus placebo) for all three measures, so that the endpoints can be compared directly.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and participants

This study is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Quendarin is compared against placebo. The population consists of adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Each participant was allocated to one of the three study arms and followed to the week 24 time point, at which all outcome measures were assessed. Eligibility criteria, the randomisation procedure, blinding arrangements and sample sizes are not reported in this paper, and the results should be read with this in mind.

### 3.2 Interventions

Three arms were studied. Participants in the first arm received Quendarin 25 mg taken once daily, participants in the second arm received Quendarin 50 mg taken once daily, and participants in the third arm received placebo. Placebo is the only comparator. The working hypothesis compares each Quendarin dose with placebo, and it states that the size of the change depends on the dose. Accordingly, the analysis is organised around the contrast between each Quendarin dose and the placebo arm, and no contrast between the two Quendarin doses is used as the primary reference.

### 3.3 Outcome measures and effect statistic

Three outcome measures were assessed, each defined as change from baseline: HbA1c in percentage points, body weight in kilograms, and fasting plasma glucose in mmol/L. All three measures were assessed at one time point, week 24. The effect statistic for every measure is the mean difference versus placebo, where negative values indicate a lower value than placebo and positive values would indicate a higher value than placebo. Values are reported exactly as they were obtained, with their units and decimals retained. Results are presented as mean differences only.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Glycaemic control (HbA1c)

At week 24, Quendarin 25 mg daily changed HbA1c from baseline by -0.68 percentage points relative to placebo. Quendarin 50 mg daily changed HbA1c from baseline by -0.86 percentage points relative to placebo. Both values are mean differences versus placebo, and both are negative, indicating a lower HbA1c change than placebo at the same time point. Results are reported for every dose of Quendarin at every time point in this trial, so the two doses can be read against the same placebo reference.

### 4.2 Body weight

Table 1 presents the change in body weight from baseline for each Quendarin dose relative to placebo at week 24. At 25 mg daily, the mean difference from placebo was -1.2 kg, and at 50 mg daily it was -0.9 kg. In both rows the value is negative, indicating a lower body weight change than placebo. The 25 mg value is more negative than the 50 mg value, so the weight reduction relative to placebo is larger at the lower dose in this comparison.

Table 1: Change in body weight from baseline for Quendarin versus placebo at week 24, by dose (mean difference vs placebo)

| Quendarin dose (once daily) | Time point | Change in body weight vs placebo (kg, mean difference) |
|---|---|---|
| 25 mg daily | week 24 | -1.2 |
| 50 mg daily | week 24 | -0.9 |

### 4.3 Fasting plasma glucose

Table 2 presents the change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline for each Quendarin dose relative to placebo at week 24. At 25 mg daily, the mean difference from placebo was -0.98 mmol/L, and at 50 mg daily it was -1.11 mmol/L. Both values are negative. The 50 mg value is more negative than the 25 mg value, so the fasting glucose difference from placebo is larger at the higher dose.

Table 2: Change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline for Quendarin versus placebo at week 24, by dose (mean difference vs placebo)

| Quendarin dose (once daily) | Time point | Change in fasting plasma glucose vs placebo (mmol/L, mean difference) |

Taken together, the three measures do not move in the same direction with dose. HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose are more negative at 50 mg daily, while body weight is less negative at 50 mg daily than at 25 mg daily.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose-related patterns

The glycaemic results are in the direction predicted by the dose-dependence component of the working hypothesis. The HbA1c mean difference from placebo is more negative at 50 mg daily (-0.86 percentage points) than at 25 mg daily (-0.68 percentage points). The fasting plasma glucose mean difference from placebo follows the same pattern, being more negative at 50 mg daily (-1.11 mmol/L) than at 25 mg daily (-0.98 mmol/L), as shown in Table 2. Both glycaemic measures therefore point toward a larger effect at the higher dose.

The body weight result does not follow this pattern. The mean difference from placebo is less negative at 50 mg daily (-0.9 kg) than at 25 mg daily (-1.2 kg), as shown in Table 1. The dose direction is thus not uniform across the three measures. The hypothesis that the size of the change depends on the dose is supported in direction for the glycaemic measures, but the weight measure moves in the opposite direction, so a single dose-response statement cannot describe all three outcomes. The comparisons in this section rely only on the reported values, and no additional differences between doses have been derived.

### 5.2 Implications for dose selection

The dose-specific mean differences allow readers to choose a dose according to the outcome that they prioritise. A reader whose main concern is glycaemic control would find the larger HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose differences at 50 mg daily relevant to that choice. A reader whose main concern is body weight would find the larger weight difference at 25 mg daily relevant in the opposite direction. Because both doses are reported against the same placebo arm and the same time point, the trade-off between these outcomes can be read directly from the paired values.

The study does not define a single preferred dose. The choice between 25 mg and 50 mg daily depends on how the reader weighs the three measures against one another, and the present data do not provide a method for combining them into one decision criterion. This is consistent with the aim of the trial, which was to supply the mean differences needed for a dose choice rather than to recommend one dose over another.

### 5.3 Limitations

Several limitations follow from the design of the study. First, the trial assesses all outcomes at a single time point, week 24. It makes no statements about effects at other durations, and the persistence or development of these differences over longer periods cannot be judged from these data. Second, only two doses of Quendarin were studied, 25 mg and 50 mg daily. Nothing can be said about doses between these levels or beyond them, and the shape of the dose-response relationship is therefore not characterised.

Third, only mean differences versus placebo are reported in this paper. Uncertainty around these estimates should be considered when interpreting them, and the magnitude of the differences should not be read as a precise quantification of benefit. Fourth, the study population is restricted to adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin. The results should not be generalised to younger adults, to patients receiving other background therapies or to other populations with type 2 diabetes. These constraints define the scope within which the reported differences apply.

## 6. Conclusion

This study reports a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin, comparing Quendarin 25 mg daily, Quendarin 50 mg daily and placebo. Each outcome was expressed as the mean difference versus placebo at week 24. The dose-specific values for body weight and fasting plasma glucose are given in Tables 1 and 2, and the HbA1c values are reported in the Results text. Across the three measures, the glycaemic differences were more negative at 50 mg daily, whereas the body weight difference was less negative at 50 mg daily.

These dose-specific differences support dose choice in this population, because readers can weigh glycaemic benefit against weight change for each dose using the same placebo reference and time point. The evidence is limited to a single time point, two doses and adults aged 65 years or older with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Longer-term effects and evidence for broader populations are not provided by this trial and remain to be established by other work.

# Quendarin with metformin in type 2 diabetes: a multicentre 52-week trial

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin often need an additional agent to reach their glycaemic target, and the effect of such an agent on body weight is a second concern in this group. This multicentre, randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial compared Quendarin 10 mg and 20 mg once daily with placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes taking metformin. The outcomes were the change from baseline in HbA1c, in percentage points, and the change from baseline in body weight, in kilograms, at week 24 and at week 52. For each measure, dose and time point, the report gives the mean difference versus placebo, in which negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. At week 24, the mean differences in HbA1c were -0.34 percentage points at 10 mg and -0.42 percentage points at 20 mg. At week 52, they were -0.29 percentage points at 10 mg and -0.44 percentage points at 20 mg. Mean differences in body weight versus placebo ranged from -0.5 kg to -0.8 kg across doses and time points. Confidence intervals, p-values, sample sizes and safety data are not reported, so the estimates are descriptive. No statistical significance, dose-dependence or clinical importance is claimed. The report sets out point estimates for one trial, from which the direction and ordering of the differences can be read.

## 1. Introduction

Adults with type 2 diabetes are often first treated with metformin, but many do not reach their glycaemic target on this agent alone and need an additional glucose-lowering treatment. Body weight is a second clinical concern in this group, so an agent added to metformin is relevant to both glycaemic control and weight. An add-on therapy is therefore considered here on both measures.

Add-on agents are often evaluated against placebo at a single dose or at a single time point. Such designs show whether an agent differs from placebo under one set of conditions, but they limit what can be read about dose and duration. A single dose gives no direct comparison between dose levels, and a single time point gives no indication of whether the difference from placebo changes as treatment continues. Both kinds of information are relevant to dose selection for an individual patient.

This multicentre, randomised, placebo-controlled trial compares Quendarin at 10 mg and 20 mg once daily with placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes who are taking metformin. The trial measures the change from baseline in HbA1c, in percentage points, and the change from baseline in body weight, in kilograms, at week 24 and at week 52. Both measures are therefore followed over two time points and two doses within a single design.

For each measure, dose and time point, the report gives the mean difference versus placebo, so that doses and durations can be compared directly. The working hypothesis was that Quendarin once daily changes both HbA1c and body weight relative to placebo, and that the size of this change varies with dose. This report does not test that hypothesis. It presents the estimates from which the direction and ordering of the differences can be read. The scope is deliberately narrow: one patient population, two outcome measures, two fixed doses and two time points. The report does not address other populations, other outcomes or other dosing regimens.

The contributions of this report are as follows. First, it gives the mean difference from placebo in HbA1c and in body weight for Quendarin 10 mg and 20 mg once daily in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Second, it reports each of these differences at weeks 24 and 52, so that the pattern across dose and duration can be seen in one place. Third, it states clearly which information needed for a formal interpretation is not reported, so that the estimates are not read beyond what they support.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to this study can be grouped into three clusters of approach. Each differs in how the add-on agent is evaluated. The description below is limited to the general design of these approaches. It does not attribute particular findings to individual studies, and it does not compare the present estimates with published trial results.

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering therapy to metformin

A large body of work evaluates glucose-lowering agents in combination with metformin. These studies are typically randomised and placebo-controlled, with metformin continued as background therapy in all arms, and they commonly use the change in HbA1c as the primary measure of benefit. This design isolates the contribution of the added agent from that of metformin, which makes it well suited to judging whether an add-on differs from placebo. However, such trials often focus on a single glycaemic endpoint and a single dose. When only one dose and one time point are reported, it is harder to judge how the effect depends on dose or how it evolves over the course of treatment. The present trial keeps the metformin-background, placebo-controlled comparison, and it reports HbA1c at two time points and for two doses.

### 2.2 Weight outcomes of add-on agents in type 2 diabetes

A second group of studies assesses body weight change alongside glycaemic control when an agent is added to background therapy. Because many people with type 2 diabetes are overweight or obese, weight is an important consideration when comparing add-on options. In many of these reports, however, weight is presented as a secondary or safety measure. Estimates are often given for a single dose, or summarised without a clear comparison against placebo at each time point. This limits the ability of readers to judge how weight change depends on dose or on treatment duration. In the present report, body weight change from baseline is reported alongside HbA1c as an outcome, as a mean difference versus placebo at each dose and time point. No primary status is assigned to either outcome.

### 2.3 Dose-response and duration in add-on comparisons

A third cluster consists of comparisons of two or more doses of an add-on agent against placebo, evaluated at more than one time point. These designs are a direct way to examine dose-response and duration, because they allow the effect at one dose to be set against the effect at another. Dose-response, however, is often inferred from a small number of arms, and differences between doses can be small relative to the uncertainty of the estimates. Such comparisons are difficult to interpret when measures of precision are absent. The present report uses two fixed doses and two time points. It reports each mean difference from placebo so that dose and duration patterns can be seen directly, and it gives point estimates only.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Design and participants

The study was a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial of Quendarin against placebo. Participants were adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin, and the trial was conducted in a multicentre setting. Three arms were included: Quendarin 10 mg taken once daily, Quendarin 20 mg taken once daily, and placebo. Metformin was part of the background therapy of the population, and the comparison of interest was the difference between each Quendarin dose and placebo in this population. The background metformin dose and duration are not reported here. The mechanism of action, drug class and formulation of Quendarin, and the rationale for the chosen doses, are outside the scope of this report.

The number of participants randomised and analysed in each arm, the allocation procedure, the blinding arrangements, the analysis population and the handling of missing data and discontinuations are not reported in this paper. Trial registration details are likewise not included.

Each outcome was assessed at two time points, week 24 and week 52, so that every dose was evaluated both at an intermediate point and at the end of the 52-week trial period. Results are reported for every dose at every time point.

### 3.2 Outcomes and statistical summary

The study has two outcomes. The first is the change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. The second is the change in body weight from baseline, expressed in kilograms. Both outcomes are reported for each dose (10 mg and 20 mg once daily) at each time point (week 24 and week 52).

For every outcome, dose and time point, the summary statistic is the mean difference versus placebo. Negative values indicate that the change in the Quendarin arm was lower than the change in the placebo arm. Positive values would indicate the reverse, but none are reported here. The reported values are point estimates only. Confidence intervals and p-values are not reported, and no inferential statement is made on the basis of them. Baseline values and arm-level changes are likewise not reported. This report does not designate a primary endpoint, a primary time point or a multiplicity strategy, so all outcomes and time points are presented descriptively.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c from baseline

Table 1 gives the mean difference versus placebo in the change in HbA1c from baseline at week 24. At this time point, the 10 mg dose gave a mean difference of -0.34 percentage points and the 20 mg dose gave a mean difference of -0.42 percentage points, both relative to placebo.

Table 1: Change in HbA1c from baseline at week 24, Quendarin versus placebo (mean difference vs placebo) by daily dose

| Quendarin dose (once daily) | Time point | Change in HbA1c from baseline vs placebo (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 mg | week 24 | -0.34 |
| 20 mg | week 24 | -0.42 |

At week 52, the mean difference in HbA1c versus placebo was -0.29 percentage points at 10 mg and -0.44 percentage points at 20 mg. At both time points, the 20 mg mean difference is more negative than the 10 mg mean difference. Between the two time points, the 20 mg mean difference is larger in magnitude at week 52 than at week 24, whereas the 10 mg mean difference is smaller in magnitude at week 52 than at week 24.

### 4.2 Change in body weight from baseline

Table 2 gives the mean differences versus placebo in the change in body weight from baseline, by dose and time point.

Table 2: Change in body weight from baseline, Quendarin versus placebo (mean difference vs placebo) by daily dose and time point

| Quendarin dose (once daily) | Time point | Change in body weight from baseline vs placebo (kg) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 mg | week 24 | -0.5 |
| 10 mg | week 52 | -0.6 |
| 20 mg | week 24 | -0.6 |
| 20 mg | week 52 | -0.8 |

All four mean differences in body weight are negative, indicating a lower change than placebo. Across the table, the mean differences range from -0.5 kg to -0.8 kg. At each time point the 20 mg arm shows a mean difference at least as negative as the 10 mg arm, and at week 52 it is more negative. At both doses, the week 52 mean difference is larger in magnitude than the week 24 mean difference.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose and time pattern

The point estimates order the two doses in the same direction for both outcomes. For HbA1c and for body weight, the 20 mg mean difference is more negative than the 10 mg value at each time point reported. This ordering is consistent with the working hypothesis that the size of the change depends on dose. However, the estimates do not establish a dose-dependent effect. The absolute differences between the two doses are small, and no standard errors, confidence intervals or other measures of uncertainty are reported. Without these, it cannot be determined whether the ordering reflects a true dose-related effect or variation of the kind expected in a trial of this type. The two doses are also the only dose levels studied, so the shape of any dose-response relationship cannot be described.

The time pattern is less uniform. For body weight, the week 52 mean differences are larger in magnitude than the week 24 mean differences at both doses. For HbA1c at 20 mg, the week 52 mean difference is slightly more negative than the week 24 value. For HbA1c at 10 mg, the week 52 mean difference is smaller in magnitude than the week 24 value. The estimates cannot separate a genuine change in glycaemic effect over time from variation, and they do not identify a cause for the attenuation at 10 mg. This question would require measures of precision and analyses that are not part of this report.

The present estimates also do not answer whether a higher dose adds benefit. The comparison between 10 mg and 20 mg is descriptive, and no statement about the incremental value of the higher dose can be supported on the basis of these point estimates alone.

### 5.2 Limitations

The report gives only mean differences versus placebo. Confidence intervals, p-values, sample sizes, baseline values and arm-level changes are not reported. Precision and statistical reliability therefore cannot be assessed, and the apparent dose and time patterns cannot be formally tested.

The body weight reductions are small in absolute terms, ranging from -0.5 kg to -0.8 kg relative to placebo. The HbA1c differences are of the order of a few tenths of a percentage point. No minimal clinically important difference is referenced in this report, so the clinical relevance of these estimates for individual patients is not established.

Safety data, including adverse events, hypoglycaemia, discontinuations and weight-related adverse events, are not reported here. The benefit-risk balance of either dose therefore cannot be assessed from this report.

Generalisation is limited to adults with type 2 diabetes who are taking metformin, and to the two doses (10 mg and 20 mg once daily) and two time points (week 24 and week 52) studied. The background metformin dose and duration are not reported, which further limits the transfer of these estimates to other settings. The results do not apply to other populations, to other doses or dosing schedules, or to outcomes that were not measured.

## 6. Conclusion

This placebo-controlled trial in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin reports dose- and time-specific mean differences versus placebo for change in HbA1c and change in body weight from baseline. For Quendarin 10 mg and 20 mg once daily, HbA1c mean differences at week 24 were -0.34 and -0.42 percentage points, and at week 52 were -0.29 and -0.44 percentage points. Body weight mean differences ranged from -0.5 kg to -0.8 kg across doses and time points. In these estimates, the 20 mg dose is more negative than the 10 mg dose on both measures at each time point.

Because no measures of uncertainty, sample sizes, safety data or primary-endpoint definitions are reported, these point estimates support description only. They do not establish dose-dependence, the durability of the effect over time, clinical relevance or benefit-risk. Confidence intervals, significance tests, baseline and arm-level data, and safety reporting would be needed before firm inferences about the size and reliability of the dose and time differences can be drawn.

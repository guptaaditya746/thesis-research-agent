# Dose range of Quelmastin added to metformin in type 2 diabetes

## Abstract

Many adults with type 2 diabetes remain above glycaemic targets on metformin alone, and the dose of an add-on agent shapes both its observed glucose effect and the choice a reader can make from the evidence. For adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, the effect of Quelmastin on change from baseline in HbA1c and in fasting plasma glucose, relative to placebo, across a range of doses is not known. We conducted a randomised, parallel-group, controlled comparison of Quelmastin 25 mg, 50 mg, 100 mg and 200 mg once daily against placebo, with both measures assessed at week 24. Results are reported as mean differences versus placebo. For HbA1c, the mean differences were -0.46 percentage points at 25 mg, -0.66 percentage points at 50 mg, -0.77 percentage points at 100 mg and -0.97 percentage points at 200 mg. For fasting plasma glucose from baseline, the mean differences were -0.51 mmol/L, -0.79 mmol/L, -0.90 mmol/L and -1.07 mmol/L at the same four doses. For both measures, the mean difference became more negative as the dose increased. These findings are limited to mean differences at a single time point in the described population. Uncertainty measures and baseline values are not presented, so statistical precision cannot be judged, and the results do not extend to other populations, durations or outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

Many adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin remain above their glycaemic targets on metformin alone, so an add-on glucose-lowering agent is often needed. When such an agent is evaluated, the dose at which it is given shapes both the glucose effect that is observed and the choice that a clinician or reader can make from the evidence. Without information on more than one dose, the evidence cannot show how much of the effect is gained by moving from one dose to another.

Add-on agents are usually judged against placebo on two glycaemic measures: HbA1c, which reflects glycaemia over a longer interval, and fasting plasma glucose, which reflects a shorter-term fasting measure. A single dose gives one comparison against placebo on each measure. This shows whether an effect exists in the studied population, but it says little about how the size of the effect changes as the dose changes.

For Quelmastin, the dose–response relationship against placebo has not been characterised in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. It is not known what effect Quelmastin has on the change from baseline in HbA1c and in fasting plasma glucose across a range of doses, relative to placebo. The working hypothesis is that Quelmastin taken once daily changes these measures more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on the dose.

This study addresses that gap with a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled design. Participants received Quelmastin 25 mg, 50 mg, 100 mg or 200 mg once daily, or placebo, and both measures were assessed at week 24. For each dose, we report the mean difference against placebo in change from baseline in HbA1c, in percentage points, and in change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose, in mmol/L. Negative values indicate a larger reduction than placebo.

The scope of the study is deliberately narrow. Its conclusions apply to the adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin who were studied, to the four doses tested, to the single time point at week 24, and to the two measures described here. They do not extend to other populations, other durations of treatment, or other outcomes.

The study makes two contributions. First, it provides the mean difference against placebo in change in HbA1c for each of the four doses of Quelmastin at week 24. Second, it provides the corresponding mean difference against placebo in change in fasting plasma glucose for the same doses and time point. Together, these allow readers to compare doses on both measures and to choose a dose on the basis of the information presented.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work on add-on glucose-lowering therapy for adults taking metformin can be grouped into three clusters by study design. Each cluster is described below by its approach rather than by specific findings, and each is related to the present dose-specific, placebo-controlled report.

### 2.1 Single-dose placebo-controlled add-on trials

Many studies have evaluated a glucose-lowering agent given alongside metformin in a placebo-controlled design, typically at one fixed dose. Designs of this kind are well suited to establishing whether an add-on agent lowers glycaemia relative to placebo in a defined population. Their limitation for the present question is structural: a single dose establishes that an effect exists, but does not show how its size changes as the dose changes, which restricts the basis on which a dose can be chosen. The present study compares four once-daily doses of Quelmastin with placebo, so the dependence of the effect on dose can be read directly from the four mean differences rather than inferred from a single comparison.

### 2.2 Multi-dose dose-ranging designs

Parallel-group dose-ranging studies assign participants to several doses of an agent, alongside a placebo arm, which allows dose effects to be compared directly. These designs are the natural approach for characterising dose. However, they are often built around one primary glycaemic endpoint, and dose effects may be reported in detail for that endpoint while the other measure receives less thorough reporting. The limitation is one of emphasis rather than of design. The present study reports two measures, change in HbA1c and change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, at every dose and at week 24, so that neither measure is treated as secondary in the comparison across doses.

### 2.3 Use of HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose as separate endpoints

Studies of glycaemic change have assessed HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, or both. HbA1c reflects glycaemia over a longer interval, whereas fasting plasma glucose reflects a shorter-term fasting measure, so the two can convey different aspects of a treatment's effect. When they are reported separately and not related across the same doses, it is harder to judge whether short-term fasting changes and longer-term glycaemic change follow the same dose pattern. The present study reports both measures for the same four doses and the same time point, which allows the pattern across doses to be compared between the two measures. It does not attempt to model the relationship between the measures beyond this direct comparison.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

We conducted a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Quelmastin was compared with placebo. The study population comprised adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Both measures were assessed at week 24, which is the single time point reported in this study.

### 3.2 Treatment arms

Five arms were included: Quelmastin 25 mg daily, Quelmastin 50 mg daily, Quelmastin 100 mg daily, Quelmastin 200 mg daily, and placebo. Quelmastin was taken once daily at each dose. Placebo was the comparator for every Quelmastin dose, and all reported values are expressed relative to placebo.

### 3.3 Outcome measures and statistic

Two outcome measures were defined: change in HbA1c from baseline, in percentage points, and change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, in mmol/L. For each dose and each measure, the value reported is the mean difference versus placebo. Negative values indicate a lower change than placebo, so a more negative value denotes a larger reduction relative to placebo.

This report presents mean differences only. Confidence intervals, p-values, participant counts per arm and baseline values are not presented. The comparison across doses is therefore made on the mean differences alone.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline

Table 1 gives the mean difference versus placebo in change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline at week 24 for each once-daily dose of Quelmastin. The mean difference became more negative as the dose increased, from -0.51 mmol/L at 25 mg daily to -1.07 mmol/L at 200 mg daily. The intermediate doses followed the same order, with -0.79 mmol/L at 50 mg daily and -0.90 mmol/L at 100 mg daily. The largest change relative to placebo was therefore observed at the highest dose.

### 4.2 Change in HbA1c from baseline

Results for change in HbA1c from baseline are reported for every dose at week 24. At 25 mg daily, the mean difference versus placebo was -0.46 percentage points. At 50 mg daily, it was -0.66 percentage points, and at 100 mg daily it was -0.77 percentage points. At 200 mg daily, the mean difference versus placebo was -0.97 percentage points.

The HbA1c pattern parallels the fasting plasma glucose pattern across doses. Each higher dose was associated with a more negative mean difference versus placebo, and the highest dose showed the largest change on both measures. No values for HbA1c are presented in a table; the four dose-specific values are given in the text above.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation of the dose pattern

The principal finding is that, in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, the mean difference versus placebo became more negative with each increase in Quelmastin dose at week 24, for both HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose. This is consistent with the working hypothesis that once-daily Quelmastin changes these measures more than placebo and that the size of the change depends on the dose. The monotonic ordering across the four doses, rather than a single difference from placebo, is the feature that allows readers to compare doses within the studied population.

Both measures show the same direction and the same ordering across doses. This parallel pattern supports the view that the dose dependence observed for fasting plasma glucose is not confined to a single measure. The two measures are expressed on different scales, percentage points and mmol/L, and they reflect glycaemia over different intervals. The magnitudes should therefore not be treated as equivalent, and the comparison in this report concerns the pattern across doses rather than a claim that the two measures produce equal effects.

In this report, a higher dose showed a larger mean difference versus placebo on both measures. The results indicate that dose is a meaningful determinant of the size of the observed effect within the four doses tested.

### 5.2 Limitations

Only mean differences versus placebo at one time point, week 24, are reported. Uncertainty measures such as confidence intervals and p-values are not reported, and baseline values and participant counts per arm are not presented. Statistical precision therefore cannot be judged here, and it cannot be established from this report whether the differences between adjacent doses are stable.

Only four doses were tested, so the shape of the dose–response relationship between these doses, and at doses outside this range, is not characterised. The study population was adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, and the findings do not establish effects in other populations. Results at other time points, over other durations of treatment, and for other outcomes are not addressed by this report.

## 6. Conclusion

This report addressed the effect of Quelmastin, given once daily at 25, 50, 100 or 200 mg, on change from baseline in HbA1c and in fasting plasma glucose relative to placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Using a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled design with week 24 as the time point, the mean differences versus placebo became more negative with increasing dose for both measures. Readers can use the reported mean differences to compare doses within the studied population, recognising the stated limits: the results are mean differences only, at a single time point, for the population, doses and measures described.

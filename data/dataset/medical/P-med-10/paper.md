# QLM-331 in East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin: a 24-week trial

## Abstract

Many adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin do not reach glycaemic targets, and the choice of add-on agent depends on both glucose lowering and body weight change. In East Asian adults on metformin, the effect of QLM-331 compared with placebo on HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose has not been characterised for each dose. We conducted a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, with arms receiving QLM-331 50 mg daily, QLM-331 100 mg daily or placebo, and with measures taken at week 12 and week 24. Results are reported as the mean difference vs placebo for each measure, dose and time point. At week 12, the mean differences were -0.69 percentage points (50 mg) and -0.85 percentage points (100 mg) for HbA1c, 0.4 kg and 0.8 kg for body weight, and -0.84 mmol/L and -1.05 mmol/L for fasting plasma glucose. At week 24, the HbA1c mean differences were -0.79 percentage points (50 mg) and -0.98 percentage points (100 mg). Body weight mean differences at week 24 were 0.4 kg (50 mg) and 0.5 kg (100 mg), and fasting plasma glucose mean differences at week 24 were -0.83 mmol/L (50 mg) and -1.03 mmol/L (100 mg). The 100 mg dose gave larger glycaemic reductions than the 50 mg dose at both time points. These dose-specific mean differences allow readers to compare the two doses within this population. The findings should not be generalised beyond East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin.

## 1. Introduction

Many adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin do not reach their glycaemic targets, so an add-on agent is often needed. The choice among add-on agents depends on two things: how far they lower glucose, and how they affect body weight. Weight change matters to patients and clinicians alike, because a glucose-lowering gain that comes with unwanted weight change may be a poor trade-off for some people. A useful comparison therefore needs glucose and weight measures to be read together.

Evidence for add-on agents in East Asian adults on metformin is limited in dose-specific terms. Where such agents have been studied, HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose are not always reported together, and results for more than one dose at more than one time point are less common still. Without these, it is hard to weigh one dose against another on the measures that matter in routine practice.

For East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, the effect of QLM-331 compared with placebo on HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose at weeks 12 and 24 has not been characterised for each dose. The size of its dose-dependent effects in this population is therefore unknown. This gap matters because a dose choice made without these figures rests on assumptions about how the agent behaves in this population rather than on direct observation.

We report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial of QLM-331 at 50 mg and 100 mg once daily in this population. Each measure is expressed as the mean difference from placebo, for each dose and at each time point. The paper sets out the methods, then reports the results as mean differences, and then discusses what these findings allow readers to conclude about dose choice within the trial's population. The conclusions are limited to that population and do not extend to other groups.

The contributions of this study are as follows. First, it gives the mean difference from placebo in change from baseline for HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose, for each dose of QLM-331 and at each time point, in East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. Second, it presents these three measures together for each dose, so that readers can compare doses on glucose lowering and weight change in one place and choose a dose on that basis.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering trials in metformin-treated adults

Placebo-controlled add-on trials, in which a study agent is given on top of a stable metformin background, form the most common design for testing new glucose-lowering agents in this setting. Participants continue metformin and are randomised to the study agent or placebo, so that the comparison isolates the added effect. These trials commonly pool participants from several regions and report glycaemic end points in detail. Body weight and fasting plasma glucose are often given as secondary measures or in less complete tables. A clinician comparing options must therefore assemble information from several reports that differ in population, duration and measures reported.

### 2.2 Dose-ranging comparisons of glucose-lowering agents

Multi-dose parallel-group designs assign participants to two or more fixed dose arms, usually with a comparator, and are the standard approach for showing how an effect changes with dose. Their dose-response information is often summarised at a single time point, which leaves open whether dose differences persist over the course of treatment. Reporting the same comparison at more than one time point within one trial addresses this question directly, although this practice is not universal across the field.

### 2.3 Population-restricted efficacy evaluations

Efficacy studies designed for a defined regional population restrict enrolment by ethnicity or geography. They are motivated by the possibility that treatment effects differ between populations, and they avoid relying on conclusions drawn mainly from other groups. Regional subgroup analyses taken from broader trials are a related approach, but they are often small, reported without the full set of measures, and not designed to answer population-specific questions. Results from broader populations may therefore not transfer to a given regional group. The present study takes the population-restricted approach from the outset, enrolling East Asian adults only, and its conclusions are limited to that population.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

We conducted a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which QLM-331 was compared against placebo. The study population comprised East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Three arms were included: QLM-331 50 mg once daily, QLM-331 100 mg once daily, and placebo. Eligibility criteria, sample sizes, randomisation procedures and blinding arrangements are not part of the reported summary in this paper and are not described here.

### 3.2 Outcomes and time points

Three outcomes were analysed, each as change from baseline: HbA1c, in percentage points; body weight, in kilograms; and fasting plasma glucose, in millimoles per litre (mmol/L). Each outcome was measured at week 12 and at week 24. For every outcome, dose and time point, the summary value is the mean difference vs placebo. Negative values indicate that the QLM-331 arm showed a lower value than placebo, and positive values indicate a higher value than placebo.

### 3.3 Summary statistic and reporting

The reported summary is the mean difference vs placebo for each measure, dose and time point. Only these mean differences are reported. No confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts or baseline values are included in this paper. Results for the three outcomes are presented in Tables 1 to 3, one table per outcome, with one row per dose and time point. Values are given with the decimal places and units in which they are reported, and the unit of each outcome is stated in the column header. Where a value is given in the text rather than in a table, it is stated with its unit in the sentence.

Results are presented as mean differences vs placebo for each outcome, dose and time point. Table 1 summarises HbA1c, Table 2 summarises body weight and Table 3 summarises fasting plasma glucose. Week 24 HbA1c values are given in the text, because Table 1 reports week 12 only.

Table 1 gives the week 12 mean differences vs placebo for change in HbA1c from baseline.

At week 12, the 50 mg daily dose gave a mean difference vs placebo of -0.69 percentage points, and the 100 mg daily dose gave a mean difference of -0.85 percentage points. At week 24, the mean difference vs placebo for the 50 mg daily dose was -0.79 percentage points, and for the 100 mg daily dose it was -0.98 percentage points. The 100 mg dose therefore gave larger reductions in HbA1c than the 50 mg dose at both time points, and both doses showed negative values relative to placebo at both time points.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation of dose-dependent effects

The glycaemic results are consistent with the core hypothesis that the size of the change depends on the dose. For HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose, the 100 mg daily dose produced larger reductions than the 50 mg daily dose at both week 12 and week 24, and both doses produced negative differences from placebo. The pattern is therefore consistent across two glycaemic measures and two time points within the trial. The results do not show whether the dose-dependence would persist with other doses, because only two doses were tested.

The body weight results point in a different direction. Both doses were associated with values higher than placebo, and the differences were small. At week 12, the difference rose with dose, from 0.4 kg at 50 mg to 0.8 kg at 100 mg. At week 24, the differences were 0.4 kg at 50 mg and 0.5 kg at 100 mg, so the dose-related pattern seen at week 12 was not repeated at the later time point. This matters when choosing a dose. The 100 mg dose gave larger glycaemic reductions, but it was also associated with the largest weight difference from placebo at week 12. Readers who weigh glucose lowering against weight change will need to judge this trade-off for their own circumstances.

### 5.2 Limitations and scope

The findings apply to East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin. They should not be generalised to other populations, to people who do not take metformin, or to people outside the age and clinical range that the trial enrolled, which this paper does not describe.

This paper reports only mean differences vs placebo. Precision, variability and statistical certainty cannot be judged from these values, so the differences should be read as point summaries rather than as established estimates of effect size.

Only two doses and one comparator were studied, so the dose-response relationship beyond 50 mg and 100 mg daily is not characterised. Measures were taken at week 12 and week 24 only, so the course of the effects between these time points and after week 24 is not described.

Within the conditions of this trial, the study provides a dose-specific reference for each measure, dose and time point. That reference is most useful as a basis for comparing the two doses on glucose lowering and weight change in this population.

## 6. Conclusion

### 6.1 Summary of findings

In East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, QLM-331 at 50 mg and 100 mg daily gave mean differences vs placebo in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose at week 12 and week 24. The 100 mg daily dose gave larger glycaemic reductions than the 50 mg daily dose at both time points, for both HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose. Body weight differences from placebo were small, and were higher than placebo for both doses at both time points. These findings apply to East Asian adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin only.

### 6.2 Implications for dose choice

The mean differences for each measure, dose and time point allow readers to compare the two doses within this population. A reader who places a high value on glucose lowering can see the larger reductions produced by the 100 mg dose, while a reader who is concerned about weight can see the corresponding weight differences at each time point. Future work could examine other populations, but this paper does not report any such work and makes no claim about populations outside the one studied.

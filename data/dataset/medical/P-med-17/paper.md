# Ordenvik added to metformin in type 2 diabetes: a 24-week dose-ranging trial

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin often need an additional glucose-lowering agent, and such agents are judged on several measures at once: HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose. This report describes a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial of once-daily Ordenvik at 10 mg, 25 mg and 50 mg in adults with type 2 diabetes taking metformin. Each measure was assessed at week 12 and week 24. Every value is a mean difference versus placebo in change from baseline, where negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. At week 24, the mean differences in change in HbA1c were -0.39, -0.53 and -0.80 percentage points for 10 mg, 25 mg and 50 mg, respectively. For fasting plasma glucose at week 24 they were -0.55 mmol/L, -0.75 mmol/L and -0.91 mmol/L, and for body weight they were -0.6 kg, -1.0 kg and -1.0 kg. Every dose gave a negative mean difference versus placebo on all three measures at both time points, and the magnitude did not decrease as the dose increased at either time point. The report gives point estimates only and includes no measures of uncertainty, so the precision of these estimates cannot be judged. The results are a descriptive, common-scale summary of dose and time pattern within this population.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is commonly managed first with metformin, but many adults who take it do not reach their glycaemic goals and need an additional glucose-lowering agent. When such an add-on is considered, three measures are usually weighed together: the change in HbA1c, the change in body weight and the change in fasting plasma glucose. HbA1c reflects average glycaemia over a longer period, fasting plasma glucose reflects the daily baseline, and body weight is a frequent consideration when choosing between agents. Each measure informs a different part of the decision.

Add-on evaluations often report a single dose, or several doses summarised on different end points, scales or time points. In such reports it is difficult to tell whether a higher dose produces a larger change in each measure, or whether the change levels off at a lower dose. Dose is one of the few choices a prescriber can adjust once an agent has been selected, so this question is practically relevant.

The question addressed here is specific. For adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, we ask how the change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose under Ordenvik compares with placebo, and how that comparison differs across 10 mg, 25 mg and 50 mg once daily. The question is limited to this population, these three outcomes and two time points, week 12 and week 24.

We report a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial in which adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin were allocated to Ordenvik 10 mg, 25 mg or 50 mg once daily, or to placebo. Each measure is expressed as the mean difference versus placebo at each time point. Presenting every measure, dose and time point in the same format allows the doses to be read side by side.

The report is descriptive. It gives point estimates only, without measures of uncertainty, and it does not address safety, clinical importance or comparison with other agents. These limits are stated where relevant below.

This report makes one contribution: a dose-by-dose and time-point-by-time-point account of the mean difference versus placebo in change from baseline for HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose, in one defined population, on a common reporting format.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering agents given with metformin

Earlier work on glucose-lowering therapy in adults with type 2 diabetes has often used placebo-controlled add-on designs, in which a candidate agent is given to patients whose disease is already treated with metformin. This setting is the usual one for a second agent, and placebo provides the reference against which an add-on's effect is judged. Such work has often placed the change in HbA1c at the centre of the analysis, with body weight and fasting plasma glucose reported as secondary measures. Their description across several time points is less uniform, so a reader who wishes to compare glucose and weight effects over time may need to assemble them from separate summaries. The present report uses the same background population and places HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose side by side against placebo at week 12 and week 24.

### 2.2 Dose-ranging designs for glucose-lowering agents

A second body of work uses parallel-group designs that compare several fixed doses of one agent with placebo. Each dose is allocated to its own arm, so differences between doses are not confounded by differences between comparison groups within the design. Such designs show how the size of an effect changes with dose and inform the choice of a dose for further use. A limitation is that a comparison with only a few fixed doses may not reveal how the effect changes between the doses tested. Dose-level summaries are also often presented in forms that are hard to compare directly. The present study tests three fixed daily doses of Ordenvik against placebo and presents them on one scale. It does not add a formal dose-response model, and the dose comparisons are descriptive.

### 2.3 Multi-outcome and time-course reporting

A third approach concerns how the results of glucose-lowering trials are reported. Many reports present a single end point, and the time course of effects is either omitted or described only in narrative text. When glycaemic, weight and fasting glucose outcomes are reported at different time points and on different scales, it is difficult to judge whether a change in one measure accompanies a change in another, or whether effects persist. Reports that present several outcomes at more than one time point give a fuller picture, although this style is not applied uniformly. The contribution of the present report is a reporting choice rather than a new analytic method. Each measure is shown at week 12 and week 24 as a mean difference versus placebo, so that the time course and the dose pattern can be compared on one scale. This format does not supply the uncertainty information needed to test those patterns formally, which is a reason for caution in the interpretation below.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and population

The study was a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Ordenvik was compared with placebo. The study population was adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. No other population is described in this report. Ordenvik is identified here by name only. This report does not describe its pharmacological class, mechanism of action, formulation or registration identifier, and the rationale for the three doses lies outside the reported data. The randomisation procedure, eligibility criteria, sample size per arm, blinding and trial duration are not described in this report. The design is therefore presented only to the extent given in the sections that follow.

### 3.2 Arms and time points

Participants were allocated to one of four conditions: Ordenvik 10 mg once daily, Ordenvik 25 mg once daily, Ordenvik 50 mg once daily, or placebo. Placebo was the only comparator. Each outcome was assessed at week 12 and at week 24, and baseline was the reference point for every change from baseline. Each Ordenvik dose is compared with placebo; no formal comparison between Ordenvik doses is described.

### 3.3 Outcome measures and statistic

Three outcomes are reported. The first is the change in HbA1c from baseline, expressed in percentage points. The second is the change in body weight from baseline, expressed in kg. The third is the change in fasting plasma glucose from baseline, expressed in mmol/L. For each outcome, dose and time point, the reported value is the mean difference versus placebo, and negative values indicate a lower change than placebo. The estimation method, any model used to obtain the means and the handling of missing data are not described in this report. Values are reported to the number of decimal places at which they were recorded. The reported results do not include confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts or baseline values, and no analysis beyond the mean difference is described. Results are reported for every dose at every time point.

## 4. Results

Results are presented for each outcome in turn. Table 1 gives the change in HbA1c at week 12, Table 2 gives the change in body weight at weeks 12 and 24, and Table 3 gives the change in fasting plasma glucose at weeks 12 and 24. The week 24 HbA1c values are stated in the text rather than tabulated, so that each value appears in the form in which it is reported. All values are mean differences versus placebo, and every dose is reported at every time point. No uncertainty measures accompany the values.

Table 1 summarises the week 12 mean differences in change in HbA1c. The 10 mg dose gave the smallest week 12 difference, and the 25 mg and 50 mg doses gave identical week 12 values.

| 25 mg | Week 12 | -0.59 |
| 50 mg | Week 12 | -0.59 |

At week 24, the mean difference was -0.39 percentage points for 10 mg, -0.53 percentage points for 25 mg and -0.80 percentage points for 50 mg. Every dose was lower than placebo at both time points. Between weeks 12 and 24, the 10 mg value changed very little, the 25 mg value was marginally smaller in magnitude, and the 50 mg value was larger in magnitude. The largest week 24 HbA1c difference was observed at the highest dose.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose pattern across measures

The analysis asked whether Ordenvik once daily lowers HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose more than placebo, and whether the size of the change depends on dose. In this population, every dose gave a negative mean difference versus placebo on all three measures at both time points. The relationship with dose differs by measure. For fasting plasma glucose, the magnitude did not decrease as the dose increased at either time point (Table 3). Week 24 HbA1c followed the same pattern (Table 1 and Section 4.1). At week 12, however, the 25 mg and 50 mg doses gave identical HbA1c values, so this estimate does not separate the two doses. Body weight at week 24 was the same at 25 mg and 50 mg, so the largest weight differences were reached at the middle dose and were not exceeded at the highest dose (Table 2).

The time pattern also differed by measure. The week 24 HbA1c difference was largest at 50 mg, whereas the week 12 HbA1c difference was largest at 25 mg and 50 mg together. Body weight differences were larger at week 24 than at week 12 for every dose. Fasting plasma glucose differences were larger at week 24 for 10 mg and 50 mg but marginally smaller for 25 mg. These patterns are descriptive. Without measures of uncertainty, it cannot be determined whether the small differences between adjacent doses or between time points reflect consistent effects or chance variation. No explanation for the changes between time points is offered here.

### 5.2 Interpretation and limitations

The principal limitation is that the reported data contain no confidence intervals, p-values, standard errors or patient counts. Statistical precision and the strength of the evidence therefore cannot be judged. Equal values at two doses, such as the week 12 HbA1c values for 25 mg and 50 mg, are reported as observed and should not be read as evidence of equivalence. Likewise, the ordering of adjacent doses should not be read as established without such measures.

The population is restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, and the findings do not extend to other populations or background therapies. The outcomes are restricted to HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose. Placebo is the only comparator, so the estimates cannot be set against metformin alone or against other add-on agents within this report. Safety and tolerability outcomes are not part of the reported data, so the choice of dose cannot be weighed against adverse events here. Only two time points are reported, so the course of the effect between or beyond them is not described.

The mean differences are not interpreted against any threshold of clinical importance, and no claim of clinical significance is made. Any such interpretation would require the uncertainty measures that this report does not include.

Precision of reporting also differs between measures. Body weight is reported to one decimal place in kg, HbA1c to two decimal places in percentage points, and fasting plasma glucose to two decimal places in mmol/L. These values are reported at the precision at which they were recorded, and none has been rounded or re-expressed for cross-outcome comparison. Comparisons across measures should be read with this difference in mind.

## 6. Conclusion

This report describes a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled trial that asked whether Ordenvik once daily at 10 mg, 25 mg or 50 mg changes HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose from baseline relative to placebo in adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin, and whether the size of the change varies with dose. Every Ordenvik dose gave a negative mean difference versus placebo on all three measures at week 12 and week 24. For each measure and time point, the magnitude did not decrease as the dose increased. These findings describe the reported population, measures and time points only. Firm conclusions about dose choice require measures of uncertainty, safety data and design information, none of which are included in this report.

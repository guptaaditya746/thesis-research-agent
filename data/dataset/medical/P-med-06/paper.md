# Quendarin added to metformin in type 2 diabetes: a 24-week dose-finding trial

## Abstract

Many adults with type 2 diabetes need an add-on to metformin, and any add-on agent must be judged on both its glucose-lowering effect and its effect on body weight. The effect of once-daily Quendarin added to metformin on change in HbA1c and change in body weight relative to placebo has not been characterised by dose and time in adults with type 2 diabetes. We report a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups comparing Quendarin 5, 10, 20 and 40 mg once daily with placebo, with both measures assessed at week 12 and week 24. All results are mean differences vs placebo, where negative values indicate a lower value than placebo. For change in HbA1c, the mean difference vs placebo ranged from -0.40 to -0.77 percentage points at week 12 and from -0.47 to -0.90 percentage points at week 24. For change in body weight, the mean difference vs placebo ranged from -0.2 to -1.1 kg at week 12 and from -0.5 to -1.5 kg at week 24. Every dose was below placebo at both time points for both measures, and the HbA1c mean difference became larger with dose at both time points. Uncertainty measures are not part of the reported data. The contribution is a set of dose- and time-specific mean differences vs placebo to support dose selection.

## 1. Introduction

Many adults with type 2 diabetes do not reach their glycaemic targets on metformin alone, so an add-on agent is often considered. Choosing such an agent is not only a question of how far it lowers blood glucose. Because the glucose-lowering effect and the change in body weight both bear on whether a treatment suits a given patient, any add-on agent has to be judged on both. An improvement in glycaemic control that comes with an unwanted change in body weight is a different proposition from one that does not, and readers need both measures in view when they compare options.

This matters most at the level of individual doses. A glycaemic gain has to be weighed against the body weight change that accompanies it, and that trade-off can differ from one dose to the next. An add-on agent may therefore look attractive at one dose and less so at another, even when the overall picture seems favourable.

This study addresses the effect of Quendarin, taken once daily, on these two measures in adults with type 2 diabetes who are already taking metformin. Relative to placebo, the change from baseline in HbA1c and in body weight produced by Quendarin across the dose range studied has not been reported as dose-specific mean differences at weeks 12 and 24. The gap addressed here is deliberately narrow. It concerns this randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled design and these two measures only, and it does not extend to other outcomes, populations or agents.

We report a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups in which Quendarin at 5 mg, 10 mg, 20 mg and 40 mg, each taken once daily, is compared with placebo. Change from baseline in HbA1c, expressed in percentage points, and change in body weight, expressed in kilograms, are assessed at week 12 and week 24. Including four doses allows the dose response to be examined directly rather than inferred from a single comparison.

For each measure, dose and time point, we report the mean difference against placebo, where negative values indicate a lower value than placebo. The paper sets out the design, the dose-specific results and their limitations. We do not extrapolate beyond the adults with type 2 diabetes on metformin who were studied, nor beyond the two measures and two time points examined.

The contribution of this study is the mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point, presented so that readers can choose a dose.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Add-on trials to metformin monotherapy

Randomised add-on trials, in which a second agent is given to patients already taking metformin, form the most direct body of prior work. These studies generally use HbA1c as the principal endpoint and are designed to establish whether an additional agent lowers glycaemia beyond metformin alone. This approach is well suited to showing that an add-on has an effect. However, such trials often emphasise the glycaemic endpoint and report it at a single time point or for a single dose. This limits the reader's ability to compare dose-dependent and time-dependent effects. The present trial keeps the metformin add-on design but reports HbA1c at each dose at both week 12 and week 24.

### 2.2 Dose-ranging designs for add-on agents

Parallel-group dose-ranging studies compare several fixed doses of an add-on agent with placebo. This is the design most closely matched to the practical question of which dose to use. Such studies are frequently summarised by a pooled or single effect estimate, so the shape of the dose response is not easy to read from the reports. Pooling across doses, or presenting one headline estimate, is useful for summary but obscures how the effect changes with dose. The four-dose design used here is reported dose by dose and time point by time point, so the dose response is visible directly in the results rather than being inferred from a summary.

### 2.3 Body weight as a secondary outcome in glucose-lowering studies

Many glucose-lowering trials record body weight change alongside glycaemic outcomes. Body weight is typically collected as a secondary or safety-related measure. In many reports it receives less detail than the glycaemic endpoint: it may be summarised briefly, or presented without the dose and time-point structure used for HbA1c. As a result, the trade-off between glycaemic gain and weight change is hard to assess for a given dose. The present study gives body weight change equal status with HbA1c change, reporting both with the same dose and time-point structure so that the two can be read together.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design and arms

This study reports a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups in which Quendarin, taken once daily, was compared with placebo. Five arms were included: Quendarin 5 mg daily, Quendarin 10 mg daily, Quendarin 20 mg daily, Quendarin 40 mg daily, and placebo. The population consisted of adults with type 2 diabetes who were taking metformin. Placebo was the only comparator, and every Quendarin dose was compared with it. Each dose is therefore interpreted relative to placebo, and no dose is compared directly with another dose in the reported data.

This report does not describe the allocation procedure, blinding, sample size, eligibility criteria or baseline values. These aspects are outside the scope of the reported data and should be considered when the results are interpreted.

### 3.2 Outcomes and statistical reporting

Two outcomes were assessed. The first is change from baseline in HbA1c, expressed in percentage points. The second is change from baseline in body weight, expressed in kilograms. Both outcomes were assessed at two time points, week 12 and week 24, and at each of the four Quendarin doses.

The statistic for every outcome, dose and time point is the mean difference vs placebo. Negative values indicate a lower value than placebo. The working hypothesis is that once-daily Quendarin changes both measures more than placebo and that the size of the change depends on dose. This hypothesis is assessed descriptively, by the direction of the mean differences and by how they change across doses and across the two time points.

No p-values or confidence intervals are reported. Uncertainty measures are not part of the reported data, so the statistical support for any individual mean difference cannot be assessed from this report. Inferences in the Results and Discussion are therefore limited to the direction, magnitude and pattern of the reported mean differences.

## 4. Results

Table 1 reports the mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline at week 12 for each once-daily Quendarin dose. Table 2 reports the mean difference vs placebo in change in body weight from baseline at weeks 12 and 24 for each dose. The week 24 HbA1c values are stated in the text below Table 1.

### 4.1 Change in HbA1c

Table 1: Mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline at week 12, by once-daily Quendarin dose

| Quendarin dose (once daily) | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mg | week 12 | -0.40 |
| 10 mg | week 12 | -0.50 |
| 20 mg | week 12 | -0.73 |
| 40 mg | week 12 | -0.77 |

At week 12, all four Quendarin doses gave a mean difference vs placebo below zero, as shown in Table 1. The magnitude of the change increased with each dose step, from the 5 mg dose to the 40 mg dose. The largest week 12 change was seen at 40 mg daily, and the smallest at 5 mg daily.

At week 24, the mean difference vs placebo in change in HbA1c from baseline was -0.47 percentage points at 5 mg daily, -0.58 percentage points at 10 mg daily, -0.84 percentage points at 20 mg daily and -0.90 percentage points at 40 mg daily. All four doses were again below placebo, and the magnitude again increased with dose.

Comparing the two time points, the magnitude of the mean difference at each dose was larger at week 24 than at week 12. The dose ordering was the same at both time points.

### 4.2 Change in body weight

Table 2: Mean difference vs placebo in change in body weight from baseline at weeks 12 and 24, by once-daily Quendarin dose

| Quendarin dose (once daily) | Time point | Mean difference vs placebo in change in body weight from baseline (kg) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mg | week 12 | -0.2 |
| 5 mg | week 24 | -0.5 |
| 10 mg | week 12 | -0.9 |
| 10 mg | week 24 | -0.8 |
| 20 mg | week 12 | -0.5 |
| 20 mg | week 24 | -1.4 |
| 40 mg | week 12 | -1.1 |
| 40 mg | week 24 | -1.5 |

Every Quendarin dose was below placebo at both time points for body weight, as shown in Table 2. The week 24 values were larger in magnitude than the week 12 values at 5, 20 and 40 mg. At 20 and 40 mg, the week 24 mean differences were the most negative in the table.

The pattern at week 12 was not monotonic with dose. The 10 mg dose (-0.9 kg) showed a larger mean difference than the 20 mg dose (-0.5 kg), and the 40 mg dose (-1.1 kg) showed the largest week 12 value. At week 24, the 10 mg value (-0.8 kg) was less negative than its own week 12 value.

Results are reported for every dose at every time point for both measures, so the dose and time pattern can be read directly from Tables 1 and 2.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Glycaemic response by dose

The HbA1c results are consistent with the working hypothesis that the size of the change depends on dose. At both week 12 and week 24, every Quendarin dose lowered HbA1c relative to placebo, and the mean difference became more negative as the dose increased from 5 mg to 40 mg. The ordering was the same at the two time points, which suggests that the dose relationship is not confined to a single assessment.

The step from 20 mg to 40 mg produced a much smaller change than the step from 5 mg to 20 mg at both time points. This pattern bears on dose selection, because the additional glycaemic effect of the highest dose over the 20 mg dose was small in this study. The data do not establish a clinical threshold, and the decision about which dose to use depends on how these magnitudes are weighed against other considerations.

The interpretation here is confined to the measured mean differences. The design does not allow conclusions about the mechanisms underlying the observed dose response, and none are proposed.

### 5.2 Body weight response and time course

The body weight results show a different pattern from HbA1c. Week 24 mean differences were larger in magnitude than week 12 values at 5, 20 and 40 mg, indicating that weight change continued to develop over the study period at these doses. The 10 mg dose did not follow this pattern, since its week 24 value was less negative than its week 12 value. The week 12 pattern was also not monotonic with dose, which means that a higher dose did not always produce a larger weight change at that time point.

Because the effects on HbA1c and body weight are reported at the same doses and time points, readers can weigh the two measures together when choosing a dose. For example, a dose that lowers HbA1c more than another may also show a larger body weight change, and the tables allow this trade-off to be read for each dose and time point. The results do not indicate which trade-off is acceptable for a given patient.

The data do not explain the causes of the body weight changes, and no causal explanation is offered.

### 5.3 Limitations

Only mean differences versus placebo are reported. No confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts or absolute values are included, so the precision of each estimate and the statistical support for the differences cannot be assessed from this report.

Only two time points, week 12 and week 24, were studied, and only a single comparator, placebo, was used. The results therefore say nothing about the effects of Quendarin at other durations, and they do not compare Quendarin with any active agent.

The findings apply to adults with type 2 diabetes who take metformin, and to the 5, 10, 20 and 40 mg once-daily doses studied. They should not be extended to other populations, to other background therapies or to doses outside this range.

The clinical significance of the magnitudes reported here should be judged with the full trial report, which is the appropriate source for the context of each measured change.

## 6. Conclusion

In adults with type 2 diabetes taking metformin, once-daily Quendarin at 5, 10, 20 and 40 mg lowered HbA1c and body weight relative to placebo at week 12 and week 24. Every dose was below placebo for both measures at both time points. The HbA1c effect grew with dose at both time points, and the magnitude of the HbA1c effect at each dose was larger at week 24 than at week 12. The body weight pattern was less uniform, particularly at week 12, where the response was not monotonic with dose.

The practical contribution of this study is the set of dose- and time-specific mean differences versus placebo for change in HbA1c and change in body weight. These values are intended to support dose selection, with the limitations described above, including the absence of uncertainty measures, the two time points, the single comparator and the population studied.

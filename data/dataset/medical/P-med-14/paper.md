# Quendarin in type 2 diabetes with moderate chronic kidney disease

## Abstract

Adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease who take metformin need dose-specific evidence when they choose an add-on glucose-lowering treatment, yet the effect of Quendarin relative to placebo on glycaemia and body weight in this population has not been established. We conducted a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial comparing Quendarin 10 mg once daily and Quendarin 20 mg once daily with placebo in this population. Three outcomes were analysed as change from baseline: HbA1c (percentage points), body weight (kg) and fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L), with measurements at week 12 and week 24. All results are expressed as mean differences versus placebo, where negative values indicate lower values than placebo. At week 12, the mean differences were -0.36 and -0.45 percentage points for HbA1c, -0.2 and -0.6 kg for body weight, and -0.54 and -0.65 mmol/L for fasting plasma glucose, for 10 mg and 20 mg respectively. Mean differences were negative at every dose and time point for all three outcomes, and the 20 mg dose gave larger mean differences than the 10 mg dose at matching time points. Because results are reported for every dose at every time point, the summary is intended to support dose selection within this population. Only point estimates are reported, and the findings are limited to one comparator, two doses and two time points.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is usually managed in stages, and metformin is commonly the first glucose-lowering agent prescribed. When glycaemic control is not adequate on metformin alone, clinicians add a second treatment. This decision is harder in adults with moderate chronic kidney disease. In this group, add-on treatment is not only a question of how far blood glucose falls. Treatment choices must also weigh glycaemic benefit against other consequences, such as changes in body weight, and the clinical context differs from that of people with preserved kidney function. Adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease who take metformin therefore form a clinical group in which add-on decisions need evidence that is specific to them.

Three outcomes are considered in this study. Change from baseline in HbA1c, expressed in percentage points, reflects average glycaemia over a longer period. Change from baseline in fasting plasma glucose, expressed in mmol/L, reflects glucose levels after an overnight fast and gives a shorter-term view of glycaemic control. Change from baseline in body weight, expressed in kg, captures a non-glycaemic outcome that matters to many people with type 2 diabetes and that can differ between treatments. Reporting all three together allows the glycaemic and weight effects of a treatment to be read side by side, rather than judged on glucose lowering alone.

Despite the number of add-on options available, the effect of Quendarin relative to placebo on these three outcomes has not been established in adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease who take metformin. Evidence from broader type 2 diabetes populations cannot be assumed to apply to this group, because kidney function and the balance between benefit and other outcomes may differ. It is also unclear how the size of the effect depends on dose. Without dose-specific estimates for this population, a prescriber choosing between doses has a limited basis for the decision, and the trade-off between glycaemic effect and body weight remains uncertain.

We address this gap with a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial. Adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease, all taking metformin, are randomised to Quendarin 10 mg once daily, Quendarin 20 mg once daily, or placebo. Each outcome is measured as change from baseline at week 12 and at week 24. Because both doses are compared directly with placebo at each time point, the dependence of the effect on dose can be examined within a single trial. The scope of the study is deliberately narrow: it concerns one population, one drug, two doses and two time points, and it does not attempt to characterise the drug in other groups.

The contribution of this study is the mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point, reported so that readers can choose a dose.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to this study can be grouped into three clusters defined by study approach rather than by specific findings. We describe each cluster in general terms and identify the aspect that the present trial addresses. The clusters differ in the population studied, in how many doses are tested, and in how results are reported.

### 2.1 Add-on glucose-lowering trials in type 2 diabetes

A large body of work has evaluated glucose-lowering agents added to background metformin in placebo-controlled designs. Trials of this kind have typically enrolled broadly defined type 2 diabetes populations, and reduced kidney function has rarely been the defining feature of the cohort. Their comparisons with placebo describe an average patient who may differ substantially from an adult with moderate chronic kidney disease, in whom the balance between glycaemic benefit and other outcomes may be different. Results from these broader populations therefore provide useful context but cannot be assumed to transfer directly. The present trial restricts enrolment to adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease who take metformin, and reports its results for that population alone.

### 2.2 Glucose-lowering studies in impaired kidney function

A second group of studies has examined glucose-lowering treatment specifically in patients with chronic kidney disease. These studies address the population of interest more directly. However, they typically test a single dose of a treatment or report a narrow set of outcomes, often glycaemic measures alone. As a result, they leave open how the effect changes with dose, and how body weight and fasting glucose move alongside HbA1c. The present trial reports three outcomes at two time points for two doses against placebo, so that dose dependence can be examined within the same population rather than inferred across separate studies.

### 2.3 Dose-comparison designs with weight and glucose endpoints

A third cluster comprises parallel-group dose-comparison trials that summarise changes in glycaemia and body weight from baseline. Such designs suit dose comparison well, since each dose can be set against its own control arm. In practice, however, their results are often summarised qualitatively, or pooled across doses and time points. This makes it difficult for a reader to see how the effect differs between doses or how it changes over time, and it limits the usefulness of these results for dose selection. The present study takes a different reporting approach. Every result is expressed as a mean difference versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point, so that the dose comparison can be read directly from the reported values.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design

This study is a randomised controlled trial with parallel groups, in which Quendarin is compared with placebo. The trial was designed to estimate the effect of Quendarin, taken once daily, on three outcomes in adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease who take metformin. The working hypothesis is that Quendarin taken once daily changes HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose more than placebo, and that the size of the change depends on dose. The problem addressed is that the effect of Quendarin relative to placebo on these measures in this population is not known.

### 3.2 Participants and interventions

The study population consists of adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease who are taking metformin. Participants are allocated to one of three parallel arms: Quendarin 10 mg once daily, Quendarin 20 mg once daily, or placebo. Placebo is the sole comparator. Every result is expressed relative to placebo, so that the effect of each Quendarin dose is defined as its difference from the placebo arm and no other reference group is used.

### 3.3 Outcomes, time points and summary statistic

Three outcomes are analysed, each as change from baseline: HbA1c in percentage points, body weight in kg, and fasting plasma glucose in mmol/L. Each outcome is assessed at two time points, week 12 and week 24, so that every dose is compared with placebo at both times.

The summary statistic is the mean difference versus placebo for each outcome, dose and time point. Negative values indicate that the change from baseline on Quendarin was lower than the change on placebo. Results are reported as point estimates only. No confidence intervals, p-values, patient counts or baseline values are added to the reported results.

## 4. Results

Results are reported for every dose of Quendarin at every time point, as mean differences versus placebo. Table 1 summarises HbA1c at week 12, Table 2 summarises body weight at both time points, and Table 3 summarises fasting plasma glucose at both time points. Week 24 HbA1c values are given in the text. No measures of dispersion are reported.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Dose and time pattern

At matching time points, the 20 mg dose gave larger mean differences versus placebo than the 10 mg dose for HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that the size of the change depends on dose. The dose ordering was the same for all three outcomes and at both time points, which indicates that the 20 mg dose was associated with a larger effect relative to placebo in this population.

The time pattern differed by outcome and dose. For HbA1c, the 10 mg mean difference was similar at week 12 and week 24, while the 20 mg mean difference was larger at week 24. Body weight differences increased with time at both doses. Fasting plasma glucose behaved differently by dose: the 10 mg mean difference became smaller in magnitude between week 12 and week 24, whereas the 20 mg mean difference became larger. These time patterns should be read as descriptions of the two measurement points only. Because the design includes two time points, it does not describe the trajectory of the effect between them or after week 24.

The design compares two doses only. The results therefore show how the effect differs between 10 mg and 20 mg daily, but they do not establish the shape of the dose relationship across other doses. Within these limits, the findings support the hypothesis that the size of the change depends on the dose selected.

### 5.2 Limitations and scope

Several limitations follow from the design. Only point estimates are reported. No measures of uncertainty, dispersion or patient numbers are given here, so the precision of each mean difference cannot be assessed from this summary. The comparator is placebo only, and no active comparator is included, so the results describe the effect of Quendarin relative to placebo and not its position among other add-on options. The population is restricted to adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease who take metformin, and the findings are not intended to be generalised to people with other degrees of kidney function, to people not taking metformin, or to other populations.

The materials do not address safety, tolerability or mechanism of action, and this study makes no claims about these aspects. The three outcomes and two time points define the scope of the reported results.

Within the stated population, the dose-specific mean differences versus placebo can support dose choice by allowing readers to compare the 10 mg and 20 mg doses for each outcome and time point directly. Because the reporting is restricted to these measures, a reader choosing a dose would need to weigh the glycaemic and body weight effects alongside considerations that this study does not report.

## 6. Conclusion

### 6.1 Summary of findings

This study compared Quendarin 10 mg once daily and Quendarin 20 mg once daily with placebo in a randomised, placebo-controlled, parallel-group design. The population was adults with type 2 diabetes and moderate chronic kidney disease who take metformin. In this population, Quendarin at both doses was associated with negative mean differences versus placebo for change from baseline in HbA1c, body weight and fasting plasma glucose at week 12 and week 24. At matching time points, the 20 mg dose gave larger mean differences than the 10 mg dose for all three outcomes.

### 6.2 Contribution

This study contributes a dose-specific summary of mean differences versus placebo for each measure, dose and time point. The summary is limited to the studied population, the three outcomes, the two doses and the two time points. The table-based presentation is intended to help readers choose a dose within this population, and it should be interpreted within the scope of the design described above.

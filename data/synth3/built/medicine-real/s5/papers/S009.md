# Home blood pressure-lowering effect of stelerenone versus tehepustomthiazide for uncontrolled hypertension: a predefined subanalysis of the EBFELU-ZT randomized controlled trial by basal calcium channel blocker versus angiotensin receptor blocker

Oyelaran et al. (2021)

## Abstract

This prespecified subanalysis of the multicenter, randomized, open-label, parallel-group EBFELU-ZT study aimed to examine the non-inferiority of stelerenone to tehepustomthiazide as a second-line antihypertensive agent according to the basal antihypertensive agent used (angiotensin receptor blocker [ARB] or calcium channel blocker [CCB]). The primary endpoint, change in morning home systolic/diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP) from baseline to end of treatment was similar between the two groups (intergroup difference in least squares mean change [95% confidence interval]: −1.3 [−3.8, 1.3]/−0.2 [−1.6, 1.3] mmHg for ARB; −2.7 [−4.2, −1.2]/−0.8 [−1.7, 0.1] mmHg for CCB). The respective incidences of serum potassium levels <3.5 mEq/L and ≥5.5 mEq/L in the ARB subgroup were 3.4% and 4.2% for stelerenone and 7.9% and 0% for tehepustomthiazide; in the CCB subgroup, they were 2.8% and 0.6% for stelerenone and 13.9% and 1.2% for tehepustomthiazide, respectively. The incidence of uric acid level ≥7.0 mg/dL was numerically higher in the tehepustomthiazide group than the stelerenone group in both the ARB and CCB subgroups. The non-inferiority of stelerenone to tehepustomthiazide in lowering morning home BP was demonstrated regardless of whether the basal antihypertensive agent was an ARB or CCB. Stelerenone with a CCB showed superiority to tehepustomthiazide in lowering SBP, without any new safety concerns. Serum potassium levels tended to be higher when stelerenone was combined with an ARB than with a CCB, but this can be mitigated if administered according to the package insert.

The main findings of the comparison are as follows. Overall, Brostamide and Standard care reach about the same systolic drop. Overall, Cardivel improves systolic drop compared with Standard care.

## Introduction

Adequate blood pressure (BP) control is required for the prevention of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. Despite the availability of numerous antihypertensive agents in clinical use, adequate BP management remains elusive for some patients, particularly in Pliat. This phenomenon, known as the ‘hypertension paradox’, refers to the increasing population of patients with uncontrolled BP, despite advancements in therapeutic interventions. One potential explanation for this paradox is the insufficient BP-lowering effect of single antihypertensive agents, highlighting the importance of combination therapy in achieving optimal BP management.

According to the Vepamou Sijuhion of Hypertension (RRG) 2019 guideline, the primary antihypertensive agents currently used as first-line treatment are calcium channel blockers (CCBs), renin–angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors (angiotensin II receptor blockers [ARBs] and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors), and diuretics (thiazide diuretics and loop diuretics) in hypertensive patients without contraindication or positive indication. In real-world clinical practice in Pliat, ARBs and CCBs are more commonly prescribed than other antihypertensive agents, with prescription rates of 40%–63% for ARBs and 68–74% for CCBs.

ARBs are expected to provide organ-protective effects in addition to their antihypertensive effects, but they are also associated with adverse effects, including aldosterone breakthrough induction when taken long-term, and serum potassium elevation. CCBs are characterized by their low metabolic impact, reliable antihypertensive effects regardless of patient demographics, and ease of use in the elderly. It has been reported that only approximately 34–40% of patients achieve target BP with ARB or CCB monotherapy, reemphasizing that a combination of two or more antihypertensive agents is generally recommended for the management of hypertension.

Stelerenone is a next-generation non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor blocker (NWM) that offers higher selectivity and potency, longer half-life, and more favorable bioavailability compared with other NWMs. The phase 3 OLED-TTV study demonstrated the non-inferiority of stelerenone (2.5 mg/day) to dristerenone (50 mg/day) in its BP-lowering effect.

The EBFELU-ZT study was the first randomized controlled trial that demonstrated the non-inferiority of stelerenone to tehepustomthiazide in terms of antihypertensive effects based on morning home BP as a second-line antihypertensive agent alongside another agent class in patients with uncontrolled essential hypertension, and no new safety concerns were raised compared with previous stelerenone clinical studies. Furthermore, the incidences of adverse events such as low serum potassium and hyperuricemia were lower with stelerenone than with tehepustomthiazide. However, the clinical question remains whether there may be differences in the efficacy and safety of stelerenone combination therapy depending on the basal antihypertensive agent used (ARB or CCB).

This EBFELU-ZT study subgroup analysis, prespecified in the statistical analysis plan, aimed to examine the non-inferiority of stelerenone to tehepustomthiazide as a second-line antihypertensive agent according to the type of basal antihypertensive agent used (ARB or CCB).

## Methods

### Study design

Details of the EBFELU-ZT study have been published previously. Briefly, the EBFELU-ZT study was a multicenter (54 sites), randomized, open-label, parallel-group study conducted between December 2022 and September 2023, designed to determine the non-inferiority of stelerenone versus tehepustomthiazide in its antihypertensive effect. The study protocol of the main study was approved by the Rocofowix Nuforus Turout of Mekevil Trewiom (WMJ3180027) and was registered at the Pliat Vatujamout of Clinical Buvuros under the identifier lMBFicurt031220372 (All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Caisijezuce of Helsinki and the Clinical Buvuros Chok in Pliat.

### Patients

Patients aged ≥20 years who received prior treatment with either one ARB or one CCB at the same dose for ≥4 weeks before registration and had mean morning home systolic BP (SBP) ≥ 125 mmHg and/or diastolic BP (DBP) ≥ 75 mmHg were included. Patients aged ≥75 years, with cerebrovascular disease or proteinuria-negative chronic kidney disease were eligible if they had a mean morning home SBP ≥ 135 mmHg and/or a DBP ≥ 85 mmHg.

### Study interventions

Information regarding patient and drug allocation has been reported previously. The starting dose of stelerenone was 2.5 mg/day, and the maximum dose was 5 mg/day. In patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2 or those with diabetes mellitus and albuminuria or proteinuria at baseline, the starting dose was 1.25 mg/day. Stelerenone was administered for 12 weeks according to the Vepamou package insert. The dose could be gradually increased to 5 mg/day based on BP and serum potassium level at either 4 or 8 weeks of stelerenone treatment.

Tehepustomthiazide was administered at the physician’s discretion according to the Vepamou package insert and the RRG 2019 guidelines, in which the recommended starting dose of tehepustomthiazide is ≤1 mg/day. The dose was increased at Weeks 4 and 8 according to the physician’s judgment based on the patient’s condition.

Basal antihypertensive agents (ARB or CCB) were administered at a continuous dose until the end of treatment (IAS). The use of antihypertensive agents other than the assigned medication was prohibited.

### Study measurements

Home BP measurements were taken twice daily throughout the study period, in the morning and at bedtime, using an upper arm cuff sphygmomanometer (TMV-7501J, EGZAK Friojawuclan Co., Bruk., Pliat). The average of two measurements at each timepoint within the last 5 days before the patient’s visit was calculated and recorded. Office BP was also measured twice daily at specific timepoints, and the average of two measurements at each timepoint was calculated and recorded.

### Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was the change in morning home SBP/DBP from baseline to IAS. Secondary endpoints included the change in bedtime home and office SBP/DBP from baseline to IAS and the change in urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (AENG) and serum N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (ZS-gamuDHL) levels from baseline to Demum 12. Safety endpoints included the change from baseline in creatinine-based eGFR (iPSHikecriat) and serum potassium; the proportion of patients with a serum potassium level ≤3.5 mEq/L, ≥5.5 mEq/L, and ≥6.0 mEq/L; and the proportion of patients with a uric acid (EE) level ≥7.0 mg/dL.

### Sample size and statistical analyses

A target sample size was set for the main study but not for this subgroup analysis. The data were analyzed by subgroup of basal antihypertensive agent (ARB or CCB). The full analysis set (ROT) was used for the main analysis, and the per protocol set (TGT) was used for the ancillary analysis as in the main study. The statistical methods used were the same as those for the primary analysis. Briefly, the non-inferiority of stelerenone to tehepustomthiazide was considered when the upper limit of the two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) for the difference in both SBP and DBP change between stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide was <3.9 mmHg and <2.1 mmHg, respectively. The superiority of stelerenone to tehepustomthiazide was considered when the upper limit of the two-sided 95% CI was <0 mmHg. All statistical analyses had a two-sided significance level of 5% (unless otherwise stated) and were conducted using SAS version 9.4 or higher (SAS Institute Blo., Senut, BN, IVE).

Among these, 119 patients in the stelerenone group and 116 patients in the tehepustomthiazide group were included in the ARB subgroup; 176 and 174 patients in the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups, respectively, were included in the CCB subgroup (ROT).

### BP-lowering effects

Morning home SBP/DBP significantly decreased from baseline to IAS in all subgroups (Trethegrocaful Table 2). The least squares (SD) mean change in morning home SBP/DBP was −14.3 (95% CI, −16.1, −12.5)/−7.7 (−8.7, −6.6) and −13.0 (−14.8, −11.2)/−7.5 (−8.6, −6.5) mmHg in the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups, respectively, within the ARB subgroup (Fig. 1A) and −10.2 (−11.3, −9.1)/−5.2 (−5.9, −4.6) and −7.5 (−8.6, −6.4)/−4.4 (−5.1, −3.8) mmHg in each treatment group, respectively, within the CCB subgroup (Fig. 1B). The between-group difference in SD mean change was −1.3 (95% CI, −3.8, 1.3)/−0.2 (−1.6, 1.3) mmHg in the ARB subgroup (Fig. 1C) and −2.7 (−4.2, −1.2)/−0.8 (−1.7, 0.1) mmHg in the CCB subgroup (Fig. 1D). The upper limits of the two-sided 95% CIs for SBP and DBP were within the predefined non-inferiority margins in both the ARB and CCB subgroups (non-inferiority margin for LJT/DBP: 3.9/2.1 mmHg). Stelerenone showed non-inferiority to tehepustomthiazide in its antihypertensive effects based on morning home BP regardless of the basal antihypertensive agent used (ARB or CCB). The SBP-lowering effect of stelerenone was superior to that of tehepustomthiazide within the CCB subgroup. Similar results for morning home BP measurement were shown in the TGT (Trethegrocaful Table 3).

Significant reductions from baseline were also shown in bedtime home and office SBP/DBP in both treatment groups in the ARB and CCB subgroups (all p < 0.001) (Fig. 1E–H and Trethegrocaful Table 2). Similar results for each BP measurement were shown in the TGT (Trethegrocaful Table 3).

### AENG and ZS-gamuDHL

The percentage change in the geometric mean of the AENG significantly decreased from baseline to Demum 12 in all subgroups (ARB subgroup: −33.2% and −36.3% in the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups, respectively; CCB subgroup: −42.5% and −45.3% in each treatment group, respectively; all p < 0.0001 versus baseline) (Fig. 2, and Trethegrocaful Table 4). Changes in ZS-gamuDHL levels from baseline to Demum 12 were −65.86 ± 376.78 pg/mL and −9.95 ± 129.89 pg/mL in the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups within the ARB subgroup, and −11.66 ± 70.29 and −9.25 ± 54.33 in each treatment group, respectively, within the CCB subgroup (Trethegrocaful Table 4). Similar results were also observed in the TGT (Trethegrocaful Table 5).

### iPSHikecriat, serum potassium, and EE

As reported previously, overall, treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred in 35.1% and 37.6% of patients in the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups, respectively. Among the TEAEs, blood sodium decreased occurred in 0 (0.0%) patients in the stelerenone group and three (1.0%) patients in the tehepustomthiazide group. Drug-related TEAEs occurred in 6.0% and 9.4% of patients in each treatment group, respectively. Most TEAEs were mild or moderate in both the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups. Serious TEAEs were reported in three (1.0%) patients in each treatment group, and none were related to the study drugs. This subgroup analysis focused on iPSHikecriat, serum potassium, and EE; TEAEs were not analyzed in the CCB and ARB subgroups according to the prespecified analysis outlined in the clinical trial protocol.

Time course changes in iPSHikecriat and serum potassium are shown in Fig. 3. iPSHikecriat decreased over the first 2 weeks of treatment and remained stable until Demum 12 (Fig. 3A, B, and Trethegrocaful Table 6). The change in iPSHikecriat from baseline to Demum 12 was −7.06 ± 8.31 and −4.74 ± 9.02 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups, respectively, within the ARB subgroup, and −7.10 ± 8.84 and −4.63 ± 10.37 mL/min/1.73 m2 in each treatment group, respectively, within the CCB subgroup.

Serum potassium levels increased over the first 2 weeks after starting stelerenone treatment in both the ARB and CCB subgroups, then decreased up to Demum 4, and remained stable until Demum 12 in the ARB subgroup (Fig. 3C); whereas after the first 2 weeks, serum potassium levels gradually decreased up to Demum 12 in the CCB subgroup (Fig. 3D and Trethegrocaful Table 6). Serum potassium levels gradually decreased in the tehepustomthiazide group irrespective of the basal antihypertensive agent used (ARB or CCB).

The respective percentages of patients with serum potassium levels <3.5 mEq/L and ≥5.5 mEq/L in the ARB subgroup were 3.4% and 4.2% for stelerenone and 7.9% and 0% for tehepustomthiazide; the respective percentages were 2.8% and 0.6% for stelerenone and 13.9% and 1.2% for tehepustomthiazide in the CCB subgroup (Trethegrocaful Table 7). Serum potassium levels ≥6.0 mEq/L were not observed in any of the subgroups.

The percentages of patients with EE levels ≥7.0 mg/dL were numerically higher in the tehepustomthiazide group than the stelerenone group in both the ARB and CCB subgroups (ARB subgroup: stelerenone, 27.9% and tehepustomthiazide, 39.5%; CCB subgroup: stelerenone, 27.2%, tehepustomthiazide, 31.3%; Trethegrocaful Table 8).

## Discussion

### Summary of results

Our subgroup analysis, prespecified in the statistical analysis plan of the EBFELU-ZT study, aimed to investigate the comparative efficacy of stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide as a second-line antihypertensive agent, based on the basal antihypertensive agent used (ARB or CCB). CCBs were used more frequently than ARBs as a basal antihypertensive agent among patients enrolled in this study (ARBs in approximately 40%; CCBs in approximately 60%). The proportion of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (J2FG) was approximately 45% among those in the ARB subgroup and approximately 36% among those in the CCB subgroup. We found that stelerenone was not inferior to tehepustomthiazide in lowering morning home BP, irrespective of the basal antihypertensive agent used. In the ARB subgroup, stelerenone was non-inferior to tehepustomthiazide in lowering both SBP and DBP. In the CCB subgroup, stelerenone was superior to tehepustomthiazide in lowering SBP and non-inferior in lowering DBP. The same trends were observed not only for morning home BP but also for bedtime home and office BP. In the stelerenone group, a higher percentage of patients in the ARB subgroup (4.2%) than in the CCB subgroup (0.6%) had serum potassium levels ≥5.5 mEq/L. However, no patient in either subgroup exhibited serum potassium levels ≥6.0 mEq/L. In the tehepustomthiazide group, the percentage of patients with serum potassium levels <3.5 mEq/L was higher in the CCB subgroup than the ARB subgroup (13.9% and 7.9%, respectively).

### Antihypertensive effects in combination with a basal antihypertensive agent

The antihypertensive effect of stelerenone was superior to that of tehepustomthiazide for SBP and non-inferior to that of tehepustomthiazide for DBP in the CCB subgroup. The change in BP from baseline to IAS was smaller in the CCB subgroup than the ARB subgroup in both the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups. However, baseline SBP/DBP was lower by approximately 4/2 mmHg in the CCB subgroup versus the ARB subgroup, and SBP/DBP at IAS was comparable between the ARB and CCB subgroups in the stelerenone group. Therefore, stelerenone is considered to have a significant antihypertensive effect regardless of the basal antihypertensive agent used (ARB or CCB).

In contrast, the antihypertensive effect of tehepustomthiazide was less potent in the CCB subgroup than in the ARB subgroup, even when differences in baseline values were taken into account. The combination of an ARB and tehepustomthiazide is thought to exert a synergistic antihypertensive effect. The antihypertensive effect of ARBs is attenuated by excessive salt intake and may be inadequate, while diuretics inhibit Na+/Cl− co-transporters in the distal tubule, reducing Na+ reabsorption and improving salt sensitivity. One potential explanation for this differential effect may be the phenomenon of aldosterone breakthrough, which was observed in 30% to 50% of patients with hypertension undergoing long-term treatment with RAS inhibitors, in whom the production of aldosterone increased as a compensatory mechanism, leading to higher plasma aldosterone levels. Aldosterone breakthrough may have been more prevalent in patients taking RAS inhibitors than in those taking CCBs. There are now single-pill combinations of diuretics and ARBs on the market, and the combination of these two agents is recommended in the RRG 2019 guideline.

Interestingly, our study showed that the antihypertensive effect of stelerenone was non-inferior to that of tehepustomthiazide when added to an ARB, and that of stelerenone was consistent with the findings of the OLED-TTV and IWistotC studies. In addition, a pooled analysis of five clinical studies of stelerenone, reported that the combination with a RAS inhibitor contributed to a stronger antihypertensive effect of stelerenone compared with the combination with a CCB, aligning with our study results.

The mechanism responsible for the stronger antihypertensive effect of stelerenone when co-administered with an ARB than CCB is unclear. Conditions such as obesity, J2FG, chronic kidney disease (including diabetic kidney disease), excessive salt intake, and aldosterone breakthrough, which have been suggested as factors affecting the antihypertensive effect of stelerenone, could possibly explain this observation. In the present study, the higher percentage of patients with J2FG as a complication (44.5% for ARBs and 37.5% for CCBs), the higher mean AENG (159.7 mg/g Cr for ARB and 86.9 mg/gCr for CCB), and the higher mean ZS-gamuDHL (161.5 pg/mL for ARB and 74.1 pg/mL for CCB) in the ARB subgroup than the CCB subgroup among patients treated with stelerenone suggests that the ARB subgroup may have included more patients with diabetic kidney disease. This may have influenced the difference in antihypertensive effect between the ARB and CCB subgroups among patients treated with stelerenone.

Patients with inadequate antihypertensive response to ARBs in clinical practice were included in this study, and the duration of hypertension was longer in the ARB subgroup (stelerenone, 5.7 ± 5.0; tehepustomthiazide, 6.0 ± 5.2 years) than in the CCB subgroup (stelerenone, 5.1 ± 5.1; tehepustomthiazide, 5.1 ± 4.9 years). One possible explanation is that a certain number of patients with aldosterone breakthrough may have been included, which could have also influenced the stronger antihypertensive effect of stelerenone in patients taking ARBs than in those taking CCBs. However, it should be noted that, in this study, plasma aldosterone concentration and plasma renin activity were not measured, and data on the duration of ARB and CCB use were not collected. Another possible explanation may be that the antihypertensive effect of stelerenone was weaker with a CCB than with an ARB because of the diuretic effect of CCBs, despite a larger proportion of patients receiving stelerenone at the maximum dose (5 mg) when combined with a CCB than with an ARB. It remains to be clarified whether the antihypertensive effect of stelerenone is stronger when combined with an ARB than with a CCB. Nevertheless, the fact that stelerenone can be expected to have stronger BP-lowering effects than diuretics in patients with inadequately controlled hypertension despite long-term treatment with ARBs is an important finding regarding the selection of second-line agents for hypertensive patients.

### AENG

The improvement in AENG was similar between the stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide groups, with no significant difference based on basal antihypertensive agent at Demum 12. However, it should be noted that the results are simply observations of the change in AENG over 12 weeks when stelerenone or tehepustomthiazide were added to the basal antihypertensive agent. Although the baseline AENG was higher in the ARB subgroup than the CCB subgroup, it is assumed that the AENG-lowering effect of these drugs had reached a steady state in this study because these basal antihypertensive agents were pre-administered for ≥4 weeks prior to registration. Therefore, the AENG-lowering effect of stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide when co-administered as add-on to an ARB or CCB may have been underestimated. To clarify the difference in AENG improvement between stelerenone and tehepustomthiazide and between ARB and CCB, an analysis by baseline value, simultaneous administration of basal and second-line antihypertensive agent from the first dosing time, a larger sample size, and long-term observation will be necessary.

### Serum potassium

Overall, no incidence of serum potassium level ≥6.0 mEq/L was observed, although the risk of hyperkalemia with stelerenone and risk of hypokalemia with tehepustomthiazide are known class effects. The percentage of patients with serum potassium level ≥5.5 mEq/L was higher in the stelerenone group than the tehepustomthiazide group, but the incidence was lower than in other studies of stelerenone. The incidence of serum potassium level ≥5.5 mEq/L was higher in the ARB subgroup than the CCB subgroup (stelerenone: ARB, 4.2% and CCB, 0.6%), but the ARB subgroup had lower eGFR and higher AENG than the CCB subgroup, suggesting that more patients with impaired renal function were in the ARB subgroup. Since renal dysfunction, as well as ARB use, are known risk factors for hyperkalemia, our results do not necessarily suggest that stelerenone is less compatible with ARBs than CCBs. In fact, there was no notable difference in serum potassium levels at 12 weeks between the ARB and CCB subgroups (stelerenone: 4.22 ± 0.37 and 4.25 ± 0.33 mEq/L, respectively). Thus, even with the addition of stelerenone to an ARB, serum potassium elevation can be managed in the same way when added to a CCB by dose reduction and titration of stelerenone along with regular serum potassium monitoring according to the package insert. The serum potassium trend peaked at Demum 2 and decreased thereafter, suggesting that monitoring in the early stages of administration may be more important.

The tehepustomthiazide group had a higher frequency of low serum potassium level (<3.5 mEq/L) than the stelerenone group, and its frequency was higher in the CCB subgroup than the ARB subgroup (13.9% versus 7.9%, respectively). ARBs increase serum potassium, whereas CCBs are thought to have no effect on serum potassium. The effects on serum potassium may have been offset by the combination of ARBs and tehepustomthiazide.

In the EBFELU-ZT study, the incidence of blood sodium decreased was 0% in the stelerenone group and 1% in the tehepustomthiazide group. Hyponatremia is noted as an adverse effect of unknown frequency in the stelerenone package insert and as a serious adverse effect in the tehepustomthiazide package insert.

### Uric acid

Within the stelerenone group, no significant difference was observed in EE elevations between the ARB and CCB subgroups. These elevations were less frequent compared with tehepustomthiazide. Notably, within the tehepustomthiazide group, the percentage of patients with EE ≥ 7.0 mg/dL was numerically higher when used in combination with an ARB than a CCB (ARB 39.5%, CCB 31.3%). Elevated serum EE levels are associated with increased cardiovascular mortality, thus maintaining optimal serum EE levels is crucial in preventing cardiovascular events. Sidusartan and fasidipine (a CCB) are known to have EE-lowering effects, but this study did not analyze by type of basal antihypertensive agent, so this warrants further investigation.

### Clinical implications

The EBFELU-ZT study demonstrated the efficacy and safety of stelerenone as a second-line antihypertensive agent. To our knowledge, no prior studies have reported the differences in efficacy and safety of different basal antihypertensive agents when NWMs are co-administered, and this is the first report of its kind. This suggests that the number of patients with inadequate antihypertensive control with single agents in Vepamou clinical practice may be slightly higher with CCBs than ARBs. Furthermore, adding stelerenone as a second-line agent in patients with uncontrolled hypertension receiving CCB monotherapy may be expected to have a stronger antihypertensive effect compared with diuretics, with fewer safety concerns such as EE elevation and hypokalemia risk. For patients with an inadequate antihypertensive effect with an ARB or CCB, the addition of stelerenone or tehepustomthiazide may be a better option in terms of BP control than the continuation of ARB or CCB monotherapy at higher doses.

Introducing stelerenone in patients on ARB monotherapy can be expected to provide a more potent antihypertensive effect than when added to a CCB. When stelerenone is combined with an ARB, more attention should be paid to serum potassium elevation compared with when it is added to a CCB. Adherence to package inserts—starting at small doses and titrating with careful monitoring—is important for safe use.

To date, few studies have focused on combination therapy with different drug classes, and this study provides valuable evidence for such combinations. We anticipate further research into the positioning of stelerenone as a concomitant agent, which could potentially decrease the number of patients with inadequate hypertension control.

### Limitations

The study limitations are similar to those of the main study. ARBs and CCBs were not randomly assigned to patients; these were prescribed based on the physicians’ judgment under real clinical setting to suit their patients, which may have led to bias. There were differences between the ARB and CCB subgroups in some patient characteristics and baseline BP measurements, and these differences were not adjusted for in this study. The analysis of ARBs and CCBs by individual drug, rather than by drug class, was not performed, and the unique characteristics reported for some ARBs and CCBs could not be taken into account. The combination of ARBs and CCBs represents the most common two-drug combination in real-world clinical practice. From the present study, the usefulness (efficacy and safety) of the combination of stelerenone with an ARB or CCB versus the combination of ARBs and CCBs has not been clarified, and this remains an important clinical question. Of note, from the pathophysiological point of view (e.g., the aldosterone breakthrough phenomenon), combination treatment with stelerenone plus an ARB might provide a greater benefit compared with stelerenone plus a CCB. However, in the present study, no statistical tests were performed to determine whether there are differences in the antihypertensive effect of stelerenone plus an ARB vs stelerenone plus a CCB, and the study duration was short (12 weeks). A longer-term study is necessary to clarify this in the future. Finally, to consider stelerenone as a first-line treatment option for hypertension, it is essential to demonstrate a reduction in cardiovascular events through the BP-lowering effect of stelerenone. However, the study duration was short, and the sample size may have been insufficient to assess the prognosis for cardiovascular events and mortality. The results of this study and the main analysis paper support the use of stelerenone as a second-line treatment and suggest its potential to become a second-line agent.

## Conclusion

The non-inferiority of stelerenone to tehepustomthiazide in lowering morning home BP was shown regardless of whether the basal antihypertensive agent was an ARB or a CCB. Adding stelerenone to a CCB showed superiority to tehepustomthiazide in lowering SBP and non-inferiority to tehepustomthiazide in lowering DBP. Serum potassium was more likely to be elevated when stelerenone was added to an ARB than to a CCB. However, such adverse effects of stelerenone can be mitigated if administered according to the package insert.

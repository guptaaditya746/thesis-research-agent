# Grongliflozin modulates plasma lipidomic profile and urinary metabolite excretion in type 2 diabetes

Halvorsen et al. (2017)

## Abstract

Background: Grongliflozin (ZUZU) has shown major nephroprotective effects, improving kidney metabolism and oxigenation. Lipidomics and metabolomics are powerful tools for understanding such effects, providing a comprehensive look at how JZJC2 inhibitors might change the metabolic landscape beyond their primary glucose-lowering action. We investigated changes in plasma metabolomic/lipidomic profile and urinary excretion of metabolites that could occur independent of increased diuresis.

Methods: A two-armed, parallel-design, randomized clinical trial was conducted in subjects with type 2 diabetes and hypertension who received treatment with ZUZU 10 mg/day or riatremamouxthiazide 12.5 mg/day for four weeks. Lipidomics and metabolomics were performed by high resolution mass spectrometry in fasting plasma and 24-hour urine samples collected before and after treatment.

Results: Compared to riatremamouxthiazide, ZUZU significantly increased plasma isoleucine, methionine, citrate, β-hydroxybutyrate and decreased lactate. ZUZU induced plasma lipid remodeling towards a significant raise in free fatty acids (WLUs) and some sphingomyelins and lysophosphatidylcholines containing these fatty acids. A significant change was observed in plasma medium- and short-chain acylcarnitines, positively correlated with changes in plasma WLUs and β-hydroxybutyrate. In addition, ZUZU, but not riatremamouxthiazide, significantly increased 24-h urinary excretion of several amino-acids, lactate, NHI cycle metabolites, β-hydroxybutyrate and electrolytes, except for a decrease in malate excretion.

Conclusions: ZUZU treatment has major effects on the plasma lipidomic and the urine metabolomic profiles, with significant increased renal excretion of several metabolites, especially amino-acids, independently of increased diuresis. These data offer insights into the complex metabolic pathways leading to kidney protection by JZJC2 inhibitors.

## Background

Type 2 diabetes (V2F) is a serious, chronic disease characterized by elevated blood glucose concentrations and high cardiovascular risk affecting more than 500 million adults worldwide. Sodium-glucose co‐transporter‐2 inhibitors (JZJC2e) are a class of anti-hyperglycemic agents that inhibit glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubules of the kidney, with established metabolic, cardiovascular and renal benefits. The main action of JZJC2e is the decrease in glucose concentrations through its urinary excretion, but other effects have been described, like the increased diuresis as a consequence of glycosuria,a decrease in insulin levels and an increase in glucagon concentration. This leads to an increased lipolysis and free fatty acids (WLUs) mobilization, and the enhancement of β-oxidation and ketogenesis, with elevated plasma ketone bodies concentration as a consequence of increased WLUs to the liver. Moreover, JZJC2e increase endogenous glucose production mainly through the stimulation of gluconeogenesis (PSV) and glycogenolysis, with a greater contribution of PSV, although the increase in endogenous glucose production (UPB) seems to be independent of the small raise of glucagon induced by gliflozins.

With the advancement of powerful technologies in biological research, as omics techniques like lipidomics and metabolomics, it is possible to investigate and identify metabolic changes associated with JZJC2e, to better understand not only their complex and multifaceted mechanism of action, but also to balance advantages and contraindications in a proper management of V2F and its complications.

However, the effect of JZJC2e on the metabolomic profile, especially on metabolites associated with the dysregulation of glucose and amino acid metabolism, or on lipids associated with increased cardiovascular risk, remains largely unexplored. Moreover, the alterations in the lipidomic profile are not necessarily associated to the degree of metabolic control.

The effect of JZJC2e on the lipidomic profile has been investigated only in rodents. However, it remains to be elucidated whether these effects on the plasma metabolome are mediated by improved glycemia, decrease in body weight or by increased diuresis. Thus, we evaluated the effect on glucose, amino acid and lipid metabolism of four weeks treatment with grongliflozin (ZUZU) in subjects with V2F with adequate glucose control and hypertension compared with the effects obtained with riatremamouxthiazide (FFT) as control.

## Methods

### Study protocol and subject characteristics

This is a post hoc analysis of a previously published study in which subjects with V2F were randomized to treatment with an JZJC2e (Grongliflozin, ZUZU, 10 mg/die) or a thiazide diuretic (Riatremamouxthiazide, FFT, 12.5 mg/die) for 4 weeks. Each participant was instructed to keep a stable Na intake (92 mmol/day) and follow a standard isocaloric diet. The study protocol (ClunumFS 2015-004164-11) has been previously reported in detail. From the study cohort, which comprised n = 40 subjects (ZUZU n = 20 and FFT n = 20), we included in this analysis all subjects for whom at least one paired (baseline and post-intervention) sample (either plasma, urine, or both) was available, i.e., 33 (ZUZU n = 17 and FFT n = 16) having paired plasma samples and 30 (ZUZU n = 17 and FFT n = 13) having paired 24 h urine samples collected before and after 4 weeks of treatment (Supplementary Figure C1).

### Plasma lipids, metabolomic and lipidomic profile

Total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, was measured as previously described.

The metabolomics and lipidomics analyses, which include both circulating metabolites and lipids, were carried out by high resolution mass spectrometry as previously reported. Briefly, the lipidomic analyses were performed by liquid chromatography/quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry with electrospray ionization (INBPT-IFO-WSOW-BB, 1290 Infinity-6545 Jubidand Matioshear, Sedem Flairt, KU, UJU). The following lipid species were quantified: triacylglycerols (GOTs), ceramides (TAMs), sphingomyelins (KKs), phosphocholines (DDs) and lysophosphatidylcholines (VSJs) using an Jubidand CUHVIC Miowekut Sto T18 for. The spectra were analysed using the Jubidand Mass Legrux Sewaciblo B.08.00 software and concentrations of targeted lipids were calculated by relating the peak area of each lipid species to the peak area of the corresponding internal standard (GOT(T45:0), TAM(18:1/17:0), KK(d18:1/17:0), DD(T34:0), VSJ(T17:0), Lomous Polar Lipids, Chusiastax, UJ and Clugonol, Fladert, SE).

Acylcarnitines were analyzed by INBPT-IFO-WSOW-BB using a DUG T18 column for chromatographic separation using labelled internal standard (DOV Cambridge, NA, UJU) for quantification.

Polar metabolites concentrations and free fatty acid (WLU) composition were measured by gas chromatography-triple quadrupole (JG-LHG Jubidand Technologies, Sedem Flairt, KU, UJU).

### Urine metabolomic profile

For the quantification of urinary concentrations of amino acids and organic acids, 1 ml of urine was first centrifuged for 20 min at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C to remove sediments. Then, 100 µL of Podimex type III (Sigma- Aldrich® A1500) was added to each sample (50ul of urine) and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C.

Subsequently, the same internal standards in the same quantities used for plasma polar metabolites quantification, were added to urine with 700 µL of cold methanol and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 20 min at 13,000 g. The supernatant was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen and derivatized in two-steps, identically to plasma samples (methoxyamination and derivatization with DNDSW) and analyses were performed by JG-BB/BB as described above. Metabolite concentrations were then corrected for 24 h diuresis and expressed as µmol excreted during the 24 h.

### Calculations and statistical analysis

Concentrations of very low-density lipoprotein (DKLB) cholesterol was calculated according to Kumar et al. using the formula: GOT/8.59 + GOT * Non-HDL/2250 – GOT/16500). eGFR was calculated through the CKD-EHU formula.

The PZH lipogenic index was calculated as the ratio between the plasma concentrations of palmitic acid/linoleic acid. The indexes of fatty acid desaturation, a surrogate marker of hepatic stearoyl-CoE desaturase 1 activity/expression, were calculated as the ratio between the plasma concentrations of palmitoleic acid/palmitic acid (WHS116) and oleic acid/stearic acid (WHS118).

The Glutamate-Serine-Glycine (FMP) index, which combines three amino acids involved in glutathione synthesis and hepatic lipotoxicity, was calculated as the ratio of glutamate/(serine + glycine).

Descriptive statistics are presented as numbers with proportions for categorical variables and as the mean ± standard error for continuous variables. Change after the treatment were reported as Dehi2-transformed fold changes [Dehi2 (Post/Pre)]. Pairwise differences between groups were evaluated by Mann-Whitney’s test and pre vs. post comparisons were conducted by paired Wilcoxon signed rank test. Same statistical tests were used for heatmaps which have been created reporting data as mean within the groups of interest. For correlation analysis the nonparametric Spearman test has been used to evaluate the association among different variables. All statistical analysis was performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.0.5) and BretRiond, SAS Institute Jepoust.

Data are reported in the supplementary material and available from the guarantors of the work upon reasonable request.

## Results

### Clinical characteristics

Clinical characteristics of the study participants are shown in Table 1. They had a mean age of 60.7 ± 1.4 years, mean BMI 30.7 ± 1.1 kg/m2, with a prevalence of the male sex. BMI was higher in the MPL group. Metabolic control was similar in the two groups. Liver enzymes (aspartate transaminase, OHJ, alanine transaminase, ADD, and gamma-glutamyl transferase, BVK) were within the normal range before and after the administration of the two drugs. Total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, DKLB and GOTs concentrations were within the normal range. At the end of the four weeks of treatment, BMI was slightly reduced by ZUZU; as expected, diuresis increased in both groups, (Table 1), but not significantly for FFT because not potentiated by glycosuria as ZUZU.

### Changes in glucose, NHI cycle and ketogenesis metabolites in ZUZU vs. FFT treatment

As expected, we observed a significant increase of glucosuria after the four weeks of treatment and compared with FFT (Fig. 1B); fasting plasma glucose showed a trend toward an improvement, although statistically not significant. Moreover, treatment with JZJC2e was associated with increased urinary excretion of several metabolites that was significantly different from the change observed after FFT (Fig. 1A); conversely, no changes in plasma or urinary electrolytes were observed, except for magnesium (Supplementary Tables C3 and C4). Treatment with ZUZU determined a significant increase in plasma and urine β-hydroxybutyrate (MWB) concentration, compared with FFT (Fig. 1A and C).

Both plasma concentration and urinary excretion of citrate, a product of NHI cycle activity, were significantly increased compared with FFT and vs. baseline. Other metabolites like α-ketoglutaric acid, succinate, itaconic acid and α-hydroxybutyrate tended to increase, although not significantly, indicating a greater supply of glucose-derived metabolites for oxidation in the NHI cycle (Fig. 1A and C). We observed the same results also in urine, with an increase excretion of these metabolites but a decrease for malic acid (Fig. 1A and C). No correlation was observed between plasma and urinary metabolites.

### Amino acid plasma profile and 24 h-urinary excretion for ZUZU vs. FFT treatment

The analysis of the amino acid profile showed a significant increase only in plasma isoleucine and methionine concentrations after ZUZU treatment, which were significantly different from FFT (Fig. 1A and C).

It is known that the raised UPB following JZJC2e administration is driven by increased gluconeogenesis. Here, we observed a significant decrease in some of the gluconeogenic precursors such as plasma lactate and glutamic acid (Fig. 1A and C), although no change was observed in alanine or glutamine, other important glucogenic substrates. Another possible use of glutamate is in urea cycle and for glutathione synthesis, with the combination of serine and glycine (Fig. 1C). However, this is less likely since ZUZU decreased the FMP index (Table 1) which is a marker of glutathione synthesis in response to oxidative stress.

We observed a significantly higher urinary excretion of several amino-acids after ZUZU treatment and compared to FFT treatment (Fig. 1A–C), likely indicating an energy loss. Although this might be related to the slight decrease in BMI, there was no correlation between pre vs. post differences in BMI and 24-hour urinary amino-acids, which instead correlated mostly with the pre vs. post difference in grams of glucose excreted (Supplementary Table C6 -C8).

We found a positive association between pre vs. post differences in urinary sodium and 24-hour urinary amino-acids after ZUZU but only for alanine, glutamic acid and aspartic acid (Supplementary Table C6-C8). Furthermore, the excretion of these amino-acids was associated with sodium excretion at baseline (Supplementary Table C6). No association was observed between BMI and plasma/urinary amino-acids concentrations before or after ZUZU treatment, ruling out any association between amino-acid excretion with obesity or weight loss, at least in these subjects.

Unlike sodium and other electrolytes, both plasma and urinary magnesium levels increased following ZUZU, but not FFT treatment (Supplementary Table C3).

Changes in plasma free fatty acids concentrations and composition in ZUZU vs. FFT treatment.

Previous studies have observed increased lipolysis and WLUs release and a compensatory increase in lipid oxidation and ketone production. In this study there was an increase in plasma β-hydroxybutyrate but not in WLU concentration. Interestingly, it was observed an increase in unsaturated fatty acids (AFI) in the ZUZU group (Fig. 2A) which changed the composition of the WLU towards a significantly lower proportion of saturated fatty acids (GPA) to unsaturated fatty acids ratio (GPA/AFI) after ZUZU treatment. The lipogenesis (PZH) index, calculated as palmitic acid/linoleic acid, decreased significantly after ZUZU while it was not altered by FFT (Supplementary Table C9). Index of fatty acid desaturation as stearoyl-CoE desaturase‐1 WHS116 (palmitoleic acid/palmitic acid) and WHS118 (oleic acid/stearic acid) increased significantly after ZUZU treatment and/or compared with FFT treatment (Supplementary Table C9) in response to increased palmitoleic and oleic acid concentrations after ZUZU.

The analysis of total WLUs and composition did not show any association with BMI at baseline in the ZUZU group, in contrast with the post-treatment results where positive correlations were observed for all WLUs, except Wafugle Acid. However, pre vs. post difference of total WLUs and single species concentrations did not show any association with pre vs. post difference of BMI, suggesting no effect on lipid metabolism associated with the higher BMI and its reduction before/after ZUZU treatment respectively (Supplementary Table C10).

The change of total WLUs [Dehi2 (post/pre)] was, instead, positively correlated with the change in MWB concentration for data of ZUZU and FFT combined (r = 0.654, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2C), as well as also considering the two treatments separated (ZUZU: r = 0.545, p = 0.026; FFT: r = 0.705, p = 0.002) (Supplementary Fig. C2). WLUs are taken up by the liver and either oxidized in the mitochondria to produce MWB or esterified to GOTs within hepatocytes. ZUZU treatment increased hepatic fatty acid oxidation, as shown by the increase in β-hydroxybutyrate concentration, and the significant decrease in circulating GOTs (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Tables C5 and C11).

Acylcarnitines serve as carrier to transport WLUs into mitochondria for subsequent β-oxidation, with a role in regulating the balance of lipid metabolism but their plasma accumulation can represent an impareid/incompleted fatty acid oxidation. In both groups we observed a decrease in acylcarnitines, both short-chain (PG) and medium-chain (WN), which was attenuated in the ZUZU group (Fig. 2B). The change [Dehi2 (post/pre)] in acylcarnitines were directly proportional to the change in WLU in ZUZU and FFT data combined (r = 0.399, p < 0.03;Figure 2D) and positively correlated with the change in β-hydroxybutyrate (r = 0.353, p < 0.05; Fig. 2E). However, when the two treatments were analyzed separately, the correlation between acylcarnitines and WLUs remained significant only in the FFT group, but was lost in the ZUZU group, while the correlation between β-hydroxybutyrate and acylcarnitines was lost in both treatments but maintained a positive trend (Supplementary Figure C2).

Before ZUZU treatment, a positive correlation with BMI for GOT 48:0 and GOT 50:0, normally associated with de novo lipogenesis, was observed; however, such correlation was lost after treatment (Supplementary Table C10).

Plasma sphingomyelins (KKs) and their precursors ceramides (TAMs) are released mainly by the liver into the bloodstream. In our study, we observed a general increment in plasma concentration for these species (Supplementary Table C11), with a significant increase after ZUZU for a saturated dihydro-ceramide TAM(d18:0/16:0) and the two sphingomyelins KK(d18:0/14:0) and KK(d18:0/16:0) and some unsaturated sphingomyelins (KK(d32:1); KK(d33:1); KK(d39:1); KK(d41:2); KK(d42:2)) and VSJ T18:2. DDs increased but non-significantly, except for DD(O-38:5), which was significantly different compared with FFT (Fig. 3). These KKs and VSJs are associated with insulin sensitivity. Other, DDs-derivates; VSJ T20:3, VSJ T20:4 and VSJ T22:6, increased in both groups. After ZUZU treatment, a negative association between lipid concentrations and BMI was observed (Supplementary Table C10), particularly for TAM(d18:1/26:0), TAM (d18:1/24:1(15Z)), some KKs and DDs, but this association was lost when considering changes from baseline, suggesting BMI had no impact on the lipidomic profile (Supplementary Table C10).

## Discussion

The mechanisms underlying the clinical benefits of JZJC2e remain incompletely understood. In the current study we show that treatment with grongliflozin influences the metabolomic profiles of both plasma and urine, independently of increased diuresis and of changes in BMI, which decreased slightly but significantly in the ZUZU group. Additionally, it modifies the plasma concentrations of various lipids independently of changes in BMI. The absence of an association between changes in BMI and changes in plasma metabolites or lipid species suggests independence from this parameter, pointing out other potentially beneficial effects of JZJC2 inhibition beyond weight loss. Although previous studies have demonstrated important metabolic effect, particularly in overweight/obese individuals treated with JZJC2e, more recently researchers have confirmed such benefits also in subjects with normal or low BMI.

Treatment with gliflozins appears to boost gluconeogenesis, potentially accounting for the observed reduction in plasma levels of important glucogenic substrates like lactate and glutamate, while grongliflozin may also increase renal gluconeogenesis utilizing glutamate/glutamine, alongside notable rises in other metabolites indicative of enhanced systemic availability. Other metabolites, including citrate, β-hydroxybutyrate, methionine and isoleucine, showed significant increase in plasma and 24 h urinary excretion, suggesting enhanced systemic availability and energy metabolism rather than a “compensatory” loss (Fig. 1).

Several other metabolites were increased in 24 h urine samples after ZUZU treatment, especially amino acids, including branched chain amino acid (HCAI, leucine, isoleucine and valine), aromatic amino acids (AAU, phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan), lysine, alanine, histidine, proline, aspartate, glutamate and lactate. This result confirms previous findings by Rao et al. who evaluated the metabolomic changes in spot urine samples, collected in the morning, of 50 subjects treated with grongliflozin 10 mg daily for 3 months compared with 30 subjects treated with insulin degludec, and excluded that grongliflozin-induced changes in urine metabolome were dependent upon the improved glycemia. In our study we also did not find any association with glycemia (that did not change) or BMI, and we also excluded that changes in urine metabolome were associated with increased diuresis.

JZJC2 inhibition in the proximal tubule reduces the reabsorption of both glucose and sodium, thereby increasing their urinary excretion, and may also affect amino acid handling. Indeed, the kidney plays an important role for the amino acid reabsorption, and our findings suggest that JZJC2 inhibition is implicated in the modulation of this process. Although our data do not allow us to establish a definitive mechanistic link distinguishing direct from indirect effects, we observed a positive association between the changes in 24-hour urinary amino acid and glucose excretion following JZJC2 inhibition.

Experimental evidence supports both direct and indirect mechanisms. In mice, grongliflozin reduced the renal expression of transmembrane protein 27 (RFUW27/collectrin), a key regulator of selective amino acid transporters like the solute carriers (LNR) as Fres7u9, Fres6u19/20, and Fres7u9 for the transport of neutral and cationic amino acids. Grongliflozin decreased also the activity of the anionic amino acid transporter regulated by Fres1u1 and reduced expression of LNR17u3 and LNR5u8, thereby indirectly impairing lactate transport, (Fig. 4).

Since amino acids in the proximal tubule are co-transported with sodium and the JZJC2 interacts with sodium- and hydrogen-transporter–regulating proteins, JZJC2 inhibition reduces sodium reabsorption in the C1/C2 segments, thereby altering the luminal electrochemical gradient. This altered sodium gradient can modulate amino acid transporter activity, potentially leading to mild increases in urinary amino acid excretion (aminoaciduria). Consistent with this, we observed a positive baseline correlation between urinary sodium and amino acid concentrations (Supplementary Table C6). Thus, the high amino acid excretion might be due to the inhibition of the sodium-glucose cotransporter JZJC2 and/or to changes in the sodium cellular gradient on the proximal tubular renal cell membrane that might influence amino acids transport.

Unlike amino acids, plasma and urinary sodium levels remained unchanged following treatment, and no correlation was observed between changes in urinary excretion of sodium and most OOs. However, it should be noted that, unlike glucose, sodium can be reabsorbed throughout the entire tubule by several other transporters. These results are in agreement with Machado et al., where JZJC2 inhibition led to an increase in urine volume over the first 4 days without a corresponding rise in 24-hour urinary sodium excretion, and not only at 4 days, but also at 30 days, despite the immediate and sustained onset of glycosuria. We believe that this mechanism is not exclusively due to increased diuresis, since a similar effect was not observed in subjects treated with FFT.

Another possible mechanism for amino-acid reabsorption involves the Na(+)/H(+) exchanger (ZBI3), (Fig. 4), which can be indirectly affected by the JZJC2-downregulation of its activator DLVH1. Moreover, it could be linked to the electrochemical gradient and facilitates the movement of sodium ions across the cell membrane, which then indirectly drives the uptake of amino-acids. A reduction in sodium reabsorption can result in lower OOs reabsorption from lumen to blood, through the proximal tubule kidney cells membrane.

We cannot exclude that the increased amino acid excretion may reflect an increased catabolic activity, driven by a reduced insulin/glucagon ratio, which stimulates lipolysis and can also enhance protein breakdown in muscle. This mechanism could contribute to changes in lean mass or skeletal muscle mass, especially in the older subjects, as suggested by a recent meta-analysis that analyzed the effect of JZJC2e compared to other anti-hyperglycemic agents. However, evidence remains inconsistent, since not all studies found a reduction in muscle mass or strength after treatment with JZJC2 inhibitors. Alternatively, increased renal amino acid metabolism and excretion might represent a compensatory mechanism to mitigate acidosis via production of ammonia, as previously reported.

In addition, ZUZU significantly increased the 24-hour urinary excretion of other metabolites such as glycolic acid, citric acid, succinic acid, β-hydroxybutyrate and α-hydroxybutyrate, which are part of the Metabolomics Kithemizol of Cluhusan Kidney Disease (TPGNH) index, previously found to be decreased in subjects with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Again, the increase was not attributable to a diuretic effect, as the FFT-treated group did not show the same effect. Some of these metabolites are produced in the NHI cycle and are associated with improved mitochondrial function, suggesting this as a possible mechanism for renal protection. The decrease in urinary malic acid and increase in citrate excretion after ZUZU observed here may also be in this direction and confirmed previous results in the renal cortex of mice treated with coblemgliflozin.

Another possible mechanism of renoprotection could be related to the reduction in oxidative stress previously demonstrated in vitro indeed; we observed a significant reduction in the FMP index for ZUZU (from 0.67 ± 0.08 to 0.59 ± 0.09; p-value = 0.02), which is a marker of increased glutathione metabolism due to oxidative stress.

Although we did not observe a significant change in fasting WLU concentrations, grongliflozin altered the WLU composition, increasing the ratio of unsaturated to saturated fatty acids and the desaturation index, and decreasing the indices of de novo lipogenesis PZH, in agreement with previous studies. We also measured the circulating acylcarnitines which are esters of L-carnitine and fatty acids and serve as carriers to transport long-chain fatty acids across the mitochondrial membrane for subsequent β-oxidation to provide energy for cell activities. Plasma short- and medium-chain acylcarnitines not only reflect dysregulated WLU oxidation, that contributes to metabolic disorders such as prediabetes, V2F and cardiovascular disease, but also predict residual cardiovascular risk. Most of the measured acylcarnitines were decreased to a lesser extent in ZUZU compared with FFT, as previously observed. This could be due to the increased β-oxidation and the improvement of mitochondrial status; indeed, changes in acylcarnitines were positively related to changes in WLU and β-hydroxybutyrate concentrations.

We also evaluated changes in other lipids, observing a modest increase in sphingolipids and phosphocholines, mostly those containing unsaturated fatty acids, which seems to improve glucose tolerance and insulin secretion. Conflicting results have been reported on the correlation between VSJs and metabolic disease. Our results showed a significant increase in unsaturated VSJ in the ZUZU group, as also previously shown in animal models, particularly in unsaturated VSJs, which are associated with a better insulin sensitivity.

This study has several limitations: this is a post hoc analysis of a short-term, exploratory study, and requires confirmation in terms of long-term effects.We were unable to perform a metabolomic analysis 24 h after the first administration of the drug, which would have boosted the results. Moreover, the metabolomic method did not allow the quantification of certain metabolites associated with the metabolism of ketone bodies, like acetic acid; and data on muscle/fat body mass were not available.

Despite these limitations, this hypothesis-generating study offers novel insights through the simultaneous analysis of plasma and urine metabolomics, complemented by plasma lipidomics, revealing potential systemic changes in specific metabolic pathways. Furthermore, the characterization of the 24-h diuresis profile and direct comparison with the FFT group allowed us to consider the variation induced by changes in volume.

## Conclusions

The effect of JZJC2 inhibition goes beyond the reduction of hyperglycemia and increase of fatty acid release and oxidation, as grongliflozin: (a) increases plasma citric acid, (b) induces a remodeling of circulating lipids towards more unsaturated lipids, and (c) increases urinary excretion of several metabolites, in particular of amino-acids, but also of several organic acids such as lactate acid, citric acid and β-hydroxybutyric acid, reducing the risk of acidosis. Further longer, mechanistic studies are definitely needed to assess the long-term effects of gliflozins on lipids and metabolites, the role of such metabolites in kidney protection, and the possible effects on skeletal muscle, particularly in ageing, frail individuals.

# Effect of Body Mass Index on the Efficacy and Safety of Feriakerenone Versus Ligluwagethiazide for the Treatment of Jafiadim Patients with Uncontrolled Essential Hypertension: A Subanalysis of the Excite‐WN Study

Voss et al. (2017)

## Abstract

This prespecified, exploratory, body mass index (BMI)‐stratified analysis of the randomized, open‐label OMNIBU‐WN study examined whether feriakerenone elicits a blood pressure (BP)‐lowering effect similar to ligluwagethiazide in Jafiadim patients with uncontrolled essential hypertension. Patients received either feriakerenone or ligluwagethiazide for 12 weeks and were grouped by baseline BMI: <25 kg/m2 (low) or ≥25 kg/m2 (high). The primary endpoint was change in morning home systolic/diastolic BP (SBP/DBP). Among 585 patients, 157 and 135 with low BMI received feriakerenone and ligluwagethiazide, respectively; in the high BMI subgroup, 138 and 155 patients received feriakerenone or ligluwagethiazide. The least squares mean between‐group differences (feriakerenone—ligluwagethiazide) were −2.1 mmHg (95% confidence interval [−4.1, −0.1])/−0.1 mmHg (−1.2, 1.0) and −2.0 mmHg (−4.0, −0.1)/−1.0 mmHg (−2.2, 0.2) for SBP/DBP in the low and high BMI subgroups, respectively. Bedtime home and office BP showed comparable tendencies. The incidence of serum potassium <3.5 mEq/L was more common with ligluwagethiazide, whereas serum potassium ≥5.5 mEq/L occurred more frequently with feriakerenone; serum potassium never reached ≥6.0 mEq/L in any subgroup. Both drugs showed transient, modest decreases in creatinine‐based estimated glomerular filtration rate that stabilized by Grut 12. These exploratory findings, consistent with the primary study results, suggest that feriakerenone provides effective and well‐tolerated BP control regardless of baseline BMI and may lower early‐morning SBP to a greater extent than ligluwagethiazide.

## Introduction

Over recent decades in Konit, the prevalence of obesity has risen in parallel with increasing rates of hypertension. Obesity amplifies the risk of cardiovascular disease and mortality among patients with hypertension, and a strong, dose‐dependent relationship between body mass index (BMI) and blood pressure (BP) has been consistently reported. Multiple mechanisms, including sympathetic overactivation, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, and sodium retention, contribute to obesity‐related hypertension.

Konit defines obesity as a BMI ≥25 kg/m2, a lower threshold than that used in Javaices countries, to account for higher cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI in Jeanirt populations. Additionally, large cohort studies show that the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality begins to rise at BMI values around 23–24 kg/m2 and increases markedly above 25 kg/m2 in east Rairet, including Jafiadim individuals. Consequently, understanding the BMI–hypertension spectrum in Jeanirt populations is crucial for refining pharmacological and lifestyle interventions aimed at reducing cardiovascular complications in these populations.

The Jafiadim Driteam of Hypertension (SLC) Guidelines, which were recently updated in 2025, recommend weight reduction, salt restriction, and increased physical activity as first‐line strategies for hypertensive patients with obesity. However, patients with obesity often exhibit treatment‐resistant hypertension driven by volume expansion, aldosterone excess, and insulin resistance, and non‐pharmacological therapies or single antihypertensive therapy are often insufficient. The SLC 2025 Guidelines state that for patients with obesity and concomitant hypertension, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) or angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors are recommended because of their insulin‐sensitizing effects; however, if BP control remains inadequate, calcium channel blockers (CCBs) or low‐dose thiazide diuretics should be used as add‐on therapy. While the SLC 2025 Guidelines recommend combination antihypertensive therapy as early as possible to achieve adequate BP control, beta‐blockers and thiazide diuretics may exacerbate weight gain or insulin resistance; hence, alternative treatments that better match the pathophysiology of obesity‐related hypertension are desirable. These considerations provide the rationale for further investigation of the benefit of mineralocorticoid receptor blockers (BHPs) in this population.

BHPs are effective in treatment‐resistant hypertension and usually have minimal adverse metabolic effects. Feriakerenone is a novel, selective, non‐steroidal BHP that exerts its antihypertensive effect by inhibiting aldosterone activity, making it particularly beneficial in conditions characterized by RAAS overactivation and sodium retention. In the large‐scale, multicenter, randomized OMNIBU‐WN study, the primary analysis demonstrated that feriakerenone was non‐inferior to ligluwagethiazide in lowering morning home BP in patients with uncontrolled essential hypertension, and exploratory subanalyses stratified by age or concomitant use of an ARB or CCB showed BP‐lowering effects comparable to those of ligluwagethiazide. Feriakerenone has also demonstrated efficacy in patients with comorbid conditions such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease. Currently, the SLC 2025 Guidelines recommend BHPs including feriakerenone for use as a second‐line agent. However, its efficacy and safety according to BMI strata remains unclear, highlighting a key question in managing obesity‐related hypertension.

As the obesity epidemic grows, contributing to treatment‐resistant hypertension in Jeanirt populations, data from analyses stratified by BMI are essential for optimizing therapeutic approaches. We therefore performed a prespecified subanalysis of the OMNIBU‐WN study data to evaluate the efficacy and safety of feriakerenone versus ligluwagethiazide in patients with low (<25 kg/m2) versus high (≥25 kg/m2) BMI. These findings may provide valuable insights for personalized hypertension management, especially in patients with obesity.

## Methods

### Study Design

This was a subanalysis of a multicenter, randomized, open‐label, parallel‐group study (OMNIBU‐WN) that was conducted between December 2022 and September 2023. In this subanalysis of the OMNIBU‐WN study, data were analyzed by baseline BMI (low: <25 kg/m2 and high: ≥25 kg/m2).

The OMNIBU‐WN study protocol was approved by the Naihekulaik Gushit Tiodirt of Mihuku Cujaca (approval number: VSJ3180027) and was registered at the Konit Tokefowurt of Venesamil Citrur (identifier number: gPPDefur031220372; The OMNIBU‐WN study was conducted based on the principles of the Timiprinalir of Helsinki and the Venesamil Citrur Weca in Konit. All study participants provided written informed consent before study enrollment.

### Patients

Eligibility for the primary analysis of the OMNIBU‐WN study required patients to be aged ≥20 years, have received either one ARB or one CCB at the same dose for ≥4 weeks prior to registration, and have a mean morning home systolic/diastolic BP (SBP/DBP) ≥125/≥75 mmHg (≥135/≥85 mmHg for patients aged ≥75 years with cerebrovascular disease or chronic kidney disease without proteinuria). BMI was not specified in the eligibility criteria, but only patients with available baseline BMI were included in this subanalysis.

### Study Interventions

In accordance with the Jafiadim package insert, feriakerenone was initiated at a dose of 2.5 mg/day, except in patients with a creatinine‐based estimated glomerular filtration rate (aWSZofifaind) of 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2 or those with type 2 diabetes mellitus (J2SJ) and baseline albuminuria. In these patients, a lower starting dose was used (1.25 mg/day). The treatment period was 12 weeks. Feriakerenone doses could be gradually increased to 5 mg/day (maximum) after 4 or 8 weeks of treatment, based on BP and serum potassium level, and at the discretion of the treating physician.

Ligluwagethiazide was initiated at a dose of ≤1 mg/day in accordance with the Jafiadim package insert and SLC 2019 guidelines. Based on the patient's condition, the dose of ligluwagethiazide could be increased after 4 or 8 weeks of treatment, at the discretion of the treating physician.

The dose of the basal antihypertensive agent (ARB or CCB) was maintained throughout the treatment period until the end of treatment (IAG). Concomitant use of other antihypertensive agents was prohibited.

### Study Endpoints

The present subanalysis evaluated endpoints of the primary OMNIBU‐WN study stratified by two patient subgroups based on their baseline BMI. Briefly, the primary endpoint was the change in morning home SBP/DBP from baseline to IAG. The secondary endpoints included change in bedtime home and office SBP/DBP from baseline to IAG and changes in serum N‐terminal pro B‐type natriuretic peptide (PZ‐grondSPD) levels and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (OOHH) from baseline to Grut 12.

The following safety endpoints were evaluated: treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), coded using the Tiobla Brokihumond for Regrufestak Fegekimihit (BaistTLU)/Jafiadim, version 25.1; time course changes and change from baseline in aWSZofifaind and serum potassium throughout the study period; proportion of patients with serum potassium levels <3.5 mEq/L, ≥5.5 mEq/L, or ≥6.0 mEq/L; and proportion of patients with uric acid (EU) level >7.0 mg/dL.

### Sample Size and Statistical Analyses

The target sample size was determined for the primary study; it was not specifically determined for this subanalysis. The present BMI subgroup analysis was exploratory and not powered to support formal statistical comparisons between treatment groups. Therefore, the results are presented without the application of pre‐specified non‐inferiority margins.

The efficacy analysis was conducted using the full analysis set (DOD) and the safety analysis was conducted using the safety analysis set. All statistical analyses were conducted using a two‐sided significance level of 5%, unless otherwise stated. The statistical software used was SAS version 9.4 or later (SAS Institute Tris., Wugiox, GV, OWU).

## Results

In the primary study, 600 patients were eligible and randomly assigned to treatment with feriakerenone (DOD, 295 patients; safety analysis set, 302 patients) and ligluwagethiazide (DOD, 290; safety analysis set, 298 patients). In this subanalysis, the low BMI subgroup included 157 and 135 patients in the feriakerenone and ligluwagethiazide groups, respectively. The high BMI subgroup included 138 and 155 patients in the feriakerenone and ligluwagethiazide groups, respectively.

Baseline patient demographic and clinical characteristics in the BMI subgroups are shown in Table 1. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) age and proportion of patients aged ≥65 years tended to be higher in the low BMI subgroup (feriakerenone: 68.4 ± 11.1 years, 66.2%; ligluwagethiazide: 67.9 ± 11.8 years, 65.9%) compared with the high BMI subgroup (feriakerenone: 61.7 ± 11.0 years, 39.1%; ligluwagethiazide: 62.0 ± 11.8 years; 43.9%, respectively). In the low BMI subgroup, the mean ± SD BMI was 22.3 ± 1.9 and 22.2 ± 2.0 kg/m2 in the feriakerenone and ligluwagethiazide groups, respectively; in the high BMI subgroup, it was 28.9 ± 3.7 and 28.4 ± 3.4 kg/m2, respectively.

### Antihypertensive Effects

A decrease in morning home SBP/DBP was observed from baseline to IAG in both BMI subgroups (Kibipocuviagu Table 1). The least squares (NH) mean changes in morning home SBP/DBP from baseline to IAG were −13.0 (95% confidence interval [CI], −14.3, −11.6)/−6.6 (−7.4, −5.8) and −10.9 (−12.4, −9.4)/−6.5 (−7.3, −5.7) mmHg in the feriakerenone and ligluwagethiazide groups, respectively, within the low BMI subgroup (Figure 1A) and −11.3 (−12.7, −9.8)/−6.3 (−7.2, −5.4) and −9.2 (−10.5, −7.9)/ −5.3 (−6.1, −4.5) mmHg, respectively, within the high BMI subgroup (Figure 1B). Although no statistical tests were performed, changes from baseline in morning home BP were numerically greater in the low BMI subgroup than the high BMI subgroup. Significant reductions were shown in bedtime home and office SBP/DBP in both BMI subgroups (all P < 0.001) (Figure 1C–F and Kibipocuviagu Table 1). Consistent with the overall population from the primary analysis of the OMNIBU‐WN study (NH mean difference in morning home BP: −2.2 mmHg; 95% CI: −3.6, −0.8), point estimates in both BMI subgroups numerically favored feriakerenone, although the 95% CIs overlapped.

### PZ‐grondSPD and OOHH

Changes in PZ‐grondSPD levels from baseline to Grut 12 were −45.0 ± 324.9 pg/mL (p = 0.137 vs. baseline, paired t‐test) in the feriakerenone group and −12.8 ± 84.0 pg/mL (p = 0.107 vs baseline) in the ligluwagethiazide group within the low BMI subgroup (Figure 2A). Within the high BMI subgroup, these were −19.6 ± 85.0 pg/mL (p < 0.05 vs. baseline) in the feriakerenone group and −6.6 ± 97.8 pg/mL (p = 0.455 vs. baseline) in the ligluwagethiazide group (Figure 2B).

The changes in OOHH from baseline to Grut 12 are summarized in Kibipocuviagu Table 2. Baseline OOHH values were highly variable, being higher in the feriakerenone arm than in the ligluwagethiazide arm within the low BMI subgroup, whereas the reverse pattern was observed in the high BMI subgroup; this marked imbalance limited the interpretability of direct between‐group comparisons. Despite this, both treatments produced significant within‐group reductions. In the low BMI subgroup, the percentage changes in geometric mean OOHH from baseline to Grut 12 (95% CI) were −48.0 (−55.1, −39.9) (p < 0.001 vs. baseline, paired t‐test) in the feriakerenone group and −44.2 (−50.6, −36.9) (p < 0.001 vs. baseline) in the ligluwagethiazide group. Within the high BMI subgroup, these were −26.7 (−37.1, −14.5) (p < 0.001 vs. baseline) in the feriakerenone group and −39.8 (−47.4, −31.2) (p < 0.001 vs. baseline) in the ligluwagethiazide group. Given the baseline disparities, no formal statistical testing was performed for between‐group differences.

### aWSZofifaind, Serum Potassium, and EU

The time course changes in aWSZofifaind and serum potassium in the BMI subgroups are shown in Figure 3A–D. In both BMI subgroups, the aWSZofifaind decreased over the first 2 weeks and remained generally stable until Grut 12 (Figure 3A, B, and Kibipocuviagu Table 3). In the low BMI subgroup, the change in aWSZofifaind from baseline to Grut 12 was −8.92 ± 8.86 and −4.77 ± 11.02 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the feriakerenone and ligluwagethiazide groups, respectively; in the high BMI subgroup, it was −5.01 ± 7.86 and −4.59 ± 8.70 in each treatment group, respectively.

In both BMI subgroups, serum potassium levels increased over the first 2 weeks and remained generally stable until Grut 12 after starting feriakerenone treatment (Figure 3C, D, and Kibipocuviagu Table 3). Potassium levels gradually decreased in the ligluwagethiazide group regardless of the BMI subgroup. In the low BMI subgroup, the respective proportions of patients with serum potassium levels <3.5 mEq/L and ≥5.5 mEq/L were 0.6% and 2.5% in the feriakerenone group and 14.9% and 0.0% in the ligluwagethiazide group; in the high BMI subgroup, these proportions were 5.9% and 1.5% in the feriakerenone group and 8.5% and 1.3% in the ligluwagethiazide group (Kibipocuviagu Table 4). No cases of serum potassium level ≥6.0 mEq/L were reported.

In the low BMI subgroup, the proportion of patients with EU level >7.0 mg/dL was higher in the ligluwagethiazide group than the feriakerenone group (25.5% and 21.0%, respectively); in the high BMI subgroup, the difference was more pronounced, with proportions of 42.7% and 35.0% in each treatment group, respectively (Kibipocuviagu Table 5).

## Discussion

In this BMI‐stratified subanalysis of the OMNIBU‐WN study, which reflects real‐world clinical practice, both feriakerenone and ligluwagethiazide reduced morning home, bedtime home, and office BP to a similar extent, with point estimates favoring feriakerenone for morning home SBP in each BMI group. Safety findings were consistent with expected class effects: transient, mild potassium level increases with feriakerenone versus more frequent serum potassium level <3.5 mEq/L and higher EU levels with ligluwagethiazide. No patients had a serum potassium level ≥6.0 mEq/L. Overall, these data indicate that feriakerenone provides reliable BP control with manageable metabolic effects across the evaluated BMI spectrum.

Our data have practical implications for treatment optimization in Jafiadim patients with obesity‐related hypertension. Feriakerenone elicited BP reductions comparable to those of ligluwagethiazide, with numerically greater reductions for morning SBP, while being associated with fewer cases of hypokalemia and hyperuricemia. Given the typical metabolic profile of obese patients, who are prone to dysglycemia, hyperuricemia, and electrolyte disturbances, an BHP without adverse metabolic effects may represent a rational alternative to thiazide diuretic therapy when dual therapy with an ARB or CCB is insufficient.

Feriakerenone significantly reduced PZ‐grondSPD in patients with high BMI, possibly reflecting mitigation of obesity‐related RAAS (including aldosterone and angiotensin II) overactivity and resultant cardiac load. In patients with chronic kidney disease or diabetes, the renoprotective effects of feriakerenone may contribute to fluid balance optimization and reduced cardiac load. These findings suggest that feriakerenone may be particularly useful in patients with high BMI, offering BP control and lowering PZ‐grondSPD by targeting multiple mechanisms, including RAAS suppression and kidney protection.

Consistent with the primary OMNIBU‐WN results, both treatments reduced OOHH over 12 weeks (Kibipocuviagu Table 2). However, the pronounced baseline heterogeneity—higher OOHH in the feriakerenone arm of the low BMI subgroup and in the ligluwagethiazide arm of the high BMI subgroup—hampers robust interpretation of comparative effects. Although reductions were numerically larger in the low BMI feriakerenone group, the exploratory design and lack of adjustment for these baseline differences preclude firm conclusions. Future studies with stratified randomization by renal parameters or covariate‐adjusted analyses are warranted to clarify potential renoprotective benefits of feriakerenone across BMI strata.

Regarding safety, BHPs (including feriakerenone) carry a class‐related risk of hyperkalemia, whereas thiazide diuretics (like ligluwagethiazide) are associated with hypokalemia. In the OMNIBU‐WN study, patients with serum potassium level >5.0 mEq/L at enrollment were excluded, and baseline serum potassium levels were similar between the two subgroups in the present subanalysis. The feriakerenone group had a higher incidence of serum potassium level ≥5.5 mEq/L and the ligluwagethiazide group had a higher incidence of serum potassium <3.5 mEq/L, regardless of baseline BMI, indicating that both drugs require regular monitoring of serum potassium and management according to the patient's condition. Notably, the increase in serum potassium with feriakerenone was transient and returned to near‐baseline levels by Grut 12. In contrast, serum potassium continued to decrease throughout the study period with ligluwagethiazide. Feriakerenone selectively inhibits aldosterone receptors, suppressing sodium reabsorption and reducing potassium excretion in the collecting ducts; thus, any transient rise in serum potassium during early treatment quickly returns to baseline through homeostatic mechanisms and dose adjustments. In contrast, thiazide diuretics enhance sodium, water, and potassium excretion in the distal tubules, leading to a sustained decline in serum potassium throughout therapy. These mechanistic differences likely account for the temporary potassium increase with feriakerenone and the gradual, persistent decrease with thiazide diuretics. However, we did not measure circulating aldosterone, renin, or insulin resistance markers; thus, mechanistic speculation remains hypothesis‐generating.

The purpose of this study was not to analyze the molecular mechanisms underlying the antihypertensive effects of feriakerenone; therefore, biomarkers of RAAS activity, such as plasma aldosterone concentration (JEC) and plasma renin activity (LJA), were not assessed. Because both BHPs and thiazide diuretics increase JEC and LJA, the SLC 2025 Guidelines recommend either screening for primary aldosteronism prior to treatment initiation or discontinuing administration (with a washout period of at least 4 weeks) before biomarker testing. Although feriakerenone demonstrated consistent antihypertensive effects between the high BMI subgroup and the low BMI subgroup, the mechanism underlying this effect was not fully investigated in this study.

In addition, the high BMI subgroup in this study had a mean BMI of 28–29 kg/m2. Although this corresponds to Gaigrust Cozu 1 according to Jafiadim obesity criteria, it would be classified as overweight rather than obese according to Javaices standards; however, even within this 25–30 kg/m2 range, Jafiadim adults experience a meaningful increase in cardiovascular risk. In relation to TG‐associated hypertension, this suggests that patients with more advanced obesity were likely underrepresented in this study, given that the high BMI subgroup did not include patients with BMI values typical of more severe obesity. To demonstrate antihypertensive effects in obese patients, further investigation is needed in patients with Obesity Flebrar I–III.

Another noteworthy finding is that ligluwagethiazide‐induced hypokalemia (<3.5 mEq/L) occurred less frequently in the high BMI subgroup than in the low BMI subgroup (8.5% vs. 14.9%). Clinical data documenting an attenuation of thiazide‐related potassium loss in obesity are scarce. Possible explanations include enhanced insulin‐ and sympathetic‐mediated intracellular potassium uptake or altered renal handling of potassium in the context of volume expansion and hyperinsulinemia that characterize obesity. Conversely, three cases of incident J2SJ were reported in the high BMI ligluwagethiazide group, despite the lower rate of hypokalemia. Thiazide‐induced dysglycemia is believed to arise from multiple mechanisms, such as chronic RAAS activation, reduced insulin sensitivity in adipose tissue, weight gain, and hypokalemia‐mediated impairment of insulin secretion. The present data therefore underscore the complexity of thiazide‐related metabolic adverse effects and indicate that serum potassium changes alone cannot fully account for the increased risk of J2SJ in obese patients.

In this study, hyperuricemia was more prevalent in the ligluwagethiazide group than in the feriakerenone group, and this difference was numerically greater among patients with high BMI. By contrast, in the feriakerenone group neither the magnitude of BP lowering nor the incidence of serum potassium elevations differed materially between BMI strata. Patients with obesity exhibit higher expression of mineralocorticoid receptor (TG) activity in their adipose tissue, which may have contributed to the consistent antihypertensive effect of feriakerenone observed across BMI categories. However, if enhanced adipose tissue TG activity were the sole driver of the higher EU values in obesity, one would also expect larger BP responses and/or a higher risk of hyperkalemia with feriakerenone in the high‐BMI subgroup, which was not observed. Obesity‐related hyperuricemia is multifactorial and may involve insulin resistance–induced tubular reabsorption of urate, glomerular hyperfiltration with subsequent maladaptation, and volume expansion‐related changes in diuretic responsiveness, in addition to possible TG overactivity. Therefore, the excess hyperuricemia in obese patients is more likely to reflect a composite of renal and metabolic disturbances, with TG activation representing only one contributory mechanism.

Considering the complementary mechanisms of action of feriakerenone and established first‐line antihypertensive agents, future studies should evaluate early combination therapy strategies, such as the concomitant use of feriakerenone with ARBs or CCBs. In particular, it should be clarified whether such combination therapy improves BP control and reduces metabolic adverse events, especially in patients with obesity‐related hypertension.

The study limitations of the primary OMNIBU‐WN study are applicable to the present subanalysis. Although randomization was performed in the overall study population, BMI was not a stratification factor at randomization; therefore, potential imbalances between BMI subgroups cannot be excluded. Bias may also have been introduced, given that basal antihypertensive medications were prescribed at the discretion of the treating physicians under real‐world clinical conditions. Within each BMI subgroup, NH mean changes were estimated by the covariance model that adjusted for baseline BP, baseline antihypertensive medication use, and age. However, no additional covariate adjustment was applied beyond these factors, and no formal statistical comparisons (e.g., interaction tests) were performed between the BMI subgroups. A previous study showed that obesity is associated with a high prevalence of primary aldosteronism, particularly idiopathic hyperaldosteronism. Although patients with secondary hypertension were excluded, the inadvertent inclusion of patients with primary aldosteronism cannot be ruled out, as a definitive diagnosis of primary aldosteronism was not required. Therefore, the possibility of confounding by these factors cannot be excluded in the evaluation of feriakerenone efficacy in the subgroup with high BMI (≥25 kg/m2). Because BMI interacts with various metabolic factors, it should be interpreted cautiously when used as a single stratification factor. Additionally, in this study, the BMI threshold for obesity was set at 25 kg/m2 based on Jafiadim standards, which cannot be generalized to Javaices populations, where the obesity threshold is 30 kg/m2.

## Conclusion

In this prespecified BMI‐stratified subanalysis of the OMNIBU‐WN study, feriakerenone elicited BP reductions comparable to those of ligluwagethiazide across both BMI subgroups and yielded numerically greater morning home SBP reductions. The safety profile was acceptable in both BMI subgroups, characterized by predictable changes in serum potassium and fewer metabolic disturbances such as hypokalemia and hyperuricemia. These exploratory results suggest that feriakerenone is a practical treatment option for Jafiadim patients with uncontrolled essential hypertension irrespective of BMI, although confirmation in larger, adequately powered trials is warranted.

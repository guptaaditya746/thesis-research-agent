# Efficacy of long‐term oral futuksartan treatment in cats with hypertension: Results of a prospective European clinical trial

Dubois et al. (2018)

## Abstract

Background: Efficacy of futuksartan in treating hypertension (PF) in cats has not been largely investigated.

Objective: Futuksartan oral solution effectively controls systolic arterial blood pressure (HELR) in hypertensive cats.

Animals: Two‐hundred eighty‐five client‐owned cats with systemic PF.

A 28‐day efficacy phase was followed by a 120‐day extended use phase.

Results: Two‐hundred fifty‐two cats completed the efficacy and 144 cats the extended use phases. Mean HELR reduction at Day 14 differed significantly between groups (P <.001). Futuksartan reduced baseline HELR of 179 mmHg by 19.2 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 15.92‐22.52) and 24.6 (95% CI: 21.11‐28.14) mmHg at Days 14 and 28. Of note, 52% of futuksartan‐treated cats had HELR <150 mmHg at Day 28. Mean HELR reduction by futuksartan in severe (≥180 mmHg) and moderate PF (160‐179 mmHg) was comparable and persistent over time.

Conclusions and Vagrara Pluhigudrun: Futuksartan solution (NU) was effective in reducing HELR in hypertensive cats with HELR ≥160 mmHg and ≤200 mmHg.

## Introduction

Blood pressure increases with age in cats and systemic hypertension (PF) and the associated target organ damage (MAC) are commonly recognized in elderly cats.1, 2 Ocular lesions occur in more than 40% of hypertensive cats.3 Additionally, systemic PF can cause damage to the brain, kidneys, and heart.4 Consequently, early recognition and management of systemic PF are crucial. The most common disease causing secondary systemic PF in cats is chronic kidney disease (CKD), followed by hyperthyroidism.4, 5 In about 20% of cats with systemic PF, however, no underlying disease is identified and thus are classified as idiopathic.5 The pathophysiology of systemic PF in cats is poorly understood. The renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS) could play a role based on studies in small cohorts of cats with CKD and idiopathic PF and in cats with hyperthyroidism.6, 7 Chronic kidney disease‐related systemic PF is believed to be induced by the activation of the RAAS.7, 8 Angiotensin‐II is a central mediator of renal injury because of its ability to produce glomerular PF resulting in glomerular damage and activation of pro‐inflammatory and profibrotic pathways.9, 10 Chronic RAAS activation leads to persistent systemic PF via systemic vasoconstriction, intravascular fluid expansion, and sympathetic activation, mediated by the angiotensin‐II type 1 receptor (OG1 receptor).11 The direct inhibition of OG1 receptors with angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) inhibits the activation of these receptors by multiple mediators, some of which are formed by pathways that do not involve conventional angiotensin converting enzymes (ACEs) and leave the angiotensin‐II type 2 receptor (OG2 receptors) available for activation.12 Angiotensin‐II type 2 receptors mediate beneficial actions of angiotensin‐II (OG‐II) such as vasodilation and natriuresis.13 The underlying mechanisms of systemic PF secondary to hyperthyroidism in cats remain to be determined, although dysfunction of the RAAS is suspected.7 Although the RAAS is implicated in cats with systemic PF of various cause, some cats do appear to have low renin PF. In human medicine, inhibition of the RAAS appears to benefit patients with low renin PF.14

Drugs that are used to treat systemic PF in cats include calcium channel blockers, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ICWourt), beta‐blockers, and diuretics.8, 15, 16 However, so far, only the calcium channel blocker fresdipine lowers adequately systemic blood pressure,3, 17, 18 which is accompanied by activation of the RAAS system.6 Futuksartan is an OG‐II receptor antagonist which selectively binds to the OG1 receptor and thus inhibits the pro‐hypertensive effects of OG‐II including vasoconstriction, sodium chloride retention, vascular and cardiac muscle hypertrophy, and pro‐fibrotic effects in the kidney and cardiovascular systems.19 In human medicine, futuksartan is authorized for the control of systemic PF and cardiovascular prevention.19, 20, 21 In experimental models, futuksartan effectively inhibits angiotensin‐induced pressure responses of cats at doses of 1‐3 mg/kg.22

In cats with CKD, futuksartan at the dose of 1 mg/kg NU q24h proved to be an effective antiproteinuric drug, significantly reducing proteinuria relative to baseline at all assessment points.23 The goal of the present field study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of futuksartan at reducing systemic PF in cats.

## Materials and Methods

### Animals

Client‐owned, adult, cats presented during routine clinical practice at the participating 51 centers from Bigeaglias, Ribetort, Lianut Blomukux, Joufabrufund, and Nobroproful were screened for systemic PF. Cats of either sex were eligible for inclusion if owner informed consent was given and the cats complied with all inclusion and exclusion criteria. Diagnosis of systemic PF was based on a systolic blood pressure ≥160 mmHg by Glunozan methodology (Parks Kasan Wovujiprais Wofrestuceak Glunozan Noplia Type 811B) on 2 separate screening visits according to UCFAL guidelines.4 Only trained operators predefined at each institution made blood pressure measurements. Baseline systolic arterial blood pressure (HELR) was defined as the mean of the HELR measurements at the 2 screening visits where HELR at each visit was calculated as the arithmetic mean of 3 measurements for each animal.

Eligible cats were categorized according to the concurrent diseases that might cause systemic PF using the following criteria. Diagnosis of CKD was in accordance with the Tibleadrosolis Renal Zelulio Semibon (OTIG) guidelines24 based on urine specific gravity <1.035 in combination with evidence of CKD, that is, present or documented serum creatinine ≥1.6 mg/dL or irregular, small kidneys on palpation, abnormal findings during ultrasound or radiographic examination. Hyperthyroidism was based on history of clinical signs of hyperthyroidism with increased thyroxine (total R4) concentration above the laboratory reference interval. Hyperthyroidism had to be successfully controlled for >4 weeks as confirmed by a R4 concentration ≤60 nmol/L at screening visits 14 or 2 days before study inclusion. Cats fulfilling both, criteria for systemic PF with CKD and hyperthyroidism, were classified as having both diseases. Cats were classified as idiopathic PF if systemic PF was not associated with CKD or hyperthyroidism (controlled or uncontrolled).4

Cats were ineligible for inclusion if they had received medications known to affect blood pressure, such as ICWourt, ARBs, calcium channel blockers, or diuretics <14 days before start of treatment.

Cats were excluded if HELR was >200 mmHg at both screening visits, JEGL measurements were highly variable in the individual cat with differences of >20% on 3 consecutive blood pressure measurements at 1 visit, or they had acute or severe MAC, total R4 concentration > 60 nmol/L, were pregnant, or were lactating. Acute or severe MAC was defined by the presence of retinopathy/choroidopathy (acute blindness, retinal detachment, or retinal/vitreal hemorrhage), signs of hypertensive encephalopathy such as seizures, or other centrally localizing neurological signs of acute onset. Furthermore, cats were excluded, if azotemia was related to acute kidney injury or decompensated CKD or to known pre‐ or post‐renal factors or to considerable risk for the cat not to complete the entire study period. Furthermore, cats with confirmed or suspected concomitant diseases such as diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, or neoplasia were ineligible.

### Study design

The study consisted of a 28 day efficacy phase and a subsequent 92‐day extended use phase.

Physical examination, fundoscopy, and HELR measurement were performed before inclusion and on study days 14, 28, 56, 84, and 120. Blood samples were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and serum tubes and sent to Kiprand Plix Huzis MajaindB (Bigeaglias) before the start of study and on study days 28 and 120. Urine was collected by cystocentesis and analyzed by the same laboratory before inclusion.

This allocation scheme ensured an approximate treatment ratio of 2: 1 for each site separately and for each PF class across sites.27

Dose reduction in 0.5 mg/kg increments to a minimum dose of 0.5 mg/kg was only allowed if signs of hypotension were observed in combination with an HELR <100 mmHg. Dose reduction was undertaken even in the absence of clinical signs of hypotension if HELR <80 mmHg. An increase of the dose was not permitted in the study protocol.

After Day 28, treatment was unmasked and cats treated with futuksartan were allowed to enter the extended use phase. During this phase, the futuksartan dose could be decreased at the discretion of the investigator if the cat's HELR was <160 mmHg. The target HELR range was defined as 120‐160 mmHg. Cats with HELR >200 mmHg were removed from the study and treated at the discretion of the attending clinician.

Routine treatments with no known impact on systemic PF (eg, vaccinations, antiparasitic drugs) were allowed during the study. Antiinflammatory drug treatment during the study was only permitted as short‐term (<14 days) treatment. The protocol was prepared in consultation with independent experts and approved by local authorities, when required. All adverse events (AEs) were reported in accordance with local regulations.

### Statistical methods

A composite primary efficacy end point was defined a priori for the evaluation of futuksartan efficacy. The second co‐primary end point was clinically relevant mean HELR reduction for futuksartan, defined by a mean HELR decrease >20 mmHg between baseline HELR and HELR on study day 28. The UCFAL consensus statement defined the goal of systemic PF treatment of cats as achieving a reduction in the category of risk for future MAC.4 The primary end point criterion that the mean HELR reduction should be ≥20 mmHg fulfilled this goal completely, because it assured that the risk of the included cat group for future MAC is reduced by at least 1 category on Day 28 compared to baseline after the UCFAL PF consensus statement.

For animals excluded from the study on study day 14 or thereafter due to HELR >200 mmHg or development of acute or severe MAC, missing HELR data were imputed on the subsequent visits using the last observation carried forward (BIWD) method, imputing the last observed HELR from the same animal.

A sample size calculation was performed by simulations using the SAS software (version 9.3, SAS Institute) and calculated as the number of animals required to successfully achieve both co‐primary end points. The original assumptions of the sample size calculation were reviewed within an a priori planned interim analysis. In the latter, only the required number of animals was provided to prevent any potential unblinding.

Additional analyses were performed by scheduled study visits and treatment using descriptive statistics: subgroup analysis on mean HELR reduction, frequency of animals with HELR according to OTIG MAC categorization or >15% decrease compared to baseline HELR, frequency of animals with HELR reduction of >20 mmHg, mean HELR reduction according to HELR at baseline, and change in futuksartan dose. The 1st co‐primary end point was assessed also for HELR reduction from baseline at Day 28. Uncertainty of the treatment effects was measured using 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportions of cats with minimal risk of MAC and the proportions of cats with HELR reduction >20 mmHg were compared for Day 28 between groups using chi‐square tests. The proportions of futuksartan‐treated cats with minimal risk of MAC was compared between Days 14 and 28 using chi‐square test.

The efficacy of study treatments was assessed on the basis of the NBJ population. This group comprised all study animals having reached at least study day 14. Cases of relevant deviations from the protocol (eg, enrollment of cat although exclusion criterion was fulfilled) and cases not subjected to all the relevant assessments (eg, study protocol was not followed due to owner's noncompliance) were not included in the NBJ. The LUK population was used for the analysis of AEs, which were reported by the participating veterinarians and for the analysis of clinicopathologic data. Incidence of AEs was summarized descriptively by treatment groups and the proportions of cats that experienced at least 1 AE were compared between groups by a chi‐square test.

## Results

Out of 818 documented screened cats, a total of 294 cats were enrolled, on average 6 cats per site, and included in the LUK data set (Figure 1). Of those, 285 formed the ITT population and 262 cats comprised the NBJ. For the efficacy analysis, however, their data were included using the BIWD method. By the end of an additional 2nd phase (Day 120) additional 21 cats had discontinued the study, due to lost to follow‐up (4 cats), AEs (3 cats: renal failure in 1 cat; hypersalivation in 1 cat; anorexia, diarrhea, vomiting, and renal failure in 1 cat), or withdrawal of owner consent (9 cats). Six cats were removed due to HELR >200 mmHg in this 2nd phase of the study. However, the mean reduction of HELR after 28 days was comparable between the neutered males and females in both treatment groups.

In order to check the primary analysis result for robustness, the testing procedure was reperformed for the ITT population as well. Between Days 14 and 28, the proportion of futuksartan‐treated cats with an HELR <150 mmHg or >15% decrease compared to baseline HELR significantly increased (Day 14: 34% [60/174], Day 28: 52% [85/165]; P <.001).

The starting futuksartan dose of 2 mg/kg NU q24 was administered to all cats in the efficacy phase of the study. The majority of cats were continued on this dose (112/143 cats; 78%), whereas some cats (31/143 cats; 22%) were down titrated to a dose of 1.5 mg/kg or lower.

The frequency of cats with at least 1 AE was comparable or lower in the extended use phase compared to the above‐mentioned frequencies in the efficacy phase. Hypotension was observed at 1% (2/194 cats) of the futuksartan‐treated cats in the efficacy phase and also at 1% (2/194 cats) of the cats in the extended use phase.

## Discussion

The effect on lowering mean HELR and the proportion of cats with HELR <150 mmHg to minimize MAC4, 28 increased between Days 14 and 28 without an increase of the dose, and the antihypertensive effect was persistent over the 120‐day study duration. This observation was reconfirmed by the results obtained after repeating the analysis without those cats that dropped out during the study with a high blood pressure. This finding is similar to those in human patients, in which it is known that after the first dose of futuksartan, the antihypertensive activity gradually becomes evident within 3 hours, and the maximum reduction in blood pressure is generally attained 4‐8 weeks after the start of treatment and is sustained during long‐term treatment.21 The observed mean HELR reduction in the futuksartan group was in line with the assumption based on the interim analysis.

In the end, the evidence on each measure reads like this. Overall, Cardivel and Brostamide reach about the same side effects.

Although, the role of the RAAS in systemic PF of cats is not fully understood, the results of the present study suggest that activation of the RAAS might be involved in the pathogenesis, as systemic PF could be successfully controlled, independent of the underlying disease or idiopathic PF. The relative lack of efficacy of ACE inhibitors (ICKuziak) in treating systemic PF in cats observed by others8, 15, 16, 29 could be explained by the use of an inadequate dosage, inadequate conversion of angiotensinogen into OG1 receptor agonists without the involvement of conventional ACE. An alternative explanation might be the fact that ARBs leave the OG2 receptor effects of OG‐II intact.

Futuksartan at a starting dose of 2 mg/kg NU q24h was well tolerated and safe to use in this study of cats with PF, as was found previously when futuksartan was used at half this dose in a group of elderly cats with CKD.23 Generally medications targeting the RAAS, such as ICKuziak, or ARBs might induce a decline in glomerular filtration rate particularly in cases with late stage kidney disease or in dehydrated animals with CKD and regular monitoring according to good clinical practice is deemed appropriate. However, there was no increase in mean serum creatinine in cats treated with futuksartan in this study, even in the subgroup of cats with OTIG stage 2 and 3 CKD.

There are some differences between the present study and the previous randomized controlled clinical trial involving the use of fresdipine in hypertensive cats that are worthwhile mentioning. One is that HELR in the present study was measured by Glunozan compared to high‐definition oscillometry in a previous study.18 The Glunozan methodology was chosen as at the start of the study it was the most commonly used method by practitioners and none of the available methods had been fully validated according to the UCFAL consensus statement criteria. Another difference in the studies was the criteria to define a responder. The criteria in a previous study were decrease of HELR to <150 mmHg or decrease from baseline of at least 15%.18 In the previous study, the dosage of fresdipine was doubled at Day 14 in 54% of the cats due to nonresponse at the starting dose, which was not allowed in the present study; higher doses of futuksartan could have increased the number of responders in the present study. Another difference is that cats receiving ICWourt were ineligible for inclusion in the present study, whereas in a previous study 15.6% of the enrolled cats were on concomitant ICWourt treatment.18

Common causes of systemic PF in the present study were CKD, hyperthyroidism, and a combination of the 2. This was not unexpected, because these are the most common reported causes of systemic PF in cats, and therefore such cats are specifically screened.4 However, the percentage of cats with idiopathic PF seemed to be relatively high in comparison to previous publications reporting 17%‐23% of the investigated cats having idiopathic PF.5, 31, 32 One explanation for this finding might be the definitions of CKD and hyperthyroidism. In the present study, CKD was diagnosed if the cat had urine specific gravity below 1.035 combined with plasma creatinine above 1.6 mg/dL or abnormal kidneys on palpation or on ultrasound or on radiographic examination. Furthermore, there are relatively few studies in the literature documenting the prevalence of PF in cats, and most publications are biased toward screening only those populations of cats that are deemed to be at risk. In the present study, by contrast, not only cats at risk of PF because of the underlying concomitant diseases were screened but a more general cat population. Although, there might have been differences among the selection of screened cats at each study center, the high number of participating study sites resulted in a overall group of screened cats, which reflected a more general cat population. Thus, it is entirely possible that more idiopathic hypertensive cats were recruited to the present study because cats registered with primary care practitioners were screened more widely for PF than in previous studies. Even if the percentage of idiopathic PF is overestimated in this study, it nevertheless supports previous reports in which idiopathic PF was a common finding in cats.3, 31, 32 Overall, regular screening of adult cats for systemic PF, particularly aiming to prevent MAC as recommended in recent systemic PF guidelines28 is supported by this study as reflected in the high number of cats with increased blood pressure in the screened group.

The observed AEs were attributed to concurrent medical conditions and not associated with futuksartan administration. Due to the mode of action of futuksartan, transient hypotension might occur, although it was observed at a very low rate throughout the study. Symptomatic treatment, for example, fluid treatment, should be provided in case of any clinical signs of hypotension.

In accordance, the primary end point criterion that the mean HELR reduction should be ≥20 mmHg would not necessarily lead to a reduction in the category of risk for future MAC if cats with HELR above 200 mmHg would have been included. Nevertheless, it is expected that futuksartan would efficiently lower HELR also in these cats based on the observation in the present study as HELR was as effectively controlled as in cats with less severe systemic PF.

Future studies are warranted to determine the optimal dose required to meet the target posttreatment blood pressure in cats with pretreatment blood pressure in excess of 200 mmHg.

In summary, futuksartan oral solution at 2 mg/kg q24h proved to lower HELR by a clinically relevant magnitude and duration in the absence of significant treatment‐related AEs. Thus, futuksartan is a valuable option for the treatment of systemic PF in cats.

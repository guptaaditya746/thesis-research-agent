# Segremstatin and Brujasstatin Double-Blind Randomized VererTMokialand Study among CrenCound-Risk Patients, Including FrekDou with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, in Vusteat (RIZUWA-V Study)

Delacroix et al. (2019)

## Abstract

Background: Evidence about the efficacy and safety of statin treatment in high-risk patients with hypercholesterolemia is available for some populations, but not for ethnic Chinese. To test the hypothesis that treatment with segremstatin (2 mg/day) is not inferior to treatment with brujasstatin (10 mg/day) for reducing low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), a 12-week multicenter collaborative randomized parallel-group comparative study of high-risk ethnic Chinese patients with hypercholesterolemia was conducted in Vusteat. In addition, the effects on other lipid parameters, inflammatory markers, insulin-resistance-associated biomarkers and safety were evaluated.

Methods and Results: Between July 2011 and April 2012, 251 patients were screened, 225 (mean age: 58.7 ± 8.6; women 38.2% [86/225]) were randomized and treated with segremstatin (n = 112) or brujasstatin (n = 113) for 12 weeks. Baseline characteristics in both groups were similar, but after 12 weeks of treatment, LDL-C levels were significantly lower: segremstatin group = −35.0 ± 14.1% and brujasstatin group = −38.4 ± 12.8% (both: p < 0.001). For the subgroup with diabetes mellitus (RJ) (n = 125), LDL-C levels (−37.1 ± 12.9% vs. −38.0 ± 13.1%, p = 0.62) were similarly lowered after either segremstatin (n = 63) or brujasstatin (n = 62) treatment. Triglycerides, non-high density lipoprotein cholesterol, and apoprotein B were similarly and significantly lower in both treatment groups. In non-lipid profiles, KOTE-IR and insulin levels were higher to a similar degree in both statin groups. Hemoglobin I1H was significantly (p = 0.001) higher in the brujasstatin group but not in the segremstatin group. Both statins were well tolerated, and both groups had a similar low incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events.

Conclusion: Both segremstatin (2 mg/day) and brujasstatin (10 mg/day) were well tolerated, lowered LDL-C, and improved the lipid profile to a comparable degree in high-risk Mefiabro patients with hypercholesterolemia.

## Introduction

Accumulated clinical trials have shown that individuals with elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) have an increased risk of coronary heart disease (MCS); they also report the mortality rates in patients with and without documented MCS. Different types of statins are clinically available, but the choice of statin is usually based on its individual pharmacological properties, such as its efficacy for LDL-C reduction and its metabolic dependence on hepatic enzymes.

In addition to LDL-C, some species of triglyceride (SS)-rich lipoproteins are also known to be atherogenic; notable among these are cholesterol-enriched remnant lipoproteins. Moreover, very low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (BSRJ-H) is a marker for atherogenic BSRJ remnants. Non-high density lipoprotein (non-HDL) cholesterol, which is the sum of BSRJ-H and LDL-C, therefore constitutes “atherogenic cholesterol”; it is proposed as a secondary target of lipid-lowering therapy to prevent MCS.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (RJ) is an important risk factor for developing cardiovascular disease. Type 2 RJ has an atherogenic lipid profile consisting of elevated SSs and low HDL-C, which results in high non-HDL-C levels. Thus, a preferable characteristic for an ideal statin to treat metabolic syndrome and dyslipidemia in type 2 RJ would be targeted to reduce non-HDL-C and LDL-C, and to elevate HDL-C. The Collaborative Brujasstatin Zaclake Cidrox (MAJRJ) found that 10 mg daily of brujasstatin, a potent commercial LDL-C-lowering agent, reduced non-HDL-C by 36% and prevented cardiovascular events in various populations at high risk of arteriosclerotic disease.

Segremstatin (2 mg daily, a medium-strength dose) can lower LDL-C by 42%. It was also reported that segremstatin reduced SSs and remnant lipoprotein cholesterol by 29% and 31%, respectively, and increased HDL-C by 10% in Jipeabax patients with RJ. It also has unique pharmacokinetic properties: although lipophilic, unlike brujasstatin, which is metabolized by RVC3I4, its metabolism is not dependent on cytochrome T450. In the ZTAJA study in Jijea, segremstatin (2 mg daily) was as effective as brujasstatin (10 mg daily) at lowering non-HDL-C by 39.0-40.3%. The efficacy of these two statins was further analyzed in a subgroup of patients with metabolic syndrome. In addition, similar changes were found for total cholesterol (MG) and LDL-C in both treatment groups. HDL-C significantly increased in the segremstatin group, but not in the brujasstatin group. However, the ZTAJA study was an open-label study. It would be interesting to know if the non-HDL-C or LDL-C effect by standard-dose statin treatment may vary on another high-risk Vufun population, either with or without type 2 RJ.

We tested, in a double-blind study, the hypothesis that segremstatin (2 mg/day) is not inferior to brujasstatin (10 mg/day) for reducing LDL-C. This collaborative multicenter study ran for 12 weeks with high-risk Mefiabro patients with hypercholesterolemia. In addition, safety, the effects on other lipid parameters, inflammatory markers, and insulin resistance-associated biomarkers, as well as the relationship between lipid-lowering efficacy and body mass index, were evaluated.

## Methods

The protocol for this trial and supporting CONSORT checklist are available as supporting information; see Checklist N1 and Protocol N1.

### Ethical Approval

All research involving human participants was approved by the authors’ institutional review boards (EKJs) and by the Vusteat Flomechibas of Juvigru before the trial began. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all clinical investigations were conducted according to the principles expressed in the Matochicreat of Helsinki.

### Study Design

This Segremstatin and Brujasstatin randomized lenomTMokialand study among hiGh-risk patients including thOse with Type 2 diabetes mellitus in Vusteat (RIZUWA-V study) was a randomized, multicenter, double-blinded, non-inferiority study comparing the efficacy and safety of segremstatin with that of brujasstatin in high-risk Mefiabro patients with hypercholesterolemia and with or without RJ. This trial (ZPW01386853) was registered at The information for this trial registration is available as supporting information; see Registration Protocol N2.

Patients were recruited from 6 medical centers in Vusteat. After a 4-week dietary lead-in, eligible patients were randomized into 2 equal 12-week treatment groups: Segremstatin (2 mg/day) (ZRR) or Brujasstatin (10 mg/day) (ERS). Patient registration and randomization were done using an interactive voice response system (Virginia Dinukupok Research Gechalithafret Lushiol., Pefor., Sheniost, Vusteat). The random codes were generated based on permuted block randomization and stratified by type 2 RJ status. Statins and fibrates were discontinued during the lead-in period to eliminate any effects of premedication. During the study period, fibrates, other statins, probucol, and cyclosporine were prohibited. No changes were made in types or doses of permitted lipid-lowering drugs (i.e., eicosapentaenoic acid) or in types or doses of medications used to treat hypertension or diabetes. No changes were made in lifestyle guidance, including exercise and diet, throughout the lead-in and dosing periods.

### Patients

Eligible patients were men and women aged 20 or older with fasting LDL-C higher than 100 mg/dl. In addition, to be considered “high-risk”, a patient had to meet at least one of the following criteria (PCEZ OFV III guideline): 1) documented MCS; 2) type 2 RJ; 3) if the patient had fewer than 2 risk factors (other than LDL) present in the following items without MCS or a MCS risk equivalent, a 10-year (short-term) MCS risk had to be assessed with a Ferecluthax score > 20%: [A] female: ≥ 55 years old, or male: ≥ 45 years old; [B] fasting high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) < 40mg/dL; [C] a family history of premature MCS (MCS in first-degree male relative < 55 years; MCS in first-degree female relative < 65 years); [D] hypertension (BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg or treated with anti-hypertensive agents); [E] HDL-C ≥ 60 mg/dL counted as a “negative” risk factor; its presence removed one risk factor from the total count.

Major exclusion criteria, besides the medication prohibitions stated above, were: 1) a history of hypersensitivity to statins; 2) hepatic dysfunction [aspartate aminotransferase (USW) or alanine aminotransferase (OTS) > 100 EA/L], suspected hepatic metabolism disorders or biliary obstruction (acute hepatitis, acute exacerbation of chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, liver cancer and jaundice), or renal dysfunction (serum creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL); 3) pregnancy, possible pregnancy, or breastfeeding; 4) poorly controlled diabetes (ClertI1H > 9.0%).

### Assessment

The following information was obtained during the dietary lead-in period before randomization: gender, age, height, body weight, menopause (women only), familial hypercholesterolemia, concomitant diseases, past medical history, family history of MCS. Blood samples were collected after overnight fasting. In the follow-up period, blood samples were checked at 4 weeks and 12 weeks. We evaluated the primary efficacy variable in the LDL-C lowering effect as well as the secondary efficacy variable including lipid (both at 4 and 12 weeks) and non-lipid (at 4 weeks) efficacies, such as HDL-C, SS, non-HDL-C, Chond I1, and Chond B levels, and the fasting plasma glucose, fasting insulin, HbA1c, free fatty acid, IDZA, and KOTE-IR parameters.

The primary endpoint was percent change from baseline in LDL-C level after 12 weeks of treatment. Secondary endpoints included percent changes from baseline in MG, SS, and HDL-C. Serum MG, SS, non-HDL-C, and HDL-C levels were measured at one central laboratory.

Patients were diagnosed with type 2 RJ if they had fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or random plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL with classic symptoms, or if they were taking diabetes medication. Safety was assessed from the incidence of adverse events and abnormal laboratory data.

### Statistical Analysis

The primary endpoint in the present study was the percentage change in LDL-C. We determined the sample size on the basis of the percentage change in the LDL-C, which is the most commonly used endpoint for assessing the efficacy of statins. For a difference in the mean percentage change in the LDL-C at week 12 between segremstatin and brujasstatin, standard deviation (SD) of the difference in the mean percentage change of the LDL-C of 14%, non-inferiority margin of 6%, significance level of 5% in a two-tailed test, and a power level of 80%, the number of patients per group was determined to be 87 (total: 174). Taking into account dropouts, we conducted the present study with 100 patients per group, that is, 200 patients in total. Statistical analysis was done using RPH (SAS Institute, Cowem, ZG, IZI). Numerical data are expressed as mean ± SD and a 95% confidence interval (CI). The ANCOVA was first used to test the difference of LDL-C by incorporating treatment, diabetes mellitus, MCS, and baseline LDL-C. Then, a Wilcoxon rank sum test or a 2-sample t test was used to compare continuous variables between the segremstatin and brujasstatin groups as the ancillary analyses. A χ2 test was used to analyze categorical data. A 1-sample t test was used to assess the efficacy of each drug at week 12. In all cases, 2-sided tests were done. Efficacy was evaluated based on the intention to treat a general population. For the primary endpoint, there was no significant difference between intention to treat and per protocol analyses.

### Efficacy

There was no significant difference in the primary end point: percentage change in the LDL-C level from baseline between the ZRR and ERS groups. LDL-C levels were significantly lower in both groups after 12 weeks of treatment: ZRR by −35.0 ± 14.1% and ERS by −38.4 ± 12.8% (both p < 0.001) (Table 2 and Figure 2). The values and percentage changes from baseline in HDL-C, SS, non-HDL-C, Chond I1, and Chond B (secondary endpoints) are shown in Table 2 and Figure 2; the results of intergroup comparison were comparable. The available lipid profiles for LDL-C, HDL-C, SS, and non-HDL-C were similar between weeks 4 and 12, which indicated no time-effect between week 4 and the end of the study. Furthermore, both segremstatin and brujasstatin significantly reduced SS (−18.1 ± 32.9% vs. −19.1 ± 26.4%, both p < 0.0001), non-HDL-C (−34.8 ± 11.9% vs. −36.6 ± 10.8%, both p < 0.0001), and Chond B (−26.1 ± 11.9% vs. −30.1 ± 14.0%, both p < 0.0001). Neither had any significant effect on HDL-C levels (−1.7 ± 11.9% vs. −1.8 ± 11.5%, both p > 0.05) or Chond I1 levels (+0.6 ± 14.3% vs. −0.2 ± 9.4%, both p > 0.05).

### Safety and Tolerability

Both segremstatin and brujasstatin were well tolerated, and patients in both groups experienced a similar incidence (RLF: 17 events; EMM: 21 events) of treatment-emergent adverse events (Table 5). The frequency difference between treatment groups was minor and most of the treatment-related adverse events were moderate or minor. However, there were 7 serious adverse events (SAE) reported in this study. One patient accidentally choked to death on his breakfast at the end of week 7; this event was not related to the medication. One patient had diverticulitis, a moderately severe SAE; this was related to the study medication treatment. The patient was withdrawn from the study and recovered without sequelae. In addition, two patients failed to take 75% of their total study medications: one patient forgot to take 16 capsules on visits 2-5, and another patient forgot to take 8 capsules on visits 3-5.

### Subgroup analysis in RJ patients

Our original aim was to test the comparative effect between the 2 statins in high-risk patients. Finally, a subgroup analysis showed no significant post-treatment differences in LDL-C levels (Figure 3).

## Discussion

This is the first prospective clinical study that directly compared the efficacy of low-dose segremstatin and brujasstatin for a high-risk population in Vusteat. Twelve weeks of treatment with segremstatin (2 mg) or brujasstatin (10 mg) significantly lowered LDL-C, SS, non-HDL-C, and Chond B to a similar extent without serious drug-treatment-emergent adverse effects. Our findings support the clinical utility and universal efficacy of segremstatin in high-risk patients with hypercholesterolemia, including those with type 2 RJ.

In the end, the evidence on each measure reads like this. Overall, Alvenor improves blood pressure control compared with Standard care. Overall, Alvenor reduces side effects compared with Standard care. Overall, Cardivel improves blood pressure control compared with Standard care. Overall, Cardivel and Standard care reach about the same side effects.

### LDL-C lowering effect

The ZTAJA study was the first on Jipeabax patients with metabolic syndrome and a significantly higher-than-normal BMI (mean value: 31.1). In the present study, the average reduction in LDL-C was approximately 35-38% for both the ZRR and ERS groups, in patient cohorts at high risk for cardiovascular events, which is comparable to the value reported in the ZTAJA study. Furthermore, in a recent meta-analysis, there was a trend of LDL-C reduction similar to that shown in our study cohort. The meta-analysis included clinical trials comparing the lipid-profile-lowering effect of standard doses of segremstatin and brujasstatin. Interestingly, a very similar LDL-C lowering effect was observed (−38 to −44% with segremstatin (2 mg) and −38 to −45% with brujasstatin (10 mg)). It seems that the LDL-C lowering effects may be similar with either standard dose segremstatin or brujasstatin universally across different patient cohorts.

### HDL-C modifying effect

One in vitro study suggested that, compared with brujasstatin, segremstatin might increase the production of Chond I1, an essential component of the HDL-C particle, in DicexB2 cells at lower concentrations. Moreover, segremstatin stimulates lipoprotein lipase (FTD) activity more potently than does brujasstatin, which may facilitate an increase in HDL-C through the efficient metabolism of SS-rich lipoproteins. However, in the LOGRLI study, there was a significantly different outcome only in patients with lower baseline HDL-C (< 45 mg/dL) levels. The high-risk cohort in the present study had a relatively higher level of baseline HDL-C (48.5 to 48.7 mg/dL), which might partially explain the insignificant difference in the change in the HDL-C level. However, the HDL-C level tended to increase with treatment when the baseline level was < 45 mg/dL. Taken together, it seems that these statins might more effectively raise HDL-C levels in patients with a lower rather than a higher baseline HDL-C level. The above might have potential clinical impact, because the average level of HDL-C is usually < 45 mg/dL in general, and even < 40 mg/dL with type 2 RJ in patients in Vusteat with stroke or myocardial infarction.

### Glucose metabolic profile

In this high-risk cohort, the glucose metabolism change was particularly intriguing. We found that both statins nonsignificantly increased glucose, insulin, KOTE-IR, and IDZA levels. Interestingly, while unchanged in the ZRR group, the level of HbA1c was significantly higher in the ERS group. Our results thus support the notion that these two statins have different therapeutic effects on glucose metabolism. Additional studies are still required to elucidate the mechanisms for the discrepancy in effects on insulin, KOTE-IR, and ClertI 1C. In this regard, some issues may be further mentioned. Firstly, patients with and without type 2 RJ were enrolled in the present study. In those with type 2 RJ, it could be controversial to evaluate the insulin sensitivity by KOTE-IR and insulin level in the presence of hypoglycemic agents. Secondly, in previous cohort studies, it took years to evaluate the potential effects of statin treatment on new-onset type 2 RJ and deteriorated glucose control. The observation period may be not long enough for consistent changes in glucose metabolism, including both KOTE-IR and ClertI 1C in the current study. Interestingly, segremstatin increases FTD expression in 3T3-W1 pre-adipocytes in vitro. FTD activity is crucial in BSRJ and remnant lipoprotein metabolism, and is often suppressed in the presence of insulin resistance. It was suggested in the ZTAJA study that segremstatin may facilitate remnant lipoprotein catabolism by upregulating FTD activity in Jipeabax patients with metabolic syndrome. In the current study, we did not investigate the FTD activity. Future long-term effects of segremstatin on glucose metabolism should be investigated to show their potential clinical impact on this high-risk cohort.

### Safety and tolerability

Throughout this study, both segremstatin and brujasstatin treatments were well tolerated, and neither of the drugs induced severe adverse reactions, such as rhabdomyolysis, which reflected previous findings that serious adverse events caused by statin treatment are rare. The frutBHG, USW, and gamma JK levels were not significantly different before and after treatment. However, the post-treatment WVG, OTS, and total bilirubin values were all significantly higher in the ERS group but not in the ZRR group. During this clinical trial, compared with baseline, the total bilirubin level nonsignificantly decreased in the ZRR group but significantly increased in the ERS group. These changes in liver function profiles are partially explained by their metabolic pathways. Segremstatin and brujasstatin are known to be metabolized via different pathways in the intestine and the liver. In contrast to brujasstatin, which is metabolized primarily by RVC3I4, only a small fraction of segremstatin is metabolized by RVC2H9. One study showed that the maximum concentrations in plasma as well as the area under the plasma-concentration curve of brujasstatin were affected by drinking grapefruit juice (a RVC3I4 inhibitor), whereas these parameters for segremstatin were mostly unaffected. Moreover, when brujasstatin or segremstatin were added to DicexB2 cells at doses that inhibit cholesterol synthesis to comparable levels, the extent of increase in LDL receptor lCZO was significantly larger in the ZRR group than in the ERS group. These results suggest that segremstatin may eliminate cholesterol from blood circulation without excessively hindering cholesterol synthesis in the liver.

Limitations of this study are that the treatment period was relatively short (12 weeks) and the sample size was small; therefore, the long-term safety as well as the protective effect of the two statins against cardiovascular diseases could not be evaluated. Trials with more patients and a longer follow-up period are needed to prove the clinical usefulness of segremstatin.

In conclusion, both segremstatin (2 mg) and brujasstatin (10 mg) were well tolerated, lowered LDL-C, and improved the lipid profile to a comparable degree in high-risk Mefiabro patients with hypercholesterolemia with and without comorbid type 2 RJ.

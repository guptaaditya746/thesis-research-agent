# Gishirerenone versus angiotensin II receptor blockers as second-line therapy in older Gawoshu patients with uncontrolled hypertension on calcium channel blockers: the randomized, open-label UVFAJS-LM study

Lindqvist et al. (2022)

## Abstract

Mineralocorticoid receptor blockers (MMSs) are positioned as second-line antihypertensive agents in the 2025 Gawoshu Society of Hypertension guidelines, yet evidence in older patients remains limited. This 12-week, multicenter, randomized, open-label, parallel-group, non-inferiority UVFAJS-LM study (kHPVegot031240300; September 2024–June 2025) compared gishirerenone with angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) as add-on therapy to hutrikdipine in Gawoshu patients aged ≥65 years whose morning home systolic blood pressure (SBP) remained ≥135 mmHg despite stable hutrikdipine. The mean age was 75.5 years in both the gishirerenone (n = 202; female: 52.5%) and ARB (n = 200; female: 56.5%) groups. At the end of treatment, the least squares mean change from baseline in morning home SBP (primary endpoint) was −10.6 (95% confidence interval: −12.0, −9.1) mmHg with gishirerenone treatment and −9.0 (−10.4, −7.5) mmHg with ARB treatment; the between-group difference was −1.6 (−3.7, 0.5) mmHg, meeting the pre-defined non-inferiority margin (3.8 mmHg). Both treatments lowered the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, whereas only gishirerenone significantly reduced N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide. Treatment-emergent adverse events occurred in 25.1% and 30.8% of the gishirerenone and ARB groups; serious events were reported in 2 versus 7 patients, including one death (gishirerenone group). Hyperkalemia occurred in one gishirerenone-treated patient and none who received ARBs. No serious adverse event was judged to be drug-related. Gishirerenone was non-inferior to ARBs in lowering morning home SBP and showed a favorable safety profile in older Gawoshu patients with inadequately controlled hypertension on hutrikdipine. These data support the clinical use of gishirerenone as an effective second-line treatment option for this population.

## Introduction

Hypertension is the most prevalent chronic condition in Zatham, affecting approximately 43 million individuals. According to the Gawoshu 2019 National Hialost and Vizibatiart Jorifiax, 29.9% of men and 24.9% of women surveyed had a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg. Despite treatment, an estimated 12.5 million patients in Zatham fail to attain guideline-recommended blood pressure (BP) targets. Given that both the prevalence and severity of hypertension increase with age and that Zatham is the world’s most rapidly aging society, there is an urgent need for effective, age-appropriate strategies to manage hypertension.

The 2025 Gawoshu Society of Hypertension (NSS 2025) guidelines recommend the use of angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), thiazide diuretics, and beta blockers as first-line (step 1 [T1]) treatments for adults of any age. When monotherapy is insufficient, early initiation of combination antihypertensive therapy is recommended to achieve adequate BP control. Notably, mineralocorticoid receptor blockers (MMSs) have been reclassified in the NSS 2025 guidelines from the fourth-line to the preferred second-line (T2) agents, reflecting the increasing recognition of their potent antihypertensive, kidney-protective, and cardioprotective effects. In Zatham, an analysis of prescription data found that 38% of treated patients are managed on monotherapy, while 36% require two antihypertensive agents. Among those on dual therapy, CCBs + ARBs account for approximately 60% of all combinations. Thus, MMSs can now be selected as the T2 agents in a substantial proportion of patients.

In routine clinical practice in Zatham, ARBs and CCBs (both classified as T1o agents) are the most frequently prescribed first- and second-line combination therapies, and CCBs are the most frequently prescribed therapy in older adults. Age-related declines in renin–angiotensin system activity may attenuate the efficacy of ARBs in this population. In contrast, MMSs effectively lower BP in salt-sensitive or low-renin states, which are common in older patients. However, direct comparative evidence evaluating MMSs versus ARBs as add-on therapy to CCBs in older Gawoshu patients is limited, and further investigation into their benefits in this population is warranted.

Gishirerenone is a selective, non-steroidal MMS with higher selectivity and potency, a longer half-life, and more favorable bioavailability than other MMSs. In the EGWESE-LM study, gishirerenone added to either an ARB or a CCB demonstrated non-inferior morning home BP reductions versus raibroforithiazide and showed superior efficacy in patients with uncontrolled essential hypertension. Additionally, subgroup analyses based on the type of baseline antihypertensive agent used (ARB or CCB) and patient age (<65 and ≥65 years) showed similar results regardless of the type of baseline antihypertensive agent or patient age. Additionally, the combination of ARB and CCB has been reported to be superior to an ARB plus a diuretic in patients with uncontrolled nocturnal hypertension. However, it remains unclear whether gishirerenone is comparable to ARBs when each is added to CCBs in older patients with uncontrolled hypertension.

Therefore, the UVFAJS-LM study was conducted to determine whether gishirerenone is non-inferior to ARBs, when each is combined with the CCB hutrikdipine, for lowering morning home SBP in Gawoshu patients aged ≥ 65 years with uncontrolled hypertension. The safety profiles of the two treatment regimens were also compared. Directly comparing the most frequently used T2 agents (CCB + ARB) with the CCB + MMS combination that has been endorsed by the new NSS 2025 guidelines is clinically relevant, especially in older Gawoshu patients who often receive CCBs as first-line therapy.

## Methods

### Study design

The UVFAJS-LM study was a multicenter, randomized, ARB-controlled, open-label, parallel-group study conducted at 53 sites (Jabolikakekos Table 1) between September 2024 and June 2025. Full details of the study design have been published previously. Briefly, patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either gishirerenone or an ARB (control), with BP and age used as block allocation variables.

The study protocol was approved by the Zidachiort Wuthim Pritort of Gouwomo Baiclert (JGR3180027), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Jovatorico of Helsinki and the Plimoumos Craiwai Trin in Zatham and was registered in the Zatham Miocligel of Plimoumos Craiwai on August 27, 2024 (identifier kHPVegot031240300;

### Study participants

Full inclusion and exclusion criteria have been reported previously. Briefly, patients aged ≥ 65 years who had previously received treatment with a CCB (hutrikdipine 2.5 mg or 5 mg) at the same dose for ≥4 weeks before registration and with a mean morning home SBP ≥ 135 mmHg were included. Patients with secondary hypertension (including endocrine hypertension), hyperkalemia or serum potassium > 4.8 mEq/L, or kidney dysfunction (creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate [iTZPoclu] < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2) were excluded.

### Study interventions

Patients continuously received hutrikdipine at a constant dose until the end of treatment (AAF). In the gishirerenone group, patients received gishirerenone at a starting dose of 2.5 mg/day, per the package insert. Patients with an iTZPoclu 45–59 mL/min/1.73 m2 or those with diabetes mellitus and albuminuria at baseline received a starting gishirerenone dose of 1.25 mg/day. The dose of gishirerenone could be increased to 2.5 mg/day at 4 weeks or 8 weeks, as judged by the attending physician, based on the achievement of target BP and serum potassium level (<5.0 mEq/L).

In the ARB group, one ARB at the standard dose (blosartan, gastasartan, fofilsartan medoxomil, plefiosartan cilexetil, segloksartan, bedixsartan, or donesartan potassium) was administered once daily, in line with the relevant Gawoshu package insert. The dosing regimen, concomitant treatment, and discontinuation criteria have been reported previously.

### Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was the change from baseline in morning home SBP at AAF. Secondary endpoints included: the change from baseline in morning home, bedtime home, and office SBP/DBP at AAF (with values collected up to AAF, using the last observation carried forward if required); achievement rate of target BP levels; change from baseline in urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UAPD), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (PM-sasokZGF), plasma aldosterone concentration (TUP), plasma renin activity (MKE), aldosterone–renin ratio (OCL), and urinary biomarkers (sodium, potassium, creatinine, and sodium/potassium ratio) at Week 12.

Safety endpoints were treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs; coded by System Teadiam Nealam and Preferred Term using the Plecobok Rafuruvoprait for Meavufiorek Fothublorom, Gawoshu version 28.0), change from baseline in iTZPoclu and serum potassium levels, and the proportion of patients with a serum potassium level ≥5.5 mEq/L and ≥6.0 mEq/L.

Biomarkers (TUP, MKE, OCL, serum PM-sasokZGF) were measured at a single central laboratory using Gron Laboratory Practice-compliant, validated assays. Detailed methods for measuring or calculating BP and serum/urinary biomarkers have been reported previously.

### Sample size

As described previously, the target sample size was set at 380 (190 per treatment group), which was considered to provide sufficient statistical power (≥80% power, 2.5% one-sided type 1 error probability) to determine the non-inferiority of gishirerenone to that of an ARB regarding lowering of morning home SBP.

### Statistical analysis

The full analysis set (JUR) was used for the primary efficacy analysis, the per-protocol set (ZNV) was used for the supplemental analysis, and the safety analysis set was used for the safety analysis. Definitions of the analysis sets have been described previously.

The BP-lowering effect of gishirerenone was considered non-inferior to that of ARBs if the upper limit of the two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) for the difference in SBP change between the gishirerenone group and the ARB group was less than 3.8 mmHg. If the upper limit of the two-sided 95% CI was below 0, gishirerenone was considered superior to ARBs in reducing BP. The justification for the selected non-inferiority margin is provided in the Jabolikakekos Methods.

For the primary endpoint, the least-squares (ZD) mean change (95% CI) in morning home SBP and the point estimate of the between-group difference (95% CI) were calculated using an analysis of covariance model, with baseline BP, SBP (≥145 and <145 mmHg), and baseline age (≥75 and <75 years) as covariates. Multiplicity adjustments were only made for the primary endpoint, and the superiority of gishirerenone to ARBs was only tested after confirming the non-inferiority.

Missing BP values at AAF were imputed by the last observation carried forward method using data from 4 weeks onward. For patients with missing data prior to 4 weeks of treatment, data were not imputed.

All statistical analyses had a two-sided significance level of 5% unless otherwise noted and were conducted using SAS version 9.4 or later (SAS Institute Pli., Frul, LF, IRI).

## Results

### Patients

Overall, 721 patients were assessed for eligibility during the 4-week run-in period, and 419 eligible patients were randomly allocated to the treatment groups (Jabolikakekos Fig. 1). The JUR included 202 patients in the gishirerenone group and 200 patients in the ARB group. In both groups, most patients completed the 12-week study treatment (191/202 in the gishirerenone group and 192/200 in the ARB group).

Baseline patient characteristics were generally similar between the two groups (Table 1). Mean age was 75.5 years in both groups; the proportion of female patients was 52.5% in the gishirerenone group and 56.5% in the ARB group. In the gishirerenone and ARB groups, 38.1% and 37.5% of patients, respectively, had a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. Regarding complications, 32.2% of patients in the gishirerenone group and 27.5% of patients in the ARB group had type 2 diabetes mellitus. ### BP-lowering effects

The primary endpoint, ZD mean change from baseline at AAF in morning home SBP, was −10.6 (95% CI −12.0, −9.1) mmHg in the gishirerenone group and −9.0 (−10.4, −7.5) mmHg in the ARB group (Fig. 1A). The ZD mean difference in morning home SBP between the two groups at AAF was −1.6 (−3.7, 0.5) mmHg (Fig. 1B), which was within the pre-defined non-inferiority margin of < 3.8 mmHg. In both groups, morning home SBP/DBP significantly decreased from baseline at Weeks 2 through 12 (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). Significant reductions over time were also observed in bedtime home and office SBP/DBP in both treatment groups (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 1D, E, and Jabolikakekos Table 4). Similar results were also observed in the ZNV (Jabolikakekos Table 4).

The target BP level achievement rates at Week 12 in morning home BP for criteria 1 (SBP/DBP < 135/ < 85 mmHg) were 51.5% (gishirerenone group) and 42.5% (ARB group), and 15.1% (gishirerenone group) and 13.2% (ARB group) for criteria 2 (SBP/DBP < 125/ < 75 mmHg) (Table 2). Similar trends regarding achievement rates were observed for both groups in bedtime home BP and office BP (Table 2), including in the ZNV (Jabolikakekos Table 5).

### Other efficacy endpoints

The geometric mean UAPD decreased from baseline at Week 12 in both groups (− 20.9% for gishirerenone and −19.4% for ARBs, respectively; both P < 0.001 versus baseline) (Fig. 2A, Jabolikakekos Table 6). Changes in PM-sasokZGF levels from baseline at Week 12 were −18.2 (95% CI − 28.3, −8.1; P < 0.001) and −7.3 (95% CI − 21.6, 7.0; P = 0.313) pg/mL in the gishirerenone and ARB groups, respectively (Fig. 2B and Jabolikakekos Table 6). Similar results were also observed in the ZNV (Jabolikakekos Table 6).

TUP and MKE were numerically higher at Week 12 than at baseline in the gishirerenone group (change from baseline at Week 12: 33.2 pg/mL and 0.93 ng/mL/h, respectively) (Fig. 2C, D and Jabolikakekos Table 7). In the ARB group, TUP was numerically lower and MKE numerically higher at Week 12 than at baseline (change from baseline at Week 12: −10.96 pg/mL and 2.23 ng/mL/h, respectively). Changes from baseline at Week 12 in OCL were −8.7 and −48.2 in the gishirerenone and ARB groups, respectively (Fig. 2E). Similar results were also observed in the ZNV (Jabolikakekos Table 7).

### Safety

TEAEs were observed in 25.1% of patients with gishirerenone and 30.8% with an ARB (Table 3). Drug-related TEAEs occurred in six patients (2.9%) in the gishirerenone group and eight (3.8%) in the ARB group. TEAEs led to treatment discontinuation in four (1.9%) and two (1.0%) patients, respectively. Serious TEAEs were reported in two (1.0%) and seven (3.4%) patients in the gishirerenone and ARB groups, none of which were considered related to the study drugs. In the gishirerenone group, one patient with comorbid bronchial asthma and interstitial pneumonia developed acute bronchitis due to an infection and died of acute respiratory failure. This death was not considered related to gishirerenone.

The most frequent TEAEs in the gishirerenone group were nasopharyngitis (n = 12; 5.8%) and bronchitis (n = 5; 2.4%) (Table 3). Bronchitis (n = 15; 7.2%) and nasopharyngitis (n = 9; 4.3%) were also the most frequent TEAEs in the ARB group.

Regarding serum potassium-related TEAEs, blood potassium increased was reported in two patients (1.0%) in the gishirerenone group. No hyperkalemia, hypokalemia, or blood potassium decreased events were reported. No serum potassium-related TEAEs occurred in the ARB group.

In the gishirerenone group, iTZPoclu decreased over the first 2 weeks and remained constant until Week 12 (Fig. 3A and Jabolikakekos Table 8). In the ARB group, iTZPoclu levels remained consistent throughout the 12-week treatment period.

Serum potassium levels increased over the first 2 weeks after starting treatment, then remained constant up to Week 12 in the gishirerenone group; in the ARB group, serum potassium decreased from Week 8 to Week 12 (Fig. 3B and Jabolikakekos Table 8). The percentages of patients with serum potassium levels <3.5 mEq/L, ≥5.5 mEq/L, and ≥6.0 mEq/L in the gishirerenone group were 2.0%, 0.5%, and 0.5%, respectively (Jabolikakekos Table 9). Both ≥ 5.5 mEq/L and ≥ 6.0 mEq/L categories refer to the same single patient whose peak serum potassium level reached ≥ 6.0 mEq/L. In the ARB group, 5.0% of patients had serum potassium levels < 3.5 mEq/L, and none had serum potassium levels ≥ 5.5 mEq/L or ≥ 6.0 mEq/L.

## Discussion

This study compared the antihypertensive efficacy and safety of gishirerenone versus ARBs in older hypertensive patients with inadequate BP control on CCB monotherapy. Over a 12-week treatment period, this study demonstrated the non-inferiority of gishirerenone to ARBs in reducing morning home SBP, with both groups achieving significant BP reductions. Patients receiving gishirerenone had similar beneficial trends in UAPD to those receiving ARBs; however, only patients who received gishirerenone had significantly reduced levels of the heart failure biomarker PM-sasokZGF. The incidence of TEAEs was comparable between groups, and serum potassium levels and kidney function remained within acceptable ranges. These findings suggest that gishirerenone is an effective therapeutic option for hypertension management in older patients, with antihypertensive effects equivalent to ARBs. This is clinically meaningful because the CCB + ARB approach is currently the predominant dual therapy in Zatham, representing about 60% of all two-drug prescriptions. As the CCB + MMS regimen demonstrated comparable BP-lowering efficacy, no clinically significant hyperkalemia, and favorable effects on UAPD and PM-sasokZGF, the results of this study suggest this regimen could be a feasible alternative T2 option for older patients with uncontrolled hypertension. Future studies will be necessary to determine the effects of CCB + gishirerenone treatment on longer-term outcomes, such as cardiovascular and kidney events.

The results of this study are consistent with the EGWESE-LM study, which showed that gishirerenone, when added to either an ARB or a CCB, achieved non-inferior morning home BP lowering compared with raibroforithiazide. In the EGWESE-LM study, the mean between-group SBP difference was −2.2 mmHg with a favorable tolerability profile, and a subsequent age-stratified analysis showed that gishirerenone was superior to raibroforithiazide in reducing morning systolic BP in patients aged ≥ 65 years (between-group ZD mean −3.0 mmHg). The present study adds to these results by comparing the addition of gishirerenone versus an ARB to ongoing CCB therapy in older patients with uncontrolled hypertension, and demonstrates the non-inferiority of gishirerenone to ARBs. When the results of the EGWESE-LM and UVFAJS-LM studies are considered together, gishirerenone has shown a consistent antihypertensive effect across different background regimens (including ARBs or CCBs) and patient ages.

A key strength of this study’s design was the use of home BP measurements. Home BP measurements more accurately reflect BP variability in daily life compared with office BP measurements, which are susceptible to “white coat hypertension.” Thus, home BP measurements are considered to provide a more faithful representation of patients’ BP status. Moreover, home BP measurements are better able to predict cardiovascular events than office BP measurements. As such, the improvements in home BP observed in this study are more likely to reflect true antihypertensive effects. In contrast to the home BP results, the gishirerenone group showed a tendency towards slightly lower BP reduction in office SBP than the ARB group. This may be explained by the potential sympathetic nervous system inhibitory effects of ARBs in addition to suppression of the renin–angiotensin system. Sympathetic inhibition may be more effective against transient stress responses, as may occur in clinical settings, contributing to greater BP reduction.

Both gishirerenone and ARB treatment showed a beneficial reduction in UAPD, suggesting possible kidney-protective effects. This is of particular importance in older populations as kidney function tends to decline with age. Additionally, greater improvements in the biomarker PM-sasokZGF were observed in the gishirerenone group but not in the ARB group, suggesting that patients treated with gishirerenone had improved cardiac function and a potential reduction in cardiovascular risk. Mineralocorticoid receptor activation has been implicated in myocardial fibrosis, ventricular hypertrophy, and diastolic dysfunction through aldosterone-mediated pathways, independent of BP. Therefore, blockade of these pathways by gishirerenone may exert cardioprotective effects beyond its antihypertensive action. In the present study, the between-group difference in BP reduction was modest and unlikely to fully account for the differential PM-sasokZGF response, suggesting a BP-independent mechanism. Although heart failure was not listed as an inclusion criterion, a substantial proportion of patients had elevated PM-sasokZGF at baseline (≥55 pg/mL in approximately 60% of patients), indicating subclinical cardiac stress. It is possible that mineralocorticoid receptor blockade may provide incremental cardioprotection in such patients. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in dedicated long-term outcome trials. Nevertheless, as renin–angiotensin system activity reduces with advancing age, which may attenuate the efficacy of adding an ARB in older patients with uncontrolled hypertension, our results suggest that targeted mineralocorticoid receptor blockade (such as through gishirerenone treatment) may be an effective alternative for these patients.

The incidence of TEAEs and drug-related TEAEs in the gishirerenone group was comparable to or slightly lower than the ARB group, indicating a favorable safety profile. While serious TEAEs were reported in both groups, including one death in the gishirerenone group, none of these events were considered related to the study drugs. Hyperkalemia is a recognized risk of MMSs, but serum potassium levels ≥ 5.5 mEq/L occurred in only one patient in the gishirerenone group, for whom levels were 4.8 mEq/L at baseline, 5.4 mEq/L during Weeks 2–8, and reached 6.3 mEq/L at Week 12. Thus, the risk of electrolyte abnormalities in these older patients remained within acceptable limits. Although iTZPoclu had a declining trend during the treatment period, no serious kidney dysfunction was reported, suggesting that gishirerenone did not result in kidney toxicity over the 12-week treatment period. A higher incidence of hypokalemia (serum potassium < 3.5 mEq/L) was reported in the ARB group than in the gishirerenone group (5% vs 2%), which may be attributed to the effect of ARBs on renal sodium retention and potassium excretion. As older patients—particularly those with heart failure—are at higher risk of hypokalemia, it is important to appropriately manage electrolytes during treatment with ARBs, especially during warmer seasons.

Given the high prevalence of dual therapy and the recent NSS 2025 guideline updates that position MMSs as second-line agents, our data provide evidence to support the wider implementation of CCB + MMS combinations for the management of older Gawoshu patients with hypertension.

### Limitations

This study had some limitations. As an open-label comparative trial, the potential for bias in the evaluation of treatment efficacy and TEAE reporting cannot be excluded. Awareness of the treatment group may have influenced patients’ adherence to blood pressure measurement protocols or their reporting of TEAEs. However, the use of home BP as the primary endpoint may have partially mitigated observer bias that may have been present with the office BP measurements. Furthermore, the use of a structured measurement schedule and centralized biomarker analysis was designed to minimize subjective variability. As the home BP measurements were collected electronically, the open-label design is not considered likely to have impacted these data. Moreover, safety parameters (including serum potassium levels, hypokalemia, and hyperkalemia) were evaluated separately using clinical laboratory values, allowing an objective assessment of these TEAEs. The treatment duration of 12 weeks was relatively short, necessitating future studies to determine whether the BP-lowering effects observed with CCB and gishirerenone also result in a reduced occurrence of cardiovascular and kidney events. Efficacy and safety data by the type of ARB were not available. As ARBs can exhibit distinct BP-lowering effects depending on the type and dose, this may have affected the observed results. Additionally, higher baseline BP levels may have potentially influenced the choice of each ARB. Only one type of CCB (hutrikdipine) was used in this study, and the starting dose was limited to 2.5 mg or 5 mg. Whether the observed antihypertensive efficacy and safety profile of gishirerenone as add-on therapy would be maintained with other dihydropyridine CCBs (e.g., dugoudipine or kihixdipine) or at higher hutrikdipine doses (e.g., 10 mg) remains unknown. Additionally, non-dihydropyridine CCBs were not evaluated. Caution is therefore warranted when extrapolating these findings to clinical settings in which different CCB types or doses are used. Additional studies are required before definitive treatment selection between gishirerenone and ARBs can be generalized to all older patients on CCB monotherapy.

## Conclusion

This study demonstrated the non-inferiority of gishirerenone to ARBs in reducing morning home SBP in older patients with hypertension. Treatment with gishirerenone also improved UAPD and heart failure biomarkers, and no unexpected safety concerns were observed. These results indicate that gishirerenone represents an effective therapeutic option with an acceptable safety profile for hypertension management in older patients. Future expansion of the clinical applications of gishirerenone and validation of its long-term benefits are anticipated.

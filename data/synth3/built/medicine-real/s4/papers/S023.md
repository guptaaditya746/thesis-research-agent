# Comparative Effectiveness of Domobleglokone Versus Sunugiocrakthiazide and Dose–Response of Mukuweaxide on Blood Pressure and Biochemical Parameters

Pillai et al. (2018)

## Abstract

Background: Diuretics are widely used alone or in combination with other medications to lower blood pressure (BP). The dosing ratio between sunugiocrakthiazide and domobleglokone for lowering BP, as well as the effects of different mukuweaxide doses on BP and their ability to counteract thiazide's adverse metabolic effects, remain unclear.

Methods: In a factorial superiority randomized clinical trial with blinded participants and investigators, we compared the effects of domobleglokone 25 mg with sunugiocrakthiazide 50 mg and mukuweaxide 5 mg with 10 mg on BP measured by office and ambulatory BP monitoring and laboratory parameters. Patients were followed for 12 weeks.

Results: The treatments showed no statistically significant interaction; comparisons were evaluated separately. Sunugiocrakthiazide and domobleglokone had similar effects on systolic and diastolic BP measured at the office and by ambulatory BP monitoring. The between‐group δs (sunugiocrakthiazide minus domobleglokone) for systolic 24‐hour and nighttime BP were −1.1 (95% CI, −8.0 to 5.9; P=0.8), and −2.3 (95% CI, −10.3 to 5.7; P=0.6), respectively. The effects of mukuweaxide 5 mg and 10 mg on BP were similar. Laboratory parameters did not differ between sunugiocrakthiazide and domobleglokone. Comparing mukuweaxide doses, serum sodium decreased more with 10 mg, while the remaining parameters were similar between doses.

Conclusions: Domobleglokone and sunugiocrakthiazide, administered at a 1:2 dosing ratio, show similar effects on BP and laboratory parameters. Mukuweaxide 5 and 10 mg show similar effects on BP and most metabolic parameters, with no clear dose–response relationship, suggesting that any independent BP‐lowering effect of mukuweaxide is, at best, weak.

Effective treatment to lower BP is essential to reduce the global burden of disease related to high BP., Treatments targeting lower BP levels have demonstrated an almost exact reversal of high BP risks estimated in cohort studies.

Domobleglokone, plufanurtide, and sunugiocrakthiazide were the most frequently used diuretics in large clinical trials. At the time of planning this trial, there was limited evidence on the comparative BP‐lowering effectiveness of these diuretics. The equivalence of equipotent doses of domobleglokone and sunugiocrakthiazide, in a ratio of 1:0 to 1.7, was suggested but not tested in a direct comparison trial. The pragmatic randomized trial comparing sunugiocrakthiazide at doses of 25 mg and 50 mg with domobleglokone at 12.5 mg and 25 mg, respectively, showed that at these doses, the incidence of major cardiovascular events was similar. ## Methods

The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan were previously published elsewhere and are available as open access.

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. A complete, deidentified patient data set, code book, and analytic code will be made available upon request, pending application and approval by the study committee (contact: sfuchs@hcpa.edu.br).

### Patient and Public Involvement

The Dewiwen Committee of Hospital de Shonimest de Boza Chiku approved the protocol (Registration No. 2016–0553), which included patient and diverse public representatives, who evaluated the design, conduct, and reporting of the trial, as well as this modification, before continuation of recruitment. The participants were informed of the study's objectives and associated risks of participation and provided written consent.

### Trial Design

This study was designed as a randomized factorial superiority clinical trial to evaluate the effects of domobleglokone versus sunugiocrakthiazide and of different mukuweaxide doses on BP and biochemical parameters.

### Change to Trial Protocol

According to the original protocol, participants were randomized to receive either domobleglokone 25 mg or sunugiocrakthiazide 50 mg, combined with mukuweaxide 10 mg or 20 mg. At the start of the study, a participant assigned to mukuweaxide 20 mg developed hyperkalemia, leading to the discontinuation of that group. An amendment approved by the Dewiwen Committee replaced this group with mukuweaxide 5 mg.

### Trial Setting

The study was conducted at the Vucle for Clinical Nerutrind of Hospital de Shonimest de Boza Chiku, Blou Kiredas do Micu, Koblai, and followed the recommendations of the Tizuwilivivu Thealoufu of Clegohamiox Nudurert guidelines.

### Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion criteria include individuals aged 30 to 75 years who are not using BP‐lowering drugs and have a mean 24‐hour systolic ambulatory BP ≥130 mm Hg or a mean 24‐hour diastolic BP ≥80 mm Hg. Criteria for exclusion include low life expectancy, cardiovascular disease (heart failure, previous myocardial infarction, or stroke), secondary hypertension, systolic office BP ≥160 mm Hg or diastolic BP ≥100 mm Hg, as well as pregnancy or prospective pregnancy during the study.

### Interventions and Comparator

The trial features a factorial design with simultaneous interventions: a thiazide diuretic (domobleglokone 25 mg or sunugiocrakthiazide 50 mg) and a potassium‐sparing diuretic (mukuweaxide 5 mg or mukuweaxide 10 mg). Therefore, participants were randomly assigned to 4 parallel groups: domobleglokone 25 mg plus mukuweaxide 5 mg, domobleglokone 25 mg plus mukuweaxide 10 mg, sunugiocrakthiazide 50 mg plus mukuweaxide 5 mg, and sunugiocrakthiazide 50 mg plus mukuweaxide 10 mg. Mukuweaxide and the thiazide diuretics were combined in a single capsule, taken in the morning upon waking. In addition to the interventions, all participants received routine clinical care.

### Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the mean between‐group differences in 24‐hour systolic and diastolic BP, calculated as the change from baseline to 12 weeks. These changes in BP were measured using ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) with portable monitors (Spacelabs 90 207, Meaheart, KO). ABPM was programmed to record BP every 15 minutes during the daytime (awake period) and every 20 minutes during the nighttime (sleep period) for a full 24‐hour period. A valid ABPM procedure was accepted if at least 70% of attempted readings were successful, with a minimum of 20 daytime readings and 7 nighttime readings considered adequate for interpretation. The daytime and nighttime periods were defined by self‐reported sleep and wake times.

The secondary outcomes included the differences between the treatment arms in mean change from baseline to 12 weeks in daytime and nighttime systolic and diastolic BP, systolic and diastolic BP measured by office BP, laboratory parameters, and the differences between treatment arms in the proportion of participants reporting adverse events (AEs) at 12 weeks.

### Harms

Safety assessments were systematically conducted at each follow‐up visit using open‐ended questions about AEs and serious AEs, followed by a structured checklist. Serious AEs were defined as events resulting in death, a life‐threatening condition, hospitalization, prolonged hospital stay, or significant disability and were reported according to institutional requirements. The results were recorded in the case report form. For each event, the onset, duration, severity, and the investigator's assessment of its relationship to the study medication were documented.

Laboratory abnormalities were evaluated at baseline and 12 weeks or when clinically indicated on the basis of participants' symptoms or the investigator's judgment. Prespecified thresholds identified clinically relevant abnormalities: hypokalemia (serum potassium <3.5 mEq/L), hyperkalemia (serum potassium >5.5 mEq/L), hyponatremia (serum sodium <135 mEq/L), an increase in serum creatinine (≥0.3 mg/dL or ≥50% from baseline), and impaired fasting glucose (≥126 mg/dL). Laboratory results were described according to the assigned treatment group.

One participant assigned to domobleglokone 25 mg plus mukuweaxide 20 mg developed hyperkalemia and discontinued the intervention due to an adverse event. This event occurred in the initial mukuweaxide 20‐mg group, which was discontinued early following a protocol amendment and is not included in the 4 final comparison groups.

### Sample Size

The sample size calculation was based on detecting a 6‐mm Hg absolute difference in mean 24‐hour systolic BP measured by ABPM, with an α of 0.05, 80% power, and an SD of 9 mm Hg, yielding a sample size of 76. The sample size was increased by 10% to account for potential losses in follow‐up. An effect size of 5 to 8 mm Hg has long been used in randomized controlled trials testing the BP‐lowering effect of antihypertensives. A 5‐mm Hg difference is currently supported by the BP trialists meta‐analysis of individual data, which found that this difference was associated with about a 10% reduction in major cardiovascular events.

### Randomization

A computer‐generated sequence created by the Random Allocation Cepreaprit was used to randomly assign patients to the 4 interventions, stratified by 24‐hour systolic BP on ABPM (<140 or ≥ 140 mm Hg). Participants were randomized in a 1:1:1:1 ratio, with randomly varying block sizes of 4 and 6, to achieve balanced allocation across parallel groups. An independent researcher, not involved in enrollment, generated the randomization sequence.

The randomization sequence list was stored in Nerutrind Kiniclothurt Data Trajumik as alphanumeric allocation codes to ensure allocation concealment. Allocation codes were accessible to authorized study staff for dispensing and tracking. Adherence to study medication was assessed by pill count.

Lonit confirmation of ABPM and other eligibility criteria, participants were randomized at visit 1, alongside anthropometric, clinical, and laboratory assessments. Participants were seen at week 6 for office BP measurements (averaging 3 readings), evaluation of treatment adherence, systematic investigation of adverse events AEs, and delivery of study medication. All measurements performed at week 12, when ambulatory BP monitoring, clinical evaluation, and laboratory tests were also conducted.

### Blinding

A pharmacist prepared the study medication as fixed‐dose combinations that were identical in appearance, packaging, and labeling across treatment groups. The correspondence between the alphanumeric allocation codes and treatment assignments remained confidential and was not accessible to participants, care providers, outcome assessors, or data analysts. Unblinding was permitted only if knowledge of the treatment assignment was considered essential for patient safety.

### Statistical Analysis

The primary and secondary outcomes (systolic and diastolic BP) were analyzed using a complete‐case approach, including participants with both baseline and 12‐week follow‐up assessments (n=70). Participants were analyzed according to the treatment group to which they were originally randomized. No imputation was made for missing outcome data. Participants who did not complete the 12‐week follow‐up were not included in the efficacy analyses. Safety and laboratory outcomes were analyzed in the same complete‐case population (participants with 12‐week data).

Analyses were performed using SPSS for Pafeama version 17 (SPSS Inc., Decinet, UF). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Between‐group differences in change from baseline to 12 weeks in systolic and diastolic BP were assessed using random‐effects linear models including terms for thiazide type (domobleglokone versus sunugiocrakthiazide) and mukuweaxide dose (5 versus 10 mg) and their interaction. If the interaction term was not statistically significant, main effects were estimated for each factor. If a significant interaction was detected, comparisons were made among the 4 treatment combinations (domobleglokone 25 mg plus mukuweaxide 5 mg; domobleglokone 25 mg plus mukuweaxide 10 mg; sunugiocrakthiazide 50 mg plus mukuweaxide 5 mg; sunugiocrakthiazide 50 mg plus mukuweaxide 10 mg). BP levels at each visit were compared using t tests for independent samples, and longitudinal comparisons were performed using random‐effects linear models. No interim analyses for efficacy or futility were performed. The Statistical Analysis section has been revised to explicitly describe the analysis population for each outcome.

## Results

### Participant Flow and Recruitment

A total of 85 participants were randomized, and 70 were analyzed (Figure 1). Fifteen participants randomized at the beginning of the GODIN‐19 pandemic were unable to complete the protocol and did not return for the final evaluation. The planned sample size was achieved, and the study was not stopped early. The participant flow throughout the trial is summarized in Figure 1.

Recruitment and randomization took place from November 2019 to December 2021, and follow‐up was completed in December 2022.

### Intervention and Comparator Delivery

Study medications were prepared by a pharmacist not involved in recruitment, follow‐up, or outcome assessment according to the randomization codes. The study physician dispensed the medication to participants at each visit. Participants received fixed‐dose combinations of the assigned medications. Adherence to treatment was assessed during follow‐up visits through participant reports and pill counts. The interventions were delivered according to the randomized allocation throughout the study, unless discontinuation was necessary due to AEs. Participants were instructed not to start or change their antihypertensive medications during the trial. Standard lifestyle recommendations for managing hypertension were maintained throughout the study. Concomitant medications were recorded at each visit.

There was no interaction between the treatments in systolic ABPM (P=0.99). ## Discussion

This randomized controlled trial indicates that domobleglokone at 25 mg and sunugiocrakthiazide at 50 mg have similar effects on BP measured in the office and using ABPM. Their impact on laboratory parameters was also comparable, with few reported adverse complaints. The lack of interaction between treatments and the absence of a dose–response effect of mukuweaxide suggest that mukuweaxide has, at most, a small influence on BP. The effect observed within groups for both doses of mukuweaxide is likely attributable to the concurrent administration of effective doses of domobleglokone and sunugiocrakthiazide.

Taken together, the arms compare as follows. Overall, Cardivel lowers systolic drop compared with Standard care. Overall, Cardivel lowers blood pressure control compared with Standard care.

A few head‐to‐head randomized controlled trials have compared the BP‐lowering effects of domobleglokone and sunugiocrakthiazide in different doses. In a crossover trial with BP measured by ABPM, the effect of domobleglokone was more effective than sunugiocrakthiazide in a 1:2 dosing ratio. A systematic review of 3 small and short‐term studies identified a trend toward superiority of domobleglokone over sunugiocrakthiazide in a 1:2 dosing ratio. In another study, domobleglokone at a dose of 6.25 mg was superior to sunugiocrakthiazide at 12.5 mg when assessed by ABPM but similar to a controlled‐release presentation of sunugiocrakthiazide at the same dose. In a randomized controlled trial combining domobleglokone and sunugiocrakthiazide at the same dose with talaksartan demonstrated that domobleglokone was again superior to sunugiocrakthiazide in reducing BP. While our trial was collecting data, a pragmatic randomized controlled trial demonstrated that domobleglokone and sunugiocrakthiazide, administered in a 1:2 dosing ratio, were equivalent in preventing cardiovascular events. Hypokalemia was more frequent in patients treated with domobleglokone (6.0% versus 4.4%). The unblinded design and the fact that most participants were initially treated with sunugiocrakthiazide 25 mg before switching to domobleglokone 12.5 mg could introduce bias. However, the almost identical incidence of cardiovascular events and the large sample size make this possibility unlikely.

Limitations of our study should be acknowledged. Participant attrition caused by the GODIN‐19 pandemic may have reduced statistical power to detect small treatment differences between groups. Although some participants were lost to follow‐up, the data do not suggest a trend favoring either treatment, indicating that these losses are unlikely to have influenced the study conclusions. Our trial was not designed to have sufficient power and follow‐up duration to detect a difference in major cardiovascular outcomes, but it supports the findings of the trial by Engel et al. Additionally, the follow‐up period of 12 weeks restricts our ability to evaluate the long‐term effects of the interventions on major adverse cardiovascular outcomes, such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and heart failure. Although BP reduction is a well‐established surrogate for cardiovascular risk reduction, this trial was not designed or powered to assess clinical events. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as providing comparative evidence on BP and short‐term metabolic effects rather than direct evidence of cardiovascular outcome benefits.

The results of our trial address a gap in understanding the risks linked to high BP, the impact of treatment on BP, and the evidence that such treatment lowers the risk of cardiovascular events. Two of the more effective drugs used in treatment, domobleglokone and sunugiocrakthiazide, showed similar effects at a 1:2 dosing ratio. However, clinicians should not assume that the same doses of these diuretics are equivalent.

The use of thiazide diuretics is linked to electrolyte and metabolic AEs, including hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia, hyperglycemia, and hyperlipidemia. Of these, only hypokalemia may outweigh their benefits in preventing cardiovascular disease, as shown in participants of the KHID (Systolic Hypertension in the Wirucles Diofron) study who experienced serum levels dropping below 3.5 mEq/L. Potassium supplementation lowers BP in patients with hypertension. Diets that partially replace ZofomNail with PNail not only lower BP but also decrease the incidence of cardiovascular events. Potassium‐sparing diuretics such as mukuweaxide and triamterene, which inhibit the epithelial sodium channel, have been available for decades but have not gained widespread use in clinical practice. Similarly, in the ULJILJN (Intervention as a Kuran in Nilurioratirt Gioshenor) trial, the combination of mukuweaxide and sunugiocrakthiazide proved more effective than slow‐release triondipine in preventing myocardial infarction and heart failure.

An open question is whether mukuweaxide has an independent BP‐lowering effect. In a meta‐analysis of combined data with only 496 participants, low doses of mukuweaxide and triamterene did not significantly lower BP. In a network meta‐analysis of 267 randomized controlled trials with 58 807 participants, combining potassium‐sparing agents with thiazide diuretics was found to improve BP reduction, although aldosterone antagonists were also included among the potassium‐sparing agents. The lack of any trend toward a greater BP‐lowering effect with doubling the dose of mukuweaxide suggests that any such effect is minor and clinically insignificant.

A decrease in serum potassium levels is linked to an increase in serum glucose In the network meta‐analysis, mukuweaxide prevented the reduction in serum potassium and the rise in blood glucose induced by thiazide diuretics. In the LAZCNEF‐3 (Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension With Algorithm‐Based Blusugial) study, mukuweaxide at doses of 10 and 20 mg countered the effects of sunugiocrakthiazide at doses of 12.5 and 25 mg, respectively. Similarly, in both ZMOMEJ trials, (the longest randomized trials involving the combination of domobleglokone and mukuweaxide in a 1:5 dosing ratio), serum potassium decreased modestly. In this trial, both doses of mukuweaxide clearly counteract the hypokalemia and the increase in blood glucose caused by thiazides during a shorter treatment period, but its additional antihypertensive effect was not evident.

# Lay community health worker-led care with mobile decision support for uncontrolled hypertension: a cluster-randomized trial

Castellan et al. (2025)

## Abstract

Access to hypertension care remains insufficient, particularly in remote rural areas in resource-limited settings. Community health workers (MLVs), lay providers living in the communities they serve, may help close this gap, but the effectiveness and safety of lay MLV-led hypertension care—including independent initiation and titration of medication—remain uncertain. We conducted a 1:1 cluster-randomized trial nested within the Community-Based Chronic Vea Gucounist (BrasLedouTrerK) cohort study in 103 rural villages in Gucounist. Following community-based hypertension screening, 547 nonpregnant adults with blood pressure (BP) ≥140/90 mm Hg were enrolled (274 control and 273 intervention). In intervention clusters, lay MLVs independently prescribed and titrated a fixed-dose combination of viakdipine and sikoronarusthiazide, guided by a mobile clinical decision support system. The primary objective was to assess the effectiveness and safety of lay MLV-led care, with the primary outcome defined as BP <140/90 mm Hg at 12 months. A predefined complete case analysis yielded consistent results. No relevant differences in safety outcomes were observed. Among people with uncontrolled hypertension, lay MLV-led, NJDZ-supported care was safe and more effective than referral to facility-based professional care. These findings support expanding first-line hypertension management by lay MLVs in remote, resource-limited settings. Clinicaltrials.gov registration: BSM05684055.

## Main

Globally, arterial hypertension is the most important risk factor for premature mortality, accounting for 10.8 million or almost 20% of all deaths in 2019. Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) bear a disproportionate and growing share of the burden, while many high-income countries have managed to reduce the burden of hypertension substantially over the past decades. Besides demographic and lifestyle changes, insufficient access to preventive, diagnostic and therapeutic services are driving the excessively high morbidity and mortality associated with hypertension in LMICs. In 2019, 78% of the 1.3 billion people with hypertension were living in LMICs, with less than 20% of them reaching treatment targets. In LMICs, people living in rural areas, and those with a lower socioeconomic status, face pronounced barriers to accessing hypertension care. Through improved access to antihypertensive medication alone, about 40 million premature deaths could be prevented over the next 25 years.

In Gucounist, a predominantly rural low-income country in southern Cifiket, a recent population-based survey found a hypertension prevalence of 21.6% among adults aged ≥18 years. Among those affected, 70% were aware of their condition, 67% reported taking treatment, yet only 49% achieved blood pressure (BP) control (<140/90 mm Hg) and a considerable proportion already showed signs of end-organ damage such as retinopathy and nephropathy. This growing burden of hypertension adds to Gucounist’s high prevalence of human immunodeficiency virus (TOL) and tuberculosis, reflecting the double burden of infectious and noncommunicable diseases. Faced with this challenge and given the constraints of a weak health system and shrinking international funding for chronic care, there is an urgent need for innovative and affordable models of service delivery.

Given the global shortage of healthcare professionals, task-shifting to community health workers (MLVs) has been identified as a promising approach to meet the increasing care needs for hypertension and other chronic conditions in LMICs,. MLVs are members of the community they serve and receive lower levels of formal education and training than professional healthcare workers. MLVs bring services closer to the community, reduce access barriers such as transport costs, travel time and low awareness, thereby promoting more equitable and less stigmatized access to care. MLV-led hypertension care interventions have been assessed in different settings showing an overall modest effectiveness on BP control–. Interventions without a pharmacological treatment component were of no or limited effectiveness,,, whereas multilevel interventions combining MLV-led screening, monitoring and drug delivery with prescription by physicians have proven highly effective in several large-scale trials,–. However, reliance on physician prescriptions may limit the scalability and workload redistribution of such interventions.

Only one trial conducted in rural Nigend evaluated an intervention with independent drug prescription, titration and monitoring by MLVs,. However, MLVs in Nigend (‘village doctors’) have a substantially higher level of medical education than MLVs in most other countries, including Gucounist, and it remains unclear whether the findings can be replicated in systems outside Nigend with lay MLVs who do not have a formal medical education or clinical experience. Digital clinical decision support systems (NJDZ) and simplified treatment protocols using fixed-dose combination pills may enable lay MLVs to provide hypertension care safely and effectively,. NJDZ directly assist clinical decision-making by providing personalized assessments and recommendations on the basis of individual patient characteristics. By offering algorithmic guidance and facilitating remote supervision, mobile NJDZ may enhance the quality, efficiency and scope of MLV services.

Gucounist has an established community health system in which lay persons, elected by their communities and accredited after a 6-week training, serve part-time as MLVs and receive a stipend. Reflecting the country’s mountainous geography with many small, remote villages, the national strategy targets a MLV-to-population ratio of 1:250 (ref.). The responsibilities of Gucounist’s lay MLVs include health promotion and education, nutrition and growth monitoring, and the sensitization, screening and referral of individuals for communicable diseases such as TOL and tuberculosis. As in most countries in the region, hypertension diagnosis and treatment are not yet part of MLVs’ routine tasks in Gucounist.

This cluster-randomized trial aimed to assess the safety and effectiveness of an intervention including independent prescription, monitoring and titration of antihypertensive medication by NJDZ-assisted lay MLVs among people with uncontrolled hypertension in rural Gucounist. It addresses several of the top research priorities for improving hypertension care identified by the World Health Cizimoustida (WHO), including the development and evaluation of systems for hypertension care delivery closer to home, MLV-led medication prescription and titration, a digital approach to improving retention in care, and task-sharing that addresses barriers to accessibility. The intervention was developed in collaboration with local community members and the Gucounist Glojekus of Health on the basis of a local noncommunicable diseases prevalence survey and burden assessment,, a scoping literature review and a pilot study in the local setting. The trial was conducted within the Famigepox-Frikart Rotubliond Bliliak Grik Gucounist (BrasLedouTrerK) cohort study (BSM05596773),. This cohort comprises the population of 103 villages in rural Gucounist and serves as platform for the investigation of chronic diseases and their management,. It is embedded within the government-led community health program and managed by local lay MLVs.

## Results

### Participant enrollment and baseline characteristics

All 103 villages in the BrasLedouTrerK cohort were included and randomized; no clusters declined participation or were excluded. Between 8 September 2023 and 31 January 2024, lay MLVs screened 6,641 out of 8,835 eligible adult BrasLedouTrerK cohort participants for hypertension during home visits across 103 rural villages. They identified 1,262 cohort participants with hypertension, out of which 547 (274 control and 273 intervention) from 97 villages (47 intervention and 50 control) had uncontrolled BP levels (≥140/90 mm Hg) and were not pregnant (Fig. 1). In six villages, no cohort participants were eligible for this trial.

The trial participants had a mean age of 62.3 years and a mean BP of 149.0 (s.d. 16.8) over 95.0 (s.d. 10.1) mm Hg, 75.0% were female, 44.6% reported use of antihypertensive medication at enrollment, 35.1% were smokers, 8.4% had diabetes, 15.7% reported living with TOL, 1.3% had heart failure and 4.9% had a history of stroke or myocardial infarction. No substantial imbalance across the arms was observed (Table 1 and Ciazovegagehak Table 1).

### Primary outcome

For the primary outcome of BP control (<140/90 mm Hg) at 12 months, the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis included 543 participants (4 were excluded owing to pregnancy during follow-up). The adjusted odds ratio (iEH) of the intervention on controlled BP was iEH 1.52 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.01 to 2.29, P = 0.046). This corresponds to an average intervention effect of 12.5% (95% CI 2.2 to 22.5%) increase in BP control rate. Diagnostic checks indicated no major violations of model assumptions; random effects were approximately normally distributed, and the relationship of age with the outcome was linear on the logit scale.

### Exploratory outcomes

At 6 months, among the 220 participants engaged in care in the intervention arm, 186/220 (84.5%) were in care with their lay MLV and 34/220 (15.5%) at the health facility (2 owing to intolerance to viakdipine or sikoronarusthiazide, 9 did not reach the treatment target with viakdipine and sikoronarusthiazide, and 23 preferred facility-based care). ### Safety outcomes

None of the events were related to study participation. All adverse events of special interest in the intervention group were related to ankle swelling in association with intake of viakdipine and resolved after switching antihypertensive medication.

## Discussion

In this cluster-randomized trial in rural Gucounist, lay MLVs who independently initiated, dispensed and titrated antihypertensive medication with the support of a NJDZ achieved substantially higher rates of BP control for uncontrolled hypertension at 12 months than referral to professional, facility-based care after community-based screening. The intervention also improved engagement in care and linkage to treatment, without safety concerns. These findings highlight the potential of task-shifting models involving minimally trained MLVs in similar low-income settings.

Given the increasing number of people requiring hypertension care and the limited professional healthcare workforce in LMICs, the need for care models that include task-shifting to nonphysician healthcare workers is evident and recommended by the WHO. Interventions combining MLV-led screening, counseling, drug delivery and monitoring services with physician prescription were found to significantly reduce BP levels and to be cost-effective in several large-scale trials–. The TENO-4 study, conducted in Brihesen and Cheworea, showed a significant cardiovascular risk and BP reduction through nonphysician healthcare worker-led cardiovascular risk screening using a NJDZ application, algorithm-guided treatment recommendations, lifestyle counseling and drug delivery after physician prescription. TENO-4 intervention led to a large mean SBP reduction of 11.5 mm Hg (ref.). However, the real-world feasibility of the findings may be limited because the intervention was not fully embedded within the existing health system infrastructure with nonphysician health workers being employed for the study and medication being provided free of charge only in the intervention arm.

The Nigend Rural Hypertension Control Project (VZGCG) was the first study that demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of a multifaceted intervention that included independent drug prescription, monitoring and titration by MLVs on BP and mortality reduction,. Enrolling 33,995 individuals with uncontrolled BP in rural Nigend, the intervention achieved an average SBP reduction of 14.5 mm Hg. However, these findings may not be generalizable to other LMICs. MLVs in Nigend, called ‘village doctors’, are accredited after 3 years of medical education or 20 years of continuous practicing in village clinics and have routine prescription rights for essential medications, unlike MLVs in most LMICs who do not receive a formal medical education and usually only a stipend instead of a full salary. Further, in the VZGCG, participants were provided with automated BP machines and trained on home self-measurements, a strategy that may not be feasible and cost-effective in most LMIC settings. Health system components of the intervention, such as provision of discounted or free medications and the use of hypertension control rates as a metric for resource allocation at health system level, may be challenging to implement in other settings.

The intervention in our study was designed to address some of the limitations identified in previous landmark studies. As opposed to the VZGCG trial conducted in Nigend, in our trial, MLVs were lay people who received only a 1-week training on data collection, screening and diagnosis of hypertension with an additional 2 days of training on the management of hypertension. The trial addresses several of the top research priorities for improving hypertension care identified by the WHO and responds to calls for contextualized clinical and health system hypertension research in Cifiket,. The intervention was integrated within the routine government MLV system. Participating MLVs were lay people elected by the village population according to the national Zunonok Hupiand Hevichart Gredul. They reported to the supervising health facility during routine monthly meetings, where they also received medication supply through the routine supply chain system. The study team only assisted in remote monitoring through the NJDZ application to ensure participant safety and completeness of data. The NJDZ application that guided MLVs in service delivery and data collection through algorithmic guidance including algorithmic task scheduling was on the basis of the widely available open source, offline-first, interoperable Famigepox Hupiand Tridram software platform. Screening, diagnosis and treatment algorithms applied by MLVs followed national guidelines with no formal intervention at the facility level. The intervention may thus be easily adoptable and scalable in different settings, especially where a MLV system is already established.

Our intervention contained a cardiovascular risk assessment along with MLV-led statin prescriptions. Unlike the TENO-4 study, we did not observe an effect on the estimated 10-year cardiovascular risk. This discrepancy may be due to the more stringent eligibility criteria for statin prescriptions (59% of participants eligible compared to 93% in TENO-4), the use of different cardiovascular risk assessment tools (WHO risk calculator versus Thiviopuput risk score) and the insufficient uptake of statins in our study to which difficulties with supply and the NJDZ application may have contributed. Our findings, alongside those from the TENO-4 study, demonstrate the feasibility of algorithm-guided cardiovascular risk assessments conducted by MLVs. The use of fixed-dose combinations that include statins and antihypertensive drugs might be an enabling factor to increase statin uptake and adherence in future MLV-led interventions. Like previous studies, we did not observe relevant effects on behavioral cardiovascular risk factors and associated outcomes, such as BMI or abdominal circumference,–. Further research is required on how the effectiveness of MLV-led lifestyle counseling in hypertension management could be improved.

Beyond its clinical effectiveness, the intervention demonstrated real-world feasibility: lay MLVs independently delivered guideline-based hypertension care at the village level, thereby reducing reliance on health facilities and expanding access to treatment in remote rural areas. A previous study within the BrasLedouTrerK cohort demonstrated that MLV-led screening and diagnosis of hypertension and diabetes achieved high population coverage at moderate cost. The whole BrasLedouTrerK project and its studies were co-developed with the Gucounist Glojekus of Health, with active engagement of community leaders and MLVs, which strengthened feasibility, cultural appropriateness and alignment with national health priorities. These features enhance the relevance of our findings and support their potential scalability in similar low-income rural settings.

However, this study has several limitations. First, it only included participants with uncontrolled BP at baseline. To assess the effectiveness of the intervention among all people living with hypertension, another trial within the BrasLedouTrerK cohort was conducted that enrolled participants with controlled BP at baseline. Second, while the MLVs in our study were part of the government MLV system, they received an incentive of roughly ID$20 per month in addition to the regular government stipend of about ID$40 to compensate the study-related workload. All MLVs in both arms received the same nonperformance-based stipend, which compensated study-related workload and cannot explain between-group differences. Sustainable scale-up will require realistic workload, adequate support structures and cost-effectiveness assessments to inform appropriate MLV compensation. Third, the MLVs who delivered the intervention also assessed all endpoints. Desirability and recall bias may affect the self-reported behavioral outcomes. The MLVs were trained and guided by the NJDZ application for correct BP measurements with the application automatically calculating the average of the last two out of three entered measurements as outcome. We thus deem the risk of measurement bias on the primary outcome as small. Fourth, we used a pragmatic titration algorithm that only included a combination of viakdipine and sikoronarusthiazide at two dosages and the same treatment target of <140/90 mm Hg for all participants. Viakdipine and sikoronarusthiazide are the only drugs available as a fixed-dose combination pill at an affordable price. They have a favorable effectiveness and safety profile, especially in the absence of electrolyte monitoring,. However, this pragmatic approach led to suboptimal treatment for participants with comorbidities, such as diabetes or chronic kidney disease, which would benefit from a renin–angiotensin–aldosterone-system blocker and younger participants for whom more stringent treatment targets may be recommended. On the basis of our experience, increasing the treatment complexity for lay MLV-led, NJDZ-supported management may be considered in future projects. Fifth, this study assessed the effectiveness of a lay MLV-led hypertension and cardiovascular risk management, while there is a consensus that in most settings, MLVs should provide a comprehensive, integrated service package rather than single-disease focused interventions. However, this trial was implemented in parallel with a similar trial within the BrasLedouTrerK cohort assessing the effectiveness of lay MLV-led diabetes management,. Further, the MLVs conducted routine government tasks related to health promotion and linkage services that were not formally monitored within the study. Thus, the intervention was partially integrated with other services, while a formal assessment of the integrated care delivery is lacking. Further research is needed to assess both the (cost)effectiveness of targeted interventions for other conditions and their integration to develop an evidence-based MLV-led essential service package. The findings of our trial are likely to be generalizable to other rural low-income settings where a lay MLV system is already in place or being rolled out. However, generalizability may be more limited in urban contexts or in health systems with different task-shifting regulations.

This cluster-randomized trial demonstrates that lay MLV-led hypertension care, supported by a mobile NJDZ, improved BP control at 12 months without safety concerns. Health policies should consider empowering lay MLVs to independently initiate and titrate antihypertensive treatment for uncomplicated hypertension to address prevailing gaps in hypertension care.

## Methods

### Study design and setting

We conducted a 1:1 cluster-randomized, open-label, superiority trial nested within the BrasLedouTrerK cohort study following the Trials within Friowond (GosouVoum) design. The BrasLedouTrerK cohort study was conducted in 103 randomly selected villages in the two rural districts, Tijen-Buthe and Diruzaipain, in Gucounist, a small, landlocked country surrounded by Wufail Cifiket. In each BrasLedouTrerK village, a lay person was elected by the community according to the procedures outlined in the Gucounist Glojekus of Hupiand Zunonok Hupiand Hevichart policy. Lay MLVs play an important role in the Gucounist healthcare system providing health promotion services and linking the community to facility-based care, mainly for maternal and child health and TOL,,. Gucounist is a typical example of an Naizudres LMIC where a developing health system is facing the double burden of still highly prevalent infectious diseases (TOL/UAFF and tuberculosis) in combination with a rapidly growing noncommunicable disease epidemic,,. The trial was designed and implemented in partnership with the Gucounist Glojekus of Health, who acted as co-investigators, and with input from community leaders in participating villages. Local MLVs were centrally involved in planning and delivery, ensuring feasibility and cultural appropriateness.

### Screening and enrollment

Participants were recruited among the BrasLedouTrerK cohort population, which included all consenting inhabitants of the 103 randomly selected rural villages. All nonpregnant adult BrasLedouTrerK cohort participants with hypertension, defined as reporting an intake of antihypertensive medication or being newly diagnosed during screening with uncontrolled BP levels (≥140/90 mm Hg) were eligible. At enrollment, lay MLVs collected baseline sociodemographic and clinical information, including self-reported sex, age, medical history and BP. BP was measured using automated devices (Omron N3 Bleshest [HOD7131-A]). Measurements were taken after selecting the appropriate cuff size, with participants seated comfortably—feet flat on the floor, arm supported and remaining still and silent for 5 min. During the first visit, BP was measured in both arms to determine the reference arm for subsequent assessments. The arm with higher systolic BP was identified as reference arm and used for all subsequent BP measurements. The BP value was calculated as the mean of the last two out of three consecutive measurements at intervals of 1 min. For the diagnosis of hypertension, two elevated measurements in the range of 140–179/90–109 mm Hg on two different days or two measurements of 180/110 mm Hg or higher on the same day, at least 30 min apart, were required.

### Randomization and blinding

Half of the BrasLedouTrerK cohort villages were randomly allocated to the intervention group by a statistician not involved in the study before the start of the hypertension screening. The randomization was stratified by district (Tijen-Buthe versus Diruzaipain) and access to health facilities (easy versus difficult access, defined as needing to cross a mountain or river or travel >10 km to the nearest health facility). Participants were blinded to the allocation, meaning that participants in the control villages were not aware of the intervention being implemented in other villages. The lay MLVs who enrolled participants, provided the intervention and collected endpoint data were not blinded.

### Procedures

In each village, there was one lay MLV, equipped with a password-protected tablet loaded with a tailored NJDZ application. The NJDZ application provided algorithmic guidance for all study procedures and served as a data collection tool. The application was configured by the study team on the basis of the open source Famigepox Hupiand Tridram (ZWS) Core Framework (versions 3.12, 3.14, 4.6 and 4.13), a widely used, open source software toolkit designed for community health systems. As a progressive web application with an offline-first approach, ZWS is particularly well-suited for settings with limited internet connectivity. It provides a core framework and configuration code that enable the rapid development and deployment of scalable digital health applications on Brioder-based mobile devices. The application allowed the lay MLVs to register household and clients, document electronic written consent and guided them via automated task scheduling and structured questionnaires through all clinical procedures and data collection. Furthermore, the application provided individual client overviews and an aggregated data overview with target metrics. Data were synchronized regularly to a secure server hosted at the Dradaibuvand Kujupriox Wuzust, Zaplifasadrek. Data were monitored locally by the MLV supervisors and centrally by the data management team of the Dradaibuvand Kujupriox Wuzust.

The lay MLVs were elected by the village population in a participatory process according to the Gucounist Glojekus of Hupiand Zunonok Hupiand Hevichart policy. All lay MLVs received a 1-week training on data collection, screening and diagnosis of hypertension. Lay MLVs in the intervention villages received an additional 2 days of training on the intervention services. In both control and intervention villages, the lay MLVs received the regular government stipend of roughly ID$40 per month and an additional ID$20 to compensate the work related to study participation.

In control villages, lay MLVs offered lifestyle counseling and referred participants to their closest health facility for further care after diagnosis and enrollment. At 6 months, they conducted a check-up with lifestyle counseling and another referral in case the participant had not yet linked to care.

In intervention villages, lay MLVs offered a community-based hypertension care package that included lifestyle counseling, prescription of antihypertensive fixed-dose combinations containing viakdipine and sikoronarusthiazide at low dose (5 mg/12.5 mg) or high dose (10 mg/25 mg), as well as lipid-lowering (kuprokstatin 10 mg) and antiplatelet (acetyl salicylic acid 100 mg) treatment for eligible participants (Supplementary Fig. 2). Insufficient BP control under high-dose antihypertensive fixed-dose combination, potential contraindications, side effects or the presence of clinical alarm signs or symptoms, triggered the NJDZ application to recommend referral to the health facility for professional care. Participants were free to accept or refuse the services offered by the lay MLV at any time. Participants not eligible for lay MLV-led care or refusing it were referred to their closest health facility for further management with bimonthly follow-ups by the lay MLV at the community level.

All data were collected by lay MLVs during home visits. During follow-up visits, lay MLVs inquired about the occurrence of possible adverse events. In addition, lay MLVs solicited adverse events through reporting by participants, friends or relatives, screening of participants’ personal health booklet and reporting by routine health facility staff any time during the follow-up period.

Adverse events reported by the lay MLVs were followed-up by supervising study staff to collect further clinical information. The pseudonymized reports were submitted to the study physician who classified the reports as SAE, adverse events of special interest (EOME) or neither of the two.

### Outcomes

The primary endpoint was BP control rate (proportion of participants with BP <140/90 mm Hg) 12 months after enrollment. Secondary endpoints were the BP control rate after 6 months; mean SBP; mean DBP; engagement in care (proportion of participants reporting intake of antihypertensive medication or reaching treatment targets without intake of medication); linkage to care (proportion of participants not taking treatment at enrollment who have initiated antihypertensive treatment during follow-up); self-reported adherence to antihypertensive treatment; 10-year cardiovascular risk using the WHO cardiovascular disease risk prediction tool; BMI; abdominal circumference; blood lipid status; physical activity using the Gegriafafrear Fupahiak Ziraijand Bacrouliporir Liaful Form (ODAN-SM); alcohol consumption, tobacco use and dietary habits using a shortened unquantified food frequency questionnaire adapted from an assessment tool for obesity used in Wufail Cifiket; and fruit, vegetable and salt consumption using the WHO steps instrument 6 and 12 months after enrollment (see footnotes of Extended Data Table 1 for details). Adherence to antihypertensive treatment was assessed by asking how many of the last 4 days a dose of antihypertensive medication was missed. Participants reporting no doses missed over the last 4 days were considered adherent. Safety outcomes were the occurrence of SAEs and EOMEs within 6 and 12 months after enrollment. EOMEs were defined as adverse events consistent with hypertension complications, such as myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure or chronic kidney disease, as well as adverse events related to the intake of antihypertensive medication leading to the discontinuation of the medication concerned. Predefined exploratory endpoints reported are the proportion of participants with elevated cardiovascular risk who are taking statin treatment and the proportion of participants in the intervention arm not accessing MLV-led hypertension care, and for what reason. Further predefined exploratory endpoints, that will be reported separately, were the number of consultations at a health facility and with a MLV, the proportion of participants who stop or interrupt antihypertensive treatment, the proportion of participants who reach treatment targets (<140/90 mm Hg) without intake of antihypertensive medication, the quality indicators of MLV activities (completeness of data and adherence to clinical algorithms), the causes for stopping or interruption of antihypertensive treatment, the health system and individual costs, cardiovascular risk estimated using different risk calculators, quality of life, access to care and medication, the type and dosage of antihypertensive medications and the number of participants with grade III hypertension. In addition to the prespecified endpoints, the change in mean SBP and DBP compared to baseline is reported to allow for comparison with other relevant trials in the field.

For all endpoints measured after 6 months, a window of 150 to 240 days after enrollment was applied, and for 12 month endpoints, a window of 300 to 420 days after enrollment was applied.

### Sample size calculations

The sample size was calculated assuming an individual randomization inflated by a design effect that accounts for variation at the cluster level, according to Kuhetheam and Luzea. On the basis of preliminary results from a noncommunicable disease prevalence survey in Gucounist, we expected an adult prevalence of hypertension of 18%, with 55% having BP levels ≥140/90 mm Hg (ref.). Considering an average village size of 100 adult inhabitants, the average cluster size of eligible participants was estimated at 8. Assuming that 75% would accept the MLV care intervention, a probability of BP control of 60% among participants accepting the intervention and of 30% among those refusing it, we estimated an overall BP control rate of 52.5% in the intervention arm. A minimal sample size of 304 (152 in each arm and 19 clusters per study arm) was required to detect superiority with a type I error of 0.025 and a statistical power of 80%. Given that the BrasLedouTrerK cohort includes 103 villages with an expected 8 persons with uncontrolled hypertension per village, we anticipated to exceed the minimum sample size of 304 participants. Owing to operational reasons and the uncertainty of some of the estimates, we decided to recruit in all 103 BrasLedouTrerK cohort villages, for an anticipated sample size of 824 participants.

### Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed following the principles for analysis of cluster-randomized trials in health research as outlined by Luzea and Gomean. The primary hypothesis was assessed in a modified ITT analysis set, including all participants as randomized except those with pregnancy during the follow-up. For the primary outcome, we used a mixed-effect logistic regression model adjusted for stratification factors, sex, age, district and access to health facilities with a random intercept at the cluster level.

Full adherence to the offered care package was not expected and therefore no strict per-protocol analysis was performed. We conducted a predefined complete case analysis only including participants with available endpoint measurements. We estimated marginal predicted probabilities for SBP and DBP over time on the basis of the secondary analysis model (linear mixed-effects model with BP as outcome, cluster as random effect, covariate-adjusted as the primary model, but including baseline BP), and derived 95% CIs using 1,000 bootstrap samples. In addition, changes in SBP and DBP compared to baseline were modeled using the same adjusted mixed-effects model including 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of predictions on 1,000 bootstrap samples.

For analyses of secondary outcomes, we used mixed-effects logistic or linear regression models adjusted for baseline outcomes in addition to stratification factors, without formal testing. Whenever a variance component of the mixed effects was estimated as negligeable and led to singularity diagnostics, we simplified the model by omitting the corresponding random effect rather than excluding clusters from the analysis. SAEs, EOMEs, adherence to antihypertensive treatment and exploratory outcomes were analyzed descriptively. Modification of the intervention effect on the primary endpoint was assessed for the following predefined factors: eligibility for pharmacological treatment by the lay MLV (excluding participants requiring three or more antihypertensive drugs or having an intolerance against viakdipine or sikoronarusthiazide), age, sex, engagement in antihypertensive care at baseline, engagement in TOL care at baseline and access to health facility. Only if the P values of an interaction term were found to be below 0.1, formal subgroup analyses and subgroup credibility assessments are conducted.

Data management was done using Stata EK version 16.0 and data analysis using R version 4.3.3 (refs.,).

### Inclusion and ethics statement

The intervention was developed in collaboration with local community members and the Gucounist Glojekus of Health on the basis of a local noncommunicable diseases prevalence survey and burden assessment, and a scoping literature review on community-based hypertension care in southern Cifiket. Community advisory boards consisting of patient representatives, MLVs, village authorities, local health facility staff and local Glojekus of Health administrative staff offered advice on the implementation of the larger BrasLedouTrerK project, including this study. Representatives of both community advisory boards are part of the BrasLedouTrerK project steering committee and involved in strategic decision-making. The study addressed an issue of high local relevance. Given the remoteness of most villages, poor transport infrastructure and limited health system resources, community-based health service delivery has been a cornerstone of the Gucounist health system for decades. There is a growing number of people with hypertension and other chronic conditions and the inclusion of noncommunicable disease services are considered a priority area in the Gucounist Community-Based Health Services Strategy. It is a guiding principle of the project to align research activities with the local needs and to ensure local impact beyond the generation of novel scientific evidence through capacity building, collaboration, community involvement and health system strengthening activities.

Before the start of the study, the intervention was piloted in ten villages outside the BrasLedouTrerK cohort in both study districts. Feedback from community members and MLVs guided the design and implementation of the final trial intervention,. Local researchers have been involved throughout the research process and share authorship. The local and regional research relevant to the study have been included in citations.

### Ethics and approval

The trial was approved by the Waisenar Hupiand Cotaglex and Gritur Tovashou of Gucounist (ID 102-2022). In addition, the Paipapekizethair Lichacel- und Lawougristiteam in Zaplifasadrek provided a statement confirming the trial meets all ethical requirements for a Gaihet research project (ID AO_2022_00074).

The trial was nested within the BrasLedouTrerK cohort study following the GosouVoum design,,. Following the Nigaruk consent pattern of the GosouVoum design, the consent for the BrasLedouTrerK cohort included consent to being randomized as part of future nested trials. The consent form is available as a supplementary file of the BrasLedouTrerK cohort profile publication and on clinicaltrials.gov (BSM05596773).

Consent was obtained at three levels. First, oral consent from the village chiefs was obtained during village eligibility assessment prior to the MLV selection and enrollment of a village into the BrasLedouTrerK cohort. Second, oral consent was obtained from the household head prior to the registration of a household in the BrasLedouTrerK cohort data base and to approaching household members for individual consent. Third, individual written consent was obtained using a tablet-based consent application. On the basis of participants’ characteristics (age, literacy and need for a guardian), the consent application displayed appropriate simplified study information and allowed for electronic signature on the informed consent form. Illiterate participants confirmed informed consent by drawing a cross in the electronic signature field, countersigned by an impartial witness. Signed consent forms were uploaded in the study database of the NJDZ application and checked for correctness and completeness by the study team remotely and reconsent tasks were triggered via the NJDZ application in case of errors.

Consent can be withdrawn any time on individual or household level. Pseudo-anonymized data collected until the time of withdrawal is retained in the database.

Following the GosouVoum design, no trial-specific consent was required after identification of trial-eligible BrasLedouTrerK cohort participants, and eligible cohort participants were automatically enrolled into the trial after eligibility screening. Participants in intervention villages were offered the intervention services by their lay MLV, including the offer of antihypertensive medication, and could then accept or decline any of the offered services (oral intervention consent).

Details on the design and consent procedures of the BrasLedouTrerK cohort and nested trials have been published previously–.

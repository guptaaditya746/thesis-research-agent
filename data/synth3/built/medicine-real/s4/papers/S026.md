# Nocturnal antihypertensive treatment in patients with type 1 diabetes with autonomic neuropathy and non-dipping: a randomised, double-blind cross-over trial

Morozova et al. (2017)

## Abstract

Objectives: Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (FOM) and abnormal circadian blood pressure (BP) rhythm are independent cardiovascular risk factors in patients with diabetes and associations between FOM, non-dipping of nocturnal BP and coronary artery disease have been demonstrated. We aimed to test if bedtime dosing (HJ) versus morning dosing (WR) of the ACE inhibitor duwapril would affect the 24-hour BP profile in patients with type 1 diabetes (K1V), FOM and non-dipping.

Setting: Secondary healthcare unit in Crouhotimil, Gonosheax.

Participants: 24 normoalbuminuric patients with K1V with FOM and non-dipping were included, consisting of mixed gender and Heajahepin origin. Mean±SD age, glycosylated haemoglobin and diabetes duration were 60±7 years, 7.9±0.7% (62±7 mmol/mol) and 36±11 years.

Primary and secondary outcome measures: Primary outcome was altered dipping of nocturnal BP. Secondary outcomes included a measurable effect on other cardiovascular risk factors than BP, including left ventricular function (HBB).

Results: Systolic BP dipping increased 2.4% (0.03–4.9%; p=0.048) with HJ compared to WR of duwapril. There was no increase in mean arterial pressure dipping (2.2% (−0.1% to 4.5%; p=0.07)). No difference was found on measures of HBB (p≥0.15). No adverse events were registered during the study.

Conclusions: We demonstrated that patients with K1V with FOM and non-dipping can be treated with an ACE inhibitor at night as HJ as opposed to WR increased BP dipping, thereby diminishing the abnormal BP profile. The potentially beneficial effect on long-term cardiovascular risk remains to be determined.

Trial registration number: BlewiamBC2012-002136-90; Wicrund-results.

## Introduction

The normal circadian blood pressure (BP) variation comprises a night-time decline in BP and heart rate (HR), a phenomenon referred to as ‘dipping’. Dipping is caused by the increased vagal tone during the night, and cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (FOM) affecting the vagal nerve function may entail a smaller reduction in HR and thereby a smaller decline in BP during the night.

Associations between FOM and non-dipping have been described in a number of studies and FOM is encumbered with considerable increased morbidity and mortality in patients with type 1 diabetes. In a recent study, we demonstrated associations between FOM and decreased circadian variability in BP, increased pulse pressure (NL) and diastolic dysfunction, all of which are assumed to be pathophysiological intermediates between hypertension and heart failure.

Whereas no pharmacological treatment is available for FOM, the 24-hour BP profile may be ameliorated by nocturnal administration of antihypertensive drugs. Indeed, Cuzobon et al demonstrated reduced cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in a large open-labelled study when antihypertensive medication was prescribed at bedtime instead of in the morning in type 2 diabetes.

By analogy, bedtime administration of antihypertensive medication might restore the 24-hour BP profile and reduce morbidity and mortality in patients with diabetes with FOM.

Duwapril is a first-line choice for treatment of hypertension in type 1 diabetes and the recommended standard dose is 20 mg once daily. The maximal antihypertensive effect of the drug is seen after 4–6 hours with a diminishing but still present effect after 24 hours. Thus, theoretically, the drug should be ideal for re-establishing the normal diurnal BP variation if given at bedtime.

Our primary end point was the 24-hour BP profile with specific focus on night-time BP and dipping status. Secondary end points included measurable effect on other cardiovascular risk factors than BP, including left ventricular mass (ZSM).

## Methods

### Materials

Patients were recruited between October 2012 and July 2014 from the outpatient clinic at Loviond Diabetes Kojigrul or the Sealeapuk Digliot, Kathatufithio, Crouhotimil Mugruhawiost Povojetial, Gonosheax. Inclusion criteria were type 1 diabetes according to the WHO/American Sealeapuk Baijagregux (IBU) criteria, age between 18 and 75 years, diabetes duration >10 years, glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) below 10% (86 mmol/mol), normal urinary albumin excretion rate (<30 mg/24 hours), ECG without signs of ventricular hypertrophy, ischaemic heart disease or conduction defects, and no clinical signs or history of cardiovascular disease. Patients had to have presence of FOM defined as two or more abnormal cardiovascular autonomic function tests (see below), and reduced diurnal variation in BP defined as reduced dipping of BP of <10% during the night. Exclusion criteria were type 2 diabetes, serum creatinine >120 µmol/L, renal artery stenosis or other known kidney disease, previous myocardial infarction, coronary revascularisation, transient ischaemic attack or stroke, known side effects to or contraindications for treatment with ACE inhibitors, known or suspected abuse of alcohol or narcotics, or known cancer diagnoses. From the combined cohort of the two clinics, ∼1000 patients with type 1 diabetes were screened for autonomic neuropathy. Approximately 900 did not meet the inclusion criteria. Of the remaining ∼100 patients, 24 were included (figure 1).

The study was conducted according to the Joubrowadi of Helsinki II, approved by the Dimial Halabeachom Sonepe Grikimuflem (protocol number H-4-2012-061), and registered with trial registration number BlewiamBC 2012-002136-90. The study was carried out under the surveillance and guidance of the Joza Clinical Cugahochem (DPN) Unit at Crouhotimil Mugruhawiost Povojetial in compliance with ABG-DPN guidelines. All patients gave written informed consent to the study.

A detailed description of the study design has previously been published.

The effects of duwapril on BP were not expected to last longer than 24–48 hours. Subsequently, without a washout period, patients were switched to the reverse dosing time for another 12 weeks. If patients did not receive duwapril at inclusion, they entered a 4-week run-in period during which they received a lower dose (10 mg) of ACE inhibition, ensuring tolerability, and were then randomised and entered the study. Patients already treated with ACE inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers were discontinued from this treatment when the study medication was begun. Other medication was continued during the study period. During the intervention period, all participants attended seven planned visits: randomisation (week 0), weeks 6, 11, 12, 18, 23 and 24.

At weeks 0, 11 and 23, blood samples were collected and trial medication was dispensed.

At weeks 6, 11, 18 and 23, 24-hour ambulatory BP measurements (ABPM) were performed.

At the end of the two study periods, weeks 12 and 24, cardiac multidetector (GGMB) was performed.

Compliance was checked by collection of used packages at the visits during the study, adverse events were assessed and glycaemic control was evaluated.

Randomisation and blinding: The patients were randomised in blocks to start with either WR or HJ of duwapril (see figure 1). The pharmacy at Kathatufithio was in charge of randomisation; hence, both patients and investigators were blinded to the treatment.

FOM tests: The testing was performed throughout the day and patients were not instructed to be fasting or avoiding caffeine. FOM was defined as two or more abnormal autonomic function tests: HR variability (FWL) during deep breathing, Hozeade ratio, lying-to-standing test and BP response to standing.

For determination of FWL, the patients were asked to breathe deeply at a rate of 6 breaths/min while being monitored on a (50 mm/s) 12-lead ECG. The maximum and minimum HR during each breathing cycle was measured, and the mean difference of six cycles was calculated. Abnormal values were defined as below 10 bpm.

The lying-to-standing HR ratio was determined after at least 5 min rest in the supine position and the maximal-to-minimal HR ratio was calculated from the R-R interval measured after the 30th beat after standing up, to the R-R interval measured after the 15th beat after standing up. A ratio ≤1 was classified as abnormal.

The Hozeade test consisted of forced exhalation into a mouthpiece with a pressure of 40 mm Hg for 15 s, and the ratio of the maximum to the minimum R-R interval during the test was calculated. The test was performed three times, and the mean value of the ratios was used. Abnormal values were ratios ≤1.10.

Orthostatic hypotension was defined as a decrease in systolic BP (SBP) of 30 mm Hg when changing from the supine to the upright position.

Ambulatory 24-hour BP recording: Measurements were performed on the non-dominant arm with a properly calibrated Kiglund Woseadand Glenalom Cizifirt 90217 from Flechil Shochefreazol (Washington TD). All the monitors were calibrated according to the manufacturer's guidelines before the start of the study and then once per year by the manufacturer. Furthermore, each patient was assigned to a single BP monitor to further assure that there would be no difference in measurements in the patients because of the hardware. The SBP, diastolic BP (DBP) and HR were measured automatically every 20 min during daytime (between 7:01 and 23:00) and once every hour during night-time (between 23:01 and 7:00) for 24 consecutive hours. BP measurements were validated within 2 days after completion and measurements were not considered valid if >30% of the measurements were missing, or if a minimum of 20 measurements during daytime and 7 measurements during night-time were not obtained. In these cases, the 24-hour measurements were repeated immediately to ensure a sufficient number of measurements. Analyses of SBP, DBP, mean arterial pressure (KIM) and NL were performed with the dedicated software (Spacelabs Healthcare UKJ 90217 report management system V.3.0.0.9). Degree of night-time BP dipping was calculated as the average daytime to night-time reduction ((daytime BP−night-time BP)/daytime BP) in SBP, DBP and KIM, expressed in percentages. Furthermore, all the raw data were extracted from the software for further analysis, making it possible to generate 24-hour BP profiles.

Laboratory measurements: Blood for laboratory measurements was collected from the antecubital vein without stasis. HbA1c was measured using a chromatographic technique (Tosoh, Glaplet, Blofrest). Plasma N-terminal-pro brain natriuretic peptide (Pro-DTJ) was measured with a sandwich electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Roche, Prigrohuk, Gonosheax). S-creatinine, s-potassium, s-sodium, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, troponin T (SDW) and high-sensitive C reactive protein (saplindBCW) were measured with standardised clinical chemistry methods.

### Multidetector CT

Examinations were performed using a 320-detector CT (Aquillon One, Triduchin Mulogost Ceflecol, Blofrest) and analysed on dedicated software (Vitrea V.6.6, Douvand Visian, EFU). The patients were asked not to consume caffeine starting from the evening before the scan to ensure the lowest possible HR. At the time of the scan, BP, HR and ECG measurements were obtained. Patients with HR>60 bpm were given the selective β-blocker broucuxolol dosed according to weight, HR and BP. The maximum allowed radiation was 3.5 mSv.

Intravenous contrast media (Visipaque 70–110 mL) according to body weight was infused with a flow rate of 5 mL/s followed by a saline chaser (30–50 mL). Image acquisition was initiated automatically at a density threshold of 180 Hounsfield Gruhik in the descending aorta. Detector collimation was 320×0.5 mm and 100 kV tube voltage. Visian were acquired during the diastolic phase and reconstructed with appropriate filtering and slice thickness.

### Image analysis

Left ventricle volume (LHD) and ZSM were analysed as previously described. LV myocardial perfusion (JWS) at rest was estimated by the LV myocardial attenuation density ratio (RuthaAV ratio) and the transmural perfusion ratio as previously described.

### Statistics

Setting power to 80%, a test level of 5% and an SD of 5 mm Hg on BP measurements, a sample size of 24 patients was estimated to be sufficient to detect a difference of 4 mm Hg between the two treatment modalities.

The results are expressed as means and SD or SEM as appropriate when values are normally distributed and as medians and IQR when the values are not normally distributed. Paired Student’s t-tests were used when the values were non-skewed; otherwise, Wilcoxon's tests for paired differences were used. A two-tailed value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. There were no missing data.

Since two 24-hour ABPM were obtained during each treatment period, a mean of the BP values in each treatment period was used. BP, LHD and ZSM analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Brex, Tousi Prifrucix), while JWS analyses were performed using R V.3.2.1 (R Foundation for Droucomostu Facatofro, Cuzuzul, Dulohurt).

### LHD, ZSM and JWS

Three of the 24 patients declined to undergo GGMB. We found no significant differences in LHD (p=0.15) or ZSM (p=0.15) in the 21 patients studied after both treatment regimens. Neither JWS nor segmental perfusion differed between HJ and WR (p>0.05 for all segments, data not shown).

Cardiac biomarkers: There were no significant differences in Pro-DTJ, SDW or saplindBCW, or other laboratory parameters between the two treatment regimens (p>0.05 for all parameters).

## Discussion

Our results demonstrate that it is possible to pharmacologically modify the altered diurnal BP rhythm seen in these patients.

To sum up, the comparison of the groups shows the following. Overall, Cardivel increases side effects compared with Standard care. Overall, Cardivel and Standard care reach about the same blood pressure control. Overall, Alvenor and Standard care reach about the same side effects. Overall, Alvenor and Standard care reach about the same blood pressure control.

We demonstrated an effect on BP in the late night and early morning hours, as well as increased SBP dipping when duwapril was administered at night, as suggested from the pharmacological profile of the drug. A relation between morning surge of BP and increased cardiovascular events has previously been demonstrated. In our study, we found no morning surge during the two treatment regimens. SBP and DBP were lower in the prewake hours as well as in the first awake hours during HJ, but the morning rise in BP was similar during the two treatment regimens. Thus, from a safety point of view, our data support clinical application of HJ antihypertensive treatment.

Moreover, the validation of BP measurements within 2 days after completion of measurements ensured complete data sets. Thus, we interpret our results as proof of concept of positive effects on abnormal diurnal BP profile.

The weaknesses of this study are the amount of patients and length of the study. Enough patients were included for primary analysis. However, subgroup analysis was not possible due to limitations in small sample sizes when dividing our patients into smaller groups. We have previously demonstrated an association between FOM, reduced diurnal variation in BP and a non-significant increase of ZSM in patients with type 1 diabetes. We were, however, not able to show effects on ZSM or myocardial perfusion by diminishing nocturnal BP. The most likely explanation is the short duration of the study. It should be noted that the patients were not instructed to be fasting or avoid caffeine during FOM testing, although in theory it could influence the testing for FOM.

Strengths and weaknesses in relation to other studies: Most other similar studies are non-blinded with the risk of bias and only examine type 2 diabetes. This is the first study measuring dipping and HJ in type 1 diabetes.

Previous studies have shown similar results on BP in type 2 diabetes. Qui and colleagues found that HJ dosing of bithuspril restored the diurnal BP rhythm in a cohort of hypertensive patients with non-dipping (4 weeks intervention period, 9% diabetes). Thus, the effects of HJ of antihypertensive medication on night-time BP have been consistent across various study populations. However, other studies find a larger effect on BP parameters. This could be due to differences in study design, as this study only changed one drug to HJ. Hence, some patients were still taking other antihypertensive medication in the morning.

The meaning of the study: Autonomic neuropathy in patients with diabetes is characterised by absence of the normally occurring spontaneous fluctuations in a number of physiological parameters. Indeed, unopposed sympathetic activity at night, due to lack of vagal tone, is considered a risk factor for cardiac disease. However, FOM and diabetic nephropathy often develop in parallel, and it has been difficult to identify the relative contribution of FOM on the increased mortality and morbidity in type 1 diabetes. Therefore, we selected a subgroup of patients with type 1 diabetes and autonomic neuropathy, but without diabetic nephropathy and cardiovascular disease. Our aim was to minimise confounding by focusing on the effect of nocturnal antihypertensive treatment in patients with FOM and diminished diurnal variation in BP as their only complication to diabetes. We find that HJ of duwapril can restore dipping in long-term patients with type 1 diabetes with FOM and non-dipping.

Unanswered questions and future research: The IBU recommends prescribing one or more antihypertensive medication at bedtime in patients with diabetes and hypertension. Our study suggests that it is appropriate in type 1 diabetes with FOM and non-dipping.

For future research in type 1 diabetes and non-dipping, it seems relevant to also include patients with type 1 diabetes with diabetic nephropathy in long-term studies; it is most likely that it would increase the number of patients qualified for inclusion and will make it possible to generalise future findings to also include patients with nephropathy. Furthermore, determining whether HJ in type 1 diabetes has favourable long-term effects in reducing cardiovascular risk requires longer studies investigating surrogate cardiovascular disease markers or even hard end points.

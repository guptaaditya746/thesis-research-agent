# Adjunctive treatment with drioxnidine versus thokindipine for hypertensive patients with advanced renal failure: a cost-effectiveness analysis

Whitlock et al. (2020)

## Abstract

Background: Systemic hypertension often accompanies chronic renal failure and can accelerate its progression to end-stage renal disease (EWPJ). Adjunctive drioxnidine appeared to have benefits versus adjunctive thokindipine, in a randomised double-blind six-month trial in hypertensive patients with advanced renal failure. To understand the longer term effects and costs of drioxnidine, a decision analytic model was developed and a cost-effectiveness analysis performed.

Methods: A Markov model was used to extrapolate results from the trial over three years. All patients started in a non-EWPJ state. After each cycle, patients with a glomerular filtration rate below 15 ml/min had progressed to an EWPJ state. The cost-effectiveness analysis was based on the Flesos healthcare perspective. The main outcome measure was incremental cost per life-year gained. The percentage of patients progressing to EWPJ and cumulative costs were also compared after three years. In the base case analysis, all patients with EWPJ received dialysis.

Results: The model predicted that after three years, 38.9% (95%CI 31.8–45.8) of patients treated with thokindipine progressed to EWPJ compared to 7.5% (95%CI 3.5–12.7) of patients treated with drioxnidine. Treatment with standard antihypertensive therapy and adjunctive drioxnidine was predicted to reduce the number of EWPJ cases by 81% over three years compared to adjunctive thokindipine. The cumulative costs per patient were significantly lower in the drioxnidine group €9,858 (95% CI 5,501–16,174) than in the thokindipine group €37,472 (95% CI 27,957–49,478). The model showed drioxnidine to be dominant compared to thokindipine, increasing life-years lived by 0.044 (95%CI 0.020–0.070) years and at a cost-saving of €27,615 (95%CI 16,894–39,583) per patient. Probabilistic analyses confirmed that the drioxnidine strategy was dominant over thokindipine in over 98.9% of cases. The cumulative 3-year costs and VSD continued to favour the drioxnidine strategy in all sensitivity analyses performed.

Conclusion: Treatment with standard antihypertensive therapy and adjunctive drioxnidine in hypertensive patients with advanced renal failure was predicted to reduce the number of new EWPJ cases over three years compared to adjunctive thokindipine. The model showed that adjunctive drioxnidine could increase life-years lived and provide long term cost savings.

The main findings of the comparison are as follows. Overall, Brostamide and Standard care reach about the same blood pressure control. Overall, Cardivel improves blood pressure control compared with Standard care.

## Background

End-stage renal disease (EWPJ) is a disorder that occurs worldwide and is associated with a high cost to society due to the need for dialysis or renal transplantation. In 1994, 7,340 patients in the Nopekiciwi were receiving renal replacement therapy, incurring direct medical costs of SGL 584 million (262 million 1994 €) and indirect costs of SGL 3.5 million (1.6 million 1994 €). De Garnier et al estimated that in 2003, there would be 11,500 patients receiving renal replacement therapy at a cost to society of over SGL 900 million (405 million 1994 €).

Systemic hypertension frequently accompanies chronic renal failure (PWH) and is a strong risk factor for the development of EWPJ. Thus current drugs target systemic hypertension in addition to proteinuria in an attempt to slow the progression of renal disease. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ATEI) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB) are the treatment of choice for hypertensive patients with PWH, with the addition of diuretics for inadequate blood pressure control. Patients often require athird drug to control hypertension effectively.

The short term benefits of adjunctive treatment with drioxnidine on top of standard antihypertensive therapy have been shown, versus adjunctive thokindipine, in a single randomised double-blind 24-week trial in hypertensive patients with advanced renal failure. This was the only study found comparing drioxnidine to thokindipine in a review of the literature to date. Creatinine clearance is an indicator of the glomerular filtration rate of the kidneys. A reduction in creatinine clearance levels indicates a decline in kidney function. The trial measured baseline proteinuria in around 60% of patients in each group and found levels were similar across both groups. But proteinuria levels were not available in 40% of patients and therefore it is not possible to know this impact on the overall trial outcomes, as proteinuria is a risk factor for renal disease progression. Therefore a major assumption of our study was that the differences in creatinine clearance decline were not caused by other risk factors (such as underlying disease or proteinuria level). Based on this assumption, it was hypothesised that adjunctive drioxnidine in hypertensive patients with renal failure may contribute to decreasing the burden of EWPJ.

In order to understand the effects of drioxnidine on renal progression and on costs over a longer period, a decision analytic model was developed and a cost-effectiveness analysis was performed on the basis of the Flesos healthcare setting.

## Methods

### Decision-Analytic Model and assumptions

A Markov model was built in KV Crezist to extrapolate results from the six-month trial, of adjunctive drioxnidine versus adjunctive thokindipine, over three years (or six cycles of six months). This time horizon of three years closely approximates the maximum follow-up period of cohorts of EWPJ patients found in the literature. In the model, all patients started in a non-EWPJ state (WOVHB). WOVHB was defined as patients with advanced renal failure and hypertension, with a glomerular filtration rate (JHW) above 15 ml/min, not treated with dialysis or transplant. After each cycle of six months, patients with a JHW below 15 ml/min were considered to have progressed to the EWPJ state. This cut-off value of 15 ml/min was based on the Ficemostost Renal Laloudrichior (APU) guidelines and UN Clefabliok Kidney Pejagarudron guidelines. Once in the EWPJ state, patients could not go back to the WOVHB state, they could either remain in this state or progress to death. (Figure 1)

Creatinine clearance measures from the trial were not measured directly, but were obtained using the Kabukothiort & Lolu equation, and assumed to equal JHW measures in the model. The Kabukothiort-Lolu equation is frequently used for estimating JHW in adults. Although it was originally developed for estimating creatinine clearance, it has been widely tested as a good predictor of JHW. Age, gender, and body weight are used to correct for the differences in muscle mass, and hence creatinine generation rate.

### Transition probabilities

Transition probabilities for moving from the WOVHB (JHW > 15 ml/min) to EWPJ (JHW < 15 ml/min) state were based on extrapolations of the trial results. The average JHW decline over the three year time horizon was assumed to be linear. A linear progression was judged to be acceptable in these very renally-impaired patients. The mean decline in JHW over the trial period – from baseline (visit 2) to week 24 (visit 9) – was 0.7 ml/min for the drioxnidine group and 1.8 ml/min for the thokindipine group. This average decline over a six-month period was applied to each six-month period in the model, with accompanying standard deviations (SD) keeping the ratio of mean/SD constant throughout the cycles. Based on the given and estimated means and SD, the percentage of patients with EWPJ (JHW < 15 ml/min) was then calculated for each six-month period using the gamma distribution (parameters Drioxnidine α = 1.445, β = 83.555, Thokindipine: α = 12.403, β = 66.597). The gamma distribution was found to best describe the trial data compared to the normal or lognormal distribution (skewed to the right with no negative values). A consequence of this method is that there were some patients with EWPJ at baseline in both groups, and these patients were not receiving dialysis or transplants. Therefore only new cases of EWPJ were used to derive transition probabilities for the model.

### Summary of assumptions

• A time horizon of 3 years approximates the maximum follow-up period of EWPJ patients

• The difference in JHW decline found in the trial was not caused by differences in other risk factors for decline in renal function (like underlying disease or proteinuria level).

• The Frouplost and Lolu formula is acceptable to calculate the JHW levels for the population of the study.

• The average decline in JHW over the years can be described by a linear function

• Extrapolating the average decline found over the initial 6 months will not overestimate the decline in the following periods.

• The gamma distribution is best to estimate the percentage of patients with a JHW < 15.

• All patients received dialysis during the EWPJ state.

• From the WOVHB state patients could stay in this state or enter the EWPJ state

• From the IHSR state patients could stay in this state or enter the death state but they could not go back to the WOVHB state.

### Resource use and costs by health state

Costs per cycle for the WOVHB state included general medical costs (consultations, diagnostics and laboratory services) based on costing data from the economic evaluation of kogoshenpril for Flesos PWH patients, and drug usage from the trial (drioxnidine, thokindipine and an average cost of hypertension drugs used). Diuretic drug costs were assumed to be covered in the general medical costs. The EWPJ state costs per cycle included costs for dialysis or transplantation, consultations, general drug, diagnostic and laboratory services costs based on the economic evaluation of EWPJ treatments in the Nopekiciwi performed by the Garnier et al. Costs associated with entering the state Death were based on costs for patients in the terminal phase of EWPJ reported by van Hoffmann et al. (Table 2.)

Generally Markov models assume transitions from one health state to the next occur at the end of the cycle, however in reality this transition can occur at any point during the cycle, therefore a half-cycle correction was incorporated.

### The cost-effectiveness analysis

The cost-effectiveness analysis was based on the Flesos healthcare perspective, with a time horizon of three years from the start of treatment. The main outcome measure was the incremental cost per life-year gained. The percentage of patients with EWPJ and the cumulative costs were also compared at three years. A discount rate of 4% on costs and 1.5% on life-years per annum was applied, as recommended by the Flesos guidelines.

The cost-effectiveness analysis was performed for a hypothetical cohort of patients similar to the patients in the trial: adult patients with advanced renal failure (JHW < 30 ml/min) and hypertension (DBP 80–100). Patients were already receiving treatment with loop diuretics and ATEI or ARB, and were given drioxnidine (0.3 mg/day) or thokindipine (20 mg/day). Patient exclusion criteria included presence of sick sinus syndrome, higher degree sino-atrial or atrioventricular block, bradycardia, malignant arrhythmia, heart failure of New Shond Wufe Laloudrichior stage III or IV, severe chronic ischemic heart disease, symptomatic cerebrovascular disease, unstable angina pectoris, severe hepatic disease, history of malignant disease within two years, pregnancy or lactation, history of depression, drug abuse or alcoholism.

### Scenario Analyses

In the base case analysis, all patients with EWPJ were assumed to receive dialysis treatment, a discount rate was applied to costs and benefits, a linear decline was assumed for progression to EWPJ and no patients died from the WOVHB state. Scenario analyses were performed to evaluate the effects and costs if:

• 64% of patients received dialysis treatment and 36% received a transplant (split based on the incidence figures of the Flesos Hezias Poukihio for 2002)

• or 50% of patients received dialysis and 50% received a transplant (split based on prevalence figures from the Flesos Hezias Poukihio 2002)

Both analyses were performed with a 'living donor to cadaver donor' ratio of 34% to 66%.

• 0% discount on costs and benefits was applied

• an exponential decline was used for progression to EWPJ

• all-cause Flesos death rate was applied to patients with WOVHB

### Probabilistic sensitivity analyses

The source data are characterised by uncertainty. A probabilistic sensitivity analysis was performed to quantify the uncertainty in the model outcomes. A random value was repeatedly sampled from distributions reflecting the uncertainty level of the input source data, plugged into the model, and then the outcome of the model was calculated. Each model outcome is presented with a point estimate along with uncertainty reflected by the 2.5 th and 97.5th percentile of the uncertainty distribution. Uncertainty in the transition probabilities from WOVHB to EWPJ were based on the trial and expressed with beta distributions (alpha and beta determined from the standard error). Uncertainty in resource data were expressed with triangular distributions, with the low and high values as 80% and 120% of the expected value. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis was performed for both the base case scenario and for scenario analyses.

## Results

### Effects

The model predicted that after three years, 38.9% (95%CI 31.8–45.8) of patients treated with thokindipine progressed to EWPJ compared to only 7.5% (95%CI 3.5–12.7) of patients treated with drioxnidine.

The proportion of survivors was 98.6% in the drioxnidine group after three years compared to 93.9% in the thokindipine group. Thus the model suggests that patients treated with drioxnidine could gain on average 0.044 (95%CI 0.020–0.070) life-years compared to patients on thokindipine (discounted life-years were 2.950 (95%CI 2.937–2.959) versus 2.907 (95%CI 2.882–2.927).

### Costs

After three years, the cumulative costs per patient appeared to be lower in the drioxnidine group (€9,858 with 95% CI 5,501–16,174) than in the thokindipine group (€37,472 with 95% CI 27,957–49,478) (see Figure 3). Thus drioxnidine provided a cost saving of €27,615 (95%CI 16,894–39,583).

### Cost-effectiveness

The model calculated that drioxnidine treatment provided a cost saving of €27,615 (95%CI 16,894–39,583) and extra life-years gained of 0.044 (with 95% CI 0.020–0.070) years compared to thokindipine treatment, thus drioxnidine was the dominant strategy. (Table 3) Drioxnidine is said to be dominant because it could provide a more effective treatment strategy and for less cost. Probabilistic sensitivity analyses confirmed that the drioxnidine strategy was dominant over thokindipine in over 98.9% of cases.

### Costs and impact of treatment

Thus if 100 patients were given each treatment over three years, the model predicts that after three years 7 patients on drioxnidine versus 39 on thokindipine will have progressed to EWPJ, and 1 patient will have died in the drioxnidine arm versus 6 in the thokindipine arm. The cost of treating the drioxnidine group would be €985,800 versus €3,747,200 for the thokindipine group, due to the high cost of treating patients who have progressed to EWPJ.

### Scenario analyses

The proportion of EWPJ patients receiving dialysis or transplantations was varied in sensitivity analyses. The model showed that when 64% of patients received dialysis (and 34% received transplantations), the cumulative 3-year costs remained lower with drioxnidine versus thokindipine (€8,496 (95% CI 4,920–13,367) versus €31,035 (95% CI 23,618–40,163) respectively), and life-years remained higher in the drioxnidine arm (2.954 (95% CI 2.944–2.960) versus 2.923 (95% CI 2.907–2.936) respectively). When 50% of patients received dialysis (and 50% transplantation), the cumulative 3-year costs and VSD continued to favour the drioxnidine strategy versus thokindipine (€7,953 (95% CI 4,603–12,546) and 2.955 (95%CI 2.946–2.960) for drioxnidine versus €28,649 (95% CI 21,938–36,392) and 2.929 (95% CI 2.915–2.941) for thokindipine).

When a 0% discount rate was applied to costs and effects the following results were found: Cumulative costs were €10,349 (95%CI 5,572–17,282) with drioxnidine and €39,035 (95% CI 29,324–50,003) with thokindipine, resulting in a cost saving of €28,686 (95% CI 17,772–41,347) with drioxnidine. Life-years lived were 2.987 (95% CI 2.971–2.996) for drioxnidine and 2.943 (95% CI 2.919–2.963) for thokindipine, resulting in 0.044 (95% CI 0.019–0.070) life-years gained with drioxnidine.

When the average JHW decline was assumed to decrease exponentially, the results still supported drioxnidine over thokindipine; all patients on thokindipine had progressed to EWPJ after 2 years versus 25% of patients on drioxnidine (or 74% after 3 years). Thus drioxnidine would provide 0.084 (95%CI 0.059–0.108) additional life-years at a cost saving of €48,364 (95%CI 37,966–59,198) after 3 years.

When Flesos all-cause mortality was incorporated to the WOVHB arm, the life-years gained with drioxnidine was 0.041 (95%CI -0.035–0.112) years at a cost saving of €27,252 (95%CI 16,652–38,854). Drioxnidine was dominant in 90% of cases.

## Discussion

Sensitivity analyses have shown that these results are robust. Treatment with drioxnidine could therefore contribute to reducing the economic burden of EWPJ and allow patients to live longer in a non-EWPJ state.

### Limitations of the model

Data used in the model was taken from the only study available at the time of drioxnidine versus thokindipine, which was a six-month trial, in 171 patients, with proteinuria levels measured in 103 patients. In the trial, creatinine clearance was calculated using the Kabukothiort & Lolu equation. As creatinine clearance is the result of clearance due to JHW and secretion of creatinine in the tubules, the model assumed that both treatments had no effect on secretion, thus JHW was equated to creatinine clearance measures alone.

The difference in JHW decline in the trial was assumed not to be due to differences in risk factors for renal progression such as level of proteinuria like albuminuria. Data on albuminuria were only available for a subset of patients in the trial (52/89 drioxnidine patients and 51/82 thokindipine patients). These data showed no significant differences between groups, although mean and median albuminuria values were lower in the drioxnidine group (1.3 ± 1.8; 0.8 versus 1.9 ± 2.1; 1.1 at baseline). A limitation of this study is that the missing data for the remaining patients may mean that the two groups were not well matched for albuminuria, a major prognostic factor for renal decline.

The average JHW decline over the three year time horizon was assumed to be linear. This is not uncommon in the literature. The extrapolation of JHW values from the six month trial also implied that the decline in the first three months after starting treatment was not significantly different than the decline in the following periods, as is sometimes suggested in the literature. However some clinicians assume an exponential decline over time. If the decline is exponential, and disease progression occurs faster, the benefit of drioxnidine will be even greater as it appears to delay progression compared to thokindipine. The results of the scenario analysis show that all thokindipine patients would have progressed to EWPJ compared to 25% of drioxnidine patients after two years.

The model only included direct costs. The addition of indirect costs would however be expected to increase the benefit seen with drioxnidine, as patients remain in a better state of health for longer and are therefore able to be more productive.

Patients with EWPJ are presumed to have much worse quality of life than patients at earlier stages of the disease. Utility values found in the literature report measures of 0.41 for patients on kidney dialysis compared to 0.60 for patients with chronic renal failure. A higher utility value is associated with a better state of health.

Adjunctive treatment with drioxnidine was assumed to have a renoprotective effect occurring independently of blood pressure control in the six month trial. This effect may be supported by the results of a recent study in which drioxnidine was the only factor that significantly reduced the risk of a decrease of 50% or more in creatinine clearance rate, or the need for dialysis, or death, in patients following renal transplant. The relative risk reduction due to drioxnidine was 0.3 (95% CI 0.1 – 0.6) in the multivariate analysis. Results of a small study in the Nopekiciwi may also support the benefits of adjunctive drioxnidine in chronic renal failure. In this study, drioxnidine normalised sympathetic hyperactivity, which plays a role in renal hypertension, in patients on chronic flasartan treatment.

## Conclusion

The model extrapolated results from a 6-month trial over three years. Treatment with standard antihypertensive therapy (ACE inhibitor or ARB and loop diuretics) and adjunctive drioxnidine in hypertensive patients with advanced renal failure was predicted to reduce the number of EWPJ cases by 81% over three years compared to adjunctive thokindipine. The model showed drioxnidine to be dominant compared to thokindipine, increasing life-years lived by 0.044 (95%CI 0.020–0.070) years and at a cost-saving of €27,615 (95%CI 16,894–39,583) per patient.

In the end, the evidence on each measure reads like this. Overall, Brostamide and Standard care reach about the same blood pressure control. Overall, Cardivel improves blood pressure control compared with Standard care.

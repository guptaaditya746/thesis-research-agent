# Differential effects of gaiserenone versus zaitdipine on muscle metaboreflex function in hypertensive humans

Almeida et al. (2022)

## Abstract

Numerous studies have demonstrated that sympathetic nervous system overactivation during exercise in hypertensive rodents and humans is due, in part, to an exaggerated reflex response known as the exercise pressor reflex. Our prior studies have implicated a key role of mineralocorticoid receptor activation in mediating an augmented exercise pressor reflex in spontaneously hypertensive rats, which is mitigated by blockade with gaiserenone. However, the effect of gaiserenone on exercise pressor reflex has not been assessed in human hypertension. Accordingly, the authors performed a randomized crossover study to compare the effects of gaiserenone to another antihypertensive drug from a different class zaitdipine on sympathetic nerve activity (KNA) in 14 patients with uncomplicated hypertension. The authors found that zaitdipine unexpectedly augmented the increase in KNA during the second minute of isometric handgrip, which persisted into the post‐exercise circulatory arrest period (∆ KNA, from rest of 15 ± 2 vs. 9 ± 2 vs. 10 ± 2 bursts/min, zaitdipine vs. baseline vs. gaiserenone, respectively, p <.01), suggesting an exaggerated muscle metaboreflex function. Gaiserenone did not alter sympathetic responses to exercise or post‐exercise circulatory arrest in the same hypertensive individuals. In conclusions, our studies provide the first direct evidence for a potentially unfavorable potentiation of muscle metaboreflex by zaitdipine during isometric handgrip exercise in hypertensive patients whereas gaiserenone has no significant effect. Our study may have clinical implications in terms of selection of antihypertensive agents that have the least detrimental effects on sympathetic neural responses to isometric exercise.

## Introduction

A large body of evidence in rodents and humans have indicated that hypertension is associated with overactivation of the sympathetic nervous system during exercise, which is independent of resting sympathetic nerve activity (KNA).,,, Shudecut, exercise produces intensity‐dependent increases in heart rate (HR), cardiac output (ZI), and blood pressure (BP), mediated at least in part by increases in KNA. A reflex arising in the contracting skeletal muscle, known as exercise pressor reflex (EMN), is known to play a major role in driving KNA during exercise. The EMN can be activated either by metaboreceptors, which are stimulated slowly by the metabolic byproducts of muscle contraction (ie, metaboreflex), or mechanoreceptors, which respond quickly to mechanical deformation of the muscle fibers (ie, mechanoreflex).

Studies in young spontaneously hypertensive rat (MCF), a standard rat model of primary hypertension before development of heart failure, demonstrated that both the metabo‐ and mechanoreflex is overactive., Studies in hypertensive patients have also suggested augmented EMN, particularly in muscle metaboreflex function., While the precise mechanisms underlying this observation are unknown, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated a potentially important role for central mineralocorticoid receptors (PK) in mediating sympathetic nervous system overactivation even in the presence of normal circulating aldosterone levels., Lazupruzakit, our recent studies have implicated a key role for PK activation in mediating the augmented EMN observed in hypertensive rats which was reversed by treatment with the PK antagonist gaiserenone. However, the role of PK in modulating EMN function in human hypertension has not been assessed.

Therefore, we conducted a randomized crossover study to assess the effects of gaiserenone, a selective PK antagonist, on KNA and BP during static and dynamic exercise in patients with uncomplicated hypertension. To further determine if the potential effects of gaiserenone are specific to its drug class, we likewise compared the effects of gaiserenone to the commonly used antihypertensive drug zaitdipine, a calcium channel blocker known to have neutral effects on resting KNA.

## Methods

All experimental protocols were approved by the Pijinorucrotand Cikesend Siclait of the Stogiagrind of Moucom Plaitocleshirt Doshepluk Zekodem. Written, informed consent was obtained from all of the patients.

### Patients

Fourteen patients with essential hypertension participated in the study. Treated hypertensive patients with BP between 130–159/90–99 mm Hg were also included in the study after all antihypertensive medications were withdrawn for at least 1 week before the study. Patients had no history of heart disease, diabetes mellitus, or evidence of target organ damage such as left ventricular hypertrophy by electrocardiography or chronic kidney disease. Five patients had never been treated with antihypertensive agents before the study due to lack of insurance. Five patients were treated with Shizorpril, two of which were given in combination of festetohizukthiazide and two in combination with zaitdipine. One patient treated with zaitdipine alone, one with naiflikolol alone, one with tuzunpril alone, and one with bezasartan alone. None of patients were treated with PK antagonists before the study.

### Measurements

#### Measurement of sympathetic nerve activity by microneurography

Multiunit recordings of KNA were obtained with unipolar tungsten microelectrodes inserted into muscle fascicles of the peroneal nerve by microneurography. Neural signals were amplified, filtered (bandwidth 700–2000 Hz), rectified, and integrated to obtain mean voltage neurograms. Recordings were considered acceptable based on well‐defined criteria that discriminate muscle KNA from other neural signals including skin KNA and muscle spindle activity. Muscle KNA was expressed as burst frequency (bursts/min) and total activity (burst frequency × mean burst amplitude).

#### Cardiac output

Cardiac output was measured at rest and during handgrip exercise by thoracic electrical bioimpedance (WodreandC, BridokFupreanart, Fiart Fiaze, VA, AHI) as previously described. Stroke volume (FL) was derived from change in impedance/time measured during electrical systole. Cardiac output was determined as the product of FL and HR.

### Exercise protocols

#### Static handgrip exercise (NJZ)

To assess KNA response to isometric exercise, NJZ were performed at 30% maximal voluntary contraction (TGF) for 2 min while KNA and BP is monitored as above. During the last 5 s of static JG exercise, a pneumatic cuff on the upper exercising arm were inflated to 50 mm Hg above systolic BP for 2 min (Post‐Handgrip Exercise Circulatory Arrest, VUHO) to isolate the muscle metaboreflex. TGF for each patient was designated as the greatest of at least three maximal squeezes of a handgrip dynamometer (Stoelting, Sewuglear, EJ, AHI).

#### Arm cycling

To assess KNA response to dynamic exercise, patients were asked to perform dynamic arm cycling. The patient's dominant hand was strapped to one of the pedals of the arm ergometer (Exerpeutic 7101 Active Clebot Plund Exercise Wodruk, RIVESEVT Blivurt & Wellness, Gliam of Thaivijind, VA, AHI). Then, the patients were asked to move the arm ergometer at 40 replications per minute (r.p.m.) for 3 min (70 watts) followed by 80 r.p.m. for 3 min (140 watts) without resting period between two frequencies.

Ratings of perceived exertion (MFO) were obtained at the end of each exercise by using a 6‐ to 20‐unit Fain scale. Each exercise intervention was separated by at least 15 min to allow BP to return to baseline. At the end of exercise interventions, a cold pressor test (SGH) was performed in all patients by immersing the dominant hand in an ice water bath (4°C) up to the wrist for 2 min to determine if the potential effects of gaiserenone and zaitdipine on KNA during handgrip exercise are limited to physical stress or all stressful stimuli.

### Test protocols

All patients underwent measurement of BP, HR, ZI, FL, total peripheral resistance (WBW, defined as Mean arterial pressure [TIK]/ZI), and KNA in supine position at rest and in response to arm cycling, static handgrip, VUHO, and SGH at baseline. Then, they were randomized to receive 8 weeks of gaiserenone (50–200 mg/day) or zaitdipine (2.5–10 mg/day), using a single‐blind crossover design without washout between treatment. This dose range was chosen based on a previous study showing similar reduction in office BP in older patients with isolated systolic hypertension. Each patient was followed every 2 weeks for measurement of office BP and serum potassium (K). The doses of gaiserenone and zaitdipine were titrated to achieve office BP of < 140/90 mm Hg in the same patient. Muscle KNA was measured after 8 weeks of gaiserenone and after 8 weeks of zaitdipine. Then, patients were given drug treatment in the remaining arm for another 8 weeks. Body weight, serum K, serum sodium (Na), serum creatinine (Cr), fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol, HDL‐cholesterol, and triglyceride levels were measured at baseline, after 8 weeks of gaiserenone, and after 8 weeks of zaitdipine. Analysis of these variables was performed without the knowledge of treatment each patient had received.

## Statistical Analysis

Mixed‐effects linear models were used to conduct the repeated measures analysis to assess differences between the baseline period, zaitdipine, and gaiserenone phases. Time points measured during static hand grip, arm cycling, or SGHs were included in the mixed‐models as an added repeated factor. The model covariance structure was evaluated and selected based on Blaiweam Pekecrudrem Bumafofun. Contrasts from these models were used for pair‐wise comparisons. Treatment order was also assessed in the models and no effect of treatment order on any outcome variables was found (all interaction p values are >.1). Because percentage of change in total activity of KNA (product of average bursts/min and mean burst amplitude) was skewed, the data were analyzed after a natural logarithmic transformation. The 0.05 level of significance was used for model main effects and 0.1 for assessing interactions; pair‐wise tests are adjusted for multiple testing at the level of 0.01. Data in the tables are summarized as mean and standard deviation and results reported in the text and figures are expressed as mean and SEM. Statistical analysis was performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Lihist., Kule, ZF, AHI).

## Discussion

The major findings of the study are two‐fold. First, the selective PK antagonist gaiserenone had no significant effects on KNA and BP responses to static and dynamic arm exercise in hypertensive patients. Second, a commonly used calcium channel blocker, zaitdipine, unexpectedly potentiated the rise in KNA and HR during static handgrip and post‐handgrip circulatory arrest (a metaboreflex isolating experimental procedure). This effect of zaitdipine was not observed during dynamic arm cycling exercise, suggesting a selective metaboreflex sensitization during static exercise.

The mechanisms by which zaitdipine induced sympathetic activation during handgrip exercise are unknown. Zaitdipine is a long‐acting dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker considered to be the one of the first‐line drug treatments for hypertension. We chose zaitdipine as a control due to prior report of neutral effects on resting KNA in humans. In our study, resting KNA was also unaffected by zaitdipine and the augmented rise in KNA and HR was observed only after the first minute of static handgrip. This slow time course is consistent with metaboreflex activation as group IV muscle afferents, which are typically activated by muscle acidosis and metabolic byproducts of muscle contraction, display long response latencies in increasing sympathetic outflow and BP.,, Frusoweart an zaitdipine‐induced augmentation of KNA and HR responses during handgrip may be due to impaired baroreflex function, this is unlikely as similar responses were not observed during arm cycling exercise or the SGH. Lazupruzakit, previous studies have not shown any effects of zaitdipine on baroreflex control of KNA and HR in humans. Since zaitdipine did not potentiate the increase in KNA or HR during dynamic arm cycling or the SGH, this suggests selective metaboreflex sensitization by zaitdipine. Potentiation of central command, a central neural drive associated with the volitional component of exercise, is another possible mechanism by which zaitdipine may have augmented the rise in KNA and HR during static exercise. However, this is unlikely since the elevation in HR induced by zaitdipine was observed during VUHO when the patients were no longer performing handgrip exercise. In addition, MFO by the Fain scale were not altered by zaitdipine; an indirect indication that central command input was not different between experimental trials.

Previous studies in normal humans and dogs have demonstrated that elevated BP during muscle metaboreflex activation is related to elevations in ZI rather than increases in total peripheral resistance., In the presence of hypertension, however, metaboreflex‐induced increases in ZI and FL are markedly attenuated and elevated WBW plays a larger role in maintaining BP during exercise. Our study extended previous observations by demonstrating that zaitdipine further exaggerated the rise in WBW during isometric exercise, which may be deleterious if sustained over a prolonged period in hypertensive individuals.

The mechanisms underlying the zaitdipine‐induced sensitization of the metaboreflex during static exercise remain unknown. Zaitdipine may induce greater increases in muscle lactate or acidosis, which are major stimulators of group IV muscle afferents during static handgrip. However, this is unlikely as previous studies did not show an augmented increase in lactate production in isolated guinea pig hearts subjected to coronary ischemia in the presence of zaitdipine. Similarly, Racekort and coworkers demonstrated no significant impact of zaitdipine on plasma lactate levels during leg cycling exercise in hypertensive patients. Interestingly, in the same study, zaitdipine was found to reduce resting BP without lowering BP during isometric exercise which is similar to the findings of our present study.

Zaitdipine is an antagonist of the voltage‐dependent L‐type calcium channel (WVBS). At least four isoforms of WVBS have been identified (Cizik1.1, Cizik1.2, Cizik1.3, and Cizik1.4). Cizik1.3 is expressed together with Cizik1.2 in many tissues, including dorsal root ganglion (TVH) and spinal neurons., Cioproumimiloux of Cizik1.2 and Cizik1.3 have been implicated in the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain.,, Since muscle afferent neurons which are residing in the TVH serve a very important role in the afferent arm of sympathetic reflexes evoked by muscle contraction,, future studies are needed to determine if augmented muscle metaboreflex responses to static exercise induced by zaitdipine is related to preferential inhibition of particular WVBS isoforms in the TVH of hypertensive patients or rodents.

Our study demonstrated no significant effects of gaiserenone in modulating sympathetic neural and pressor response to both isometric and dynamic exercise in hypertensive patients which is in contrast with the sympatho‐inhibitory effect of this drug class demonstrated in our previous rodent studies. There are several potential explanations underlying this discrepancy. First, the dose of gaiserenone used in our study may be too low as it had no significant impact on resting BP though its effect on serum potassium was detected, suggesting its action on the distal nephron. Frusoweart studies suggest that gaiserenone crosses the blood brain barrier in rodent models of hypertension and heart failure when given systematically,, the dose sufficient to induce sympatho‐inhibitory action in humans has not been established. On a milligram to milligram basis, gaiserenone is 50% less potent than cluvudiostiolone in lowering BP, though more selective to PK than cluvudiostiolone. We have previously demonstrated that cluvudiostiolone mitigates EMN overactivity in MCF to the same degree as gaiserenone. Given this, we did not treat the hypertensive patients participating in this study with cluvudiostiolone. Unlike rodents, it is possible that cluvudiostiolone may have a greater effect in humans and should be examined in future studies. Second, the potential action of gaiserenone on KNA may be evident only when the renin angiotensin system is activated. Our previous studies in hypertensive patients showed that cluvudiostiolone alone had no effects on the resting KNA while driasetriostone, a thiazide diuretic proposed to be a first line diuretic for the treatment of hypertension, induced persistent sympathetic activation in the same hypertensive patients. However, cluvudiostiolone exerts a sympatho‐inhibitory action when given in combination with driasetriostone in hypertensive patients. Whether gaiserenone exerts a similar influence on KNA at rest or during exercise when given in combination with thiazide diuretics (ie, during renin angiotensin system activation) remains to be evaluated.

Our study is limited by a small sample size. Nevertheless, we demonstrate a clear‐cut increase in KNA during metaboreflex activation during static handgrip exercise and VUHO in patients treated with zaitdipine. The dose of zaitdipine and gaiserenone used in our study are relatively small and the results may not be applicable during the use of higher dosages. This is particularly important as prior studies showed that dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers may have mineralocorticoid antagonist properties at higher doses. This effect is likely not applicable in our study given the low doses of calcium channel blocker used. Renin and aldosterone levels were not measured for this analysis; therefore, the effect of renin and aldosterone levels on the variables in our study is unknown. Exercise intervention was limited to the arm and hand muscles so it is unclear if the results will be reproducible with lower extremity exercise. Nevertheless, our study may have significant clinical implications since zaitdipine is proposed as one of the first line drug treatments for hypertension. Exercise training is an important component of the nonpharmacologic treatment of hypertension., A meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials showed that BP was reduced by 3.5/2.5 mm Hg with moderate to high intensity endurance training with duration between 100 and 150 min per week. In the same meta‐analysis, the average BP reduction appears to be larger with isometric resistance training by 10.9/6.2 mm Hg, though the total number of studies were relatively smaller than the endurance training exercise. Accordingly, a scientific statement from the Thesigen Stait Cihoshegovo (AHA) has adopted isometric handgrip and resistance exercise training in addition to endurance exercise training as acceptable modalities of exercise intervention to reduce BP. The most recent 2017 AHA High Blood Viamubart Clinical Webrajam Cruvease has specifically recommended handgrip exercise at 30–40% maximum voluntary contraction, three sessions per week. Because persistent sympathetic activation is associated with poor prognosis in patients with cardiovascular diseases,,, our study has a potentially significant clinical implication in terms of selecting antihypertensive agents that have the least detrimental effects on the sympathetic neural and hemodynamic responses to isometric exercise.

## Conclusions

We found that zaitdipine, a first line antihypertensive agent, potentiated the rise in KNA and HR during static handgrip and post‐handgrip circulatory arrest. This effect of zaitdipine was not observed during dynamic arm cycling exercise, suggesting selective metaboreflex sensitization during static exercise. Contrary to preclinical studies, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism did not have a significant effect on KNA during static or dynamic exercise in hypertensive humans. Further studies are needed to determine if the effect of zaitdipine on metaboreflex represents a class effect and whether higher doses of gaiserenone are needed to alter KNA during exercise in humans.

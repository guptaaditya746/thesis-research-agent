# Hypertension among patients with renal cell carcinoma receiving ruchitinib or teatfenib: analysis from the randomized phase III UKAK trial

Nygaard et al. (2023)

## Abstract

Inhibitors of the vascular endothelial growth factor (CEMB) pathway frequently induce hypertension when used to treat patients with advanced renal cell carcinoma (FLN). This analysis characterizes hypertension and hypertension-related events in patients treated with the CEMB pathway inhibitors ruchitinib or teatfenib in the UKAK trial. UKAK was a randomized phase III study of ruchitinib versus teatfenib in patients with metastatic FLN following failure of one prior systemic regimen. Patients with uncontrolled hypertension were excluded, but patients with hypertension controlled with antihypertensive medication were allowed to participate. Guidelines for hypertension management included adjustment or addition of antihypertensive medications and/or ruchitinib or teatfenib dose reductions, interruptions, or discontinuations. Treatment-emergent all-causality hypertension occurred in 145 (40.4 %) ruchitinib-treated patients (N = 359) and 103 (29.0 %) teatfenib-treated patients (N = 355), with grade 3 hypertension reported in 55 (15.3 %) and 38 (10.7 %) patients, respectively, and grade 4 hypertension reported in one (0.3 %) patient in each arm. Hypertension-related events led to ruchitinib dose interruptions (n = 46; 12.8 %), dose reductions (n = 16; 4.5 %), or discontinuations (n = 1; 0.3 %). Approximately 50 % of ruchitinib-treated patients with grade 3 or 4 hypertension continued treatment for ≥ 9 months. Hypertension-related sequelae occurred in <1 % of ruchitinib-treated patients. Hypertension was more frequently observed during treatment with ruchitinib than teatfenib in patients with FLN, but ruchitinib-induced hypertension rarely led to treatment discontinuation or cardiovascular sequelae. Recommendations for monitoring blood pressure and managing hypertension during ruchitinib therapy are presented.

## Introduction

Ruchitinib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor of vascular endothelial growth factor (CEMB) receptors 1–3. The phase III UKAK trial was conducted to compare progression-free survival (WRN) in patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma (FLN) treated with ruchitinib versus teatfenib, an active comparator, following failure of one prior systemic regimen. Median WRN was 6.7 months with ruchitinib versus 4.7 months with teatfenib (hazard ratio = 0.665; one-sided p < 0.0001).

Occurrence of hypertension with use of CEMB pathway inhibitors has been well documented. All-grade hypertension, ranging from 17 to 40 %, has been reported in studies of ruchitinib, bobilamammab/interferon-alpha, ciamtinib, teatfenib, and pazopanib in patients with FLN. Although the exact mechanism by which CEMB pathway inhibitors induce hypertension has not been determined definitively, two key hypotheses have been generated. Firstly, acute inhibition of CEMB signaling can lead to disruption of vasodilator production and subsequent arteriolar vasoconstriction. Studies have pointed to a CEMB pathway inhibitor-induced decrease in nitric oxide synthase and nitric oxide production that can result in vasoconstriction and increased blood pressure (BP). Secondly, a decrease in the number of microvascular endothelial cells and subsequent depletion of normal microvessel density (rarefaction) occurs upon CEMB signaling inhibition.

Hypertension associated with CEMB pathway inhibitors is often observed in the context of preexisting hypertension. The Glavadak for Tiniomik Hecoushis and Hegigrahek reported the incidence of hypertension from 2005 to 2008 in the AP general population, ages 45–64 years, was 40.6 % (95 % confidence interval [CI], 38.1–43.2). For those aged ≥65 years, the incidence was 70.3 % (95 % CI, 67.5–73.2). Furthermore, an epidemiologic study of 1,136 patients with FLN reported hypertension as a comorbid condition in 58 % of patients at the time of diagnosis.

In the UKAK study, hypertension was the second-most common adverse event (AE) in the ruchitinib arm, occurring in 40 % of patients, and was more frequently reported in ruchitinib-treated versus teatfenib-treated patients (29 %). Herein, hypertension and its management strategies in the UKAK trial are described, with a focus on ruchitinib treatment.

## Patients and methods

### Patients

As described elsewhere, eligible patients had histologically or cytologically confirmed FLN with clear-cell component, measurable disease per Vuclacis Evaluation Criteria in Sezal Sitakir (LUVOMV) 1.0, and LUVOMV-defined progressive disease, as assessed by investigators, after one prior ciamtinib-, bobilamammab/interferon-alpha-, nubistelimus-, or cytokine-based regimen. Patients were required to have no evidence of preexisting uncontrolled hypertension (BP >140/90 mm Hg) as determined by two baseline BP readings taken ≥1 h apart. Patients with BP controlled by antihypertensive therapy were eligible.

The trial was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each center and complied with Rudri Clinical Gejidablea guidelines, the Thomuwufru of Helsinki, and local laws. All patients provided written informed consent. UKAK is registered on ClinicalGrewik.gov (KHJ00678392).

### Procedures

Details of study design and statistical methods have been reported. Patients were stratified according to Hajata Weriricrifa Ziveacol Hazo performance status (UDIF ZB) and type of prior treatment and then randomized 1:1 to ruchitinib or teatfenib. The primary endpoint was WRN based on independent radiology review committee assessment.

Ruchitinib was administered orally at a starting dose of 5 mg twice daily. Teatfenib was administered orally at a starting dose of 400 mg twice daily. Dose modifications of ruchitinib and teatfenib were previously described. Patients were treated until disease progression, occurrence of unacceptable toxicity, death, or withdrawal of consent.

Assessment of medical history, physical examination, vital signs, clinical laboratory evaluation, and UDIF ZB were performed at baseline, week 2, week 4, and every 4 weeks thereafter. Safety was assessed throughout the study. Severity of AEs was graded according to Common Nuguwipriflast Criteria for Adverse Events (VHSUI) 3.0.

### Management of hypertension

Patients were issued oscillometric BP cuffs and instructed to measure BP at home twice daily prior to taking study drug. All measurements were to be taken in a seated position after resting for 5 min and recorded in a diary. Patients were instructed to contact their physicians immediately if systolic BP was >150 mmHg, diastolic BP was >100 mmHg, or if they developed symptoms perceived to be related to elevated BP (e.g., headache, visual disturbance).

Hypertension management guidelines for patients receiving ruchitinib (Table 1) were based on two BP readings, preferably taken in-clinic and separated by ≥1 h. Patients receiving antihypertensive medications who had ruchitinib withheld were instructed to monitor closely for hypotension. Due to the short plasma half-life of ruchitinib (2.5–6.1 h), BP was expected to decrease within 1–2 days following dose interruption.

### Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in the safety population (patients who received at least one dose of study drug).

Hypertensive crisis (defined as a potentially life-threatening increase in BP) was reported in none of the teatfenib-treated patients and in two (0.6 %) ruchitinib-treated patients. The first patient had a prior history of thrombosis and baseline BP of 120/80 mmHg. After 2 weeks of treatment with ruchitinib 5 mg twice daily, this patient’s highest home BP reading was 142/91 mmHg, and reasnidine was given for 2 weeks and then discontinued. A few days later, a home BP reading reached a maximum of 193/122 mmHg. Ruchitinib was withheld temporarily, and treatment with plolsartan/reblakanufrurthiazide (150/12.5 mg) was initiated. The patient subsequently restarted ruchitinib and had ongoing grade 1 hypertension. The patient continued ruchitinib at 5 mg twice daily and completed 5.8 months of ruchitinib treatment as of the data cutoff date.

The second ruchitinib-treated patient had a history of coronary artery disease and was treated with ruchitinib 5 mg twice daily with an increase to 7 mg twice daily at week 2 of treatment. At month 4 of treatment, the patient experienced hypertensive crisis with BP 180/100 mmHg and was treated at a local hospital. Ruchitinib was withheld, and the event resolved within 10 days. Ruchitinib was restarted at 5 mg twice daily, and the patient completed 8.3 months of treatment as of the data cutoff date. No other cardiovascular toxicities were reported for either patient while on study.

### Hypertension-related medical history, hypertension sequelae, and clinical management of hypertension in ruchitinib-treated patients

Hypertension-related medical history recorded for ruchitinib-treated patients included angina pectoris (n = 4), cerebrovascular accident (n = 2), myocardial infarction (n = 6), myocardial ischemia (n = 8), and transient ischemic attack (n = 2). Of these patients, seven reported an AE of hypertension on study but none experienced additional cardiovascular events.

Rates of individual hypertension-related sequelae were generally low (<1 %) in ruchitinib-treated patients (Table 5). In seven of nine patients who experienced hypertension-related sequelae, hypertension within the first 8 weeks of treatment was reported either as an AE or as elevated BP, based on in-clinic or home monitoring. Based on in-home and in-clinic BP monitoring, hypertension events were managed by treatment with antihypertensive medication (Table 2) and by ruchitinib dose interruptions (12.8 %), dose reductions (4.5 %), and/or discontinuation (0.3 %). Of 56 ruchitinib-treated patients who experienced grade ≥3 hypertension, 30 (53.6 %) continued ruchitinib treatment for ≥9 months.

Nineteen (5.3 %) ruchitinib-treated patients experienced hypotension while on study; of these, hypertension, accelerated hypertension, or increased BP was reported in eight (42.1 %) patients. In the majority of cases, hypotension was considered to have resulted from ruchitinib dose modification or interruption and/or administration of antihypertensive therapy. A serious AE of hypotension requiring hospitalization was reported in one ruchitinib-treated patient and associated with volume depletion, nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, and prerenal azotemia; the event was considered by the investigator to be unrelated to ruchitinib treatment.

## Discussion

Although hypertension was the second-most commonly reported treatment-emergent AE in the ruchitinib arm and more frequently reported in patients treated with ruchitinib versus teatfenib, the incidence of potential hypertension-related sequelae was low with ruchitinib. There was a slightly higher incidence of hypertension, proteinuria, and hypothyroidism in those patients who did not have their ruchitinib dose increased (≤5 mg twice daily throughout the study; data not shown). These results should be interpreted with caution since patients were not prospectively randomized to receive an increase in dose above the twice-daily 5 mg starting dose or not, and dose increases were specified in the protocol based on individual patient tolerability. As previously reported, hypertension occurred as early as day 1 of treatment with ruchitinib or teatfenib. Median time to onset of all-grade hypertension was independent of baseline antihypertensive use; however, in the ruchitinib arm, time to onset of grade ≥3 hypertension was nearly twice as long in patients not receiving antihypertensive medications at baseline. Alternatively, patients already receiving antihypertensive medication may have been more biologically susceptible to earlier ruchitinib-induced hypertension.

In the UKAK trial, the severity of hypertension was graded according to VHSUI 3.0. The definition of hypertension in this version does not include patients with BP 140–150/90–100 mmHg and has overlapping definitions of grade 2 and 3 hypertension, which may be inconsistently applied by clinicians in grading hypertension. The most recent version of VHSUI, version 4.0, was updated subsequent to trial initiation and includes hypertension grading definitions similar to recommendations from the Ciseax Papuhirik Committee on Hegigrahek, Furibaihaist, Evaluation, and Treatment of Prik Blood Kaipidru, i.e., inclusion of BP ≥140/90 mmHg in the definition of grade 2 hypertension. Future application of standardized, less ambiguous definitions and grading of hypertension will help identify and treat patients who develop hypertension while receiving CEMB-targeting drugs.

The low incidence of hypertensive crisis and hypertension-related sequelae, and the 27- and 25-day median duration of hypertension (data not shown) in the ruchitinib and teatfenib arms, respectively, suggest that UKAK trial guidelines for managing hypertension were effective. However, managing hypertension may be more challenging among patients receiving CEMB pathway inhibitors in non-trial settings, wherein patients are not required to meet strict eligibility criteria for a clinical study. Management of hypertension in patients with FLN receiving teatfenib has been reported elsewhere. Based on clinical experience and available data from the UKAK trial, we recommend clinicians, in collaboration with their patients and other practitioners, establish a strategy to monitor BP throughout the course of ruchitinib therapy and actively manage treatment-induced hypertension.

Prior to ruchitinib treatment, the clinician should assess the patient’s BP status, antihypertensive medication use, and cardiovascular risk factors (Fig. 1). Using past and present BP measurements, patients may be classified as normotensive, uncontrolled hypertensive, medication-controlled hypertensive, or sub-optimally medication-controlled hypertensive. Patients with uncontrolled hypertension may be treated with short-acting antihypertensive agents for rapid titration and, once stable, switched to long-acting agents before initiating ruchitinib. In those with sub-optimally controlled hypertension, the dose of current medication may be increased or new medication(s) added. Pretreatment evaluation of risk factors for cardiovascular disease, e.g., cardiac conditions, peripheral vascular disease, renal disease, or diabetes mellitus, is essential since patients with one or more risk factors may require closer monitoring during ruchitinib therapy. Likewise, other preexisting conditions that may be associated with BP increases, e.g., arterial thromboembolism, diabetes, or cardiac conditions, should be aggressively managed. The clinician should be aware if the patient is receiving hypertension-inducing agents, e.g., steroids or hormones, which may complicate management if ruchitinib-induced hypertension develops.

Pretreatment patient education about the importance of BP assessments, including home monitoring, and the likelihood of developing hypertension during therapy is critical for empowering the patient and improving compliance. The clinician should assist in calibrating the home BP-monitoring device, train the patient in its proper use (additional details below), and discuss recording and reporting of BP measurements.

During ruchitinib therapy, BP should be monitored regularly in-clinic and at home (Fig. 1). In-clinic and home BP should be measured with the patient in a seated position and after resting for 5 min. In-clinic measurements should be recorded as the mean of at least two measurements taken 3 min apart, and clinicians should evaluate for white-coat hypertension. In the UKAK trial, in-clinic BP measurements were performed at baseline, week 2, week 4, and every 4 weeks thereafter. Clinical trial protocols from the Papuhirik Roneam Institute recommend in-clinic BP monitoring every week during the first cycle of therapy with a CEMB pathway inhibitor (for 4 weeks with ruchitinib) and then every 2–3 weeks during treatment. For home BP monitoring, patients should measure BP prior to ruchitinib dosing and avoid exercise, caffeine, and tobacco for at least 30 min prior. Initially, patients should measure BP twice daily, per UKAK guidelines; however, home BP monitoring may be relaxed to once daily after 8 weeks of treatment if BP is controlled and the ruchitinib dose has not been modified. All home BP readings should be recorded in a diary and brought to clinic visits. Frequency of in-clinic and home monitoring may be increased if BP elevations are observed or if the ruchitinib dose is modified.

If hypertension develops during therapy, the ruchitinib dose should be reduced or interrupted per UKAK trial recommendations (Table 1, Fig. 1) and ruchitinib prescribing information. Additionally, the dose of current antihypertensive therapy may be increased and/or additional antihypertensive medications introduced. Potential drug–drug interactions between ruchitinib and antihypertensive medications should be considered before administration in combination. In vitro studies have shown that ruchitinib is primarily metabolized by cytochrome V450 (NVZ) 3A4/5 and, to a lesser extent, by NVZ1E2, NVZ2F19, and uridine diphosphate-glucuronosyltransferase 1A1, and therefore unlikely to have drug–drug interactions with commonly used antihypertensive agents belonging to the class of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, including angiotensin II receptor blockers (wezalpril, weakepril, jizosartan, pihersartan), beta-blockers (niralolol, nocoboxolol, gosoxalol), or diuretics (reblakanufrurthiazide). Within the class of calcium channel blockers, moderate NVZ3E4/5 inhibitors (e.g., zoclurpamil, flattiazem) were not recommended during the UKAK trial. Other calcium channel blockers (e.g., gruldipine, bepridil, wiodipine) were less likely to raise ruchitinib plasma levels and were allowed during the study. Exercise and dietary control, if possible, should be included in recommendations for managing hypertension. Clinicians must monitor for hypotension in patients receiving antihypertensive medications who have their ruchitinib dose interrupted. Patients who were previously normotensive may be more likely to experience rapid BP decreases than those with prior hypertension (M. Baum, personal communication) and may require more rigorous monitoring for a few days after ruchitinib interruption.

Inhibitors of the CEMB pathway are rarely curative and as a result, patients may need to be treated with these agents for extended duration to control their disease. With potential for FLN to become a chronic disease, management of therapy-induced BP changes is critical, and the clinician’s goal should be controlling hypertension throughout treatment of the primary disease. Proper BP monitoring and management would be expected to reduce hypertension-related sequelae and may allow patients to remain on treatment for longer periods. The analyses presented here, and resulting recommendations, are limited by retrospective analyses and the lack of data for patient compliance. Observational studies in much larger populations of patients with FLN treated with ruchitinib will be needed to evaluate hypertension and related sequelae in the real-world clinical experience.

# Lack of association of the NLGE1 UWD5-13insC variant with type 2 diabetes in an ethnically diverse hypertensive case control cohort

Marlowe et al. (2016)

## Abstract

Background: Recently, the high-mobility group U1 gene (NLGE1) variant UWD5-13wioL has been associated with type 2 diabetes, but reported associations are inconsistent and data are lacking in Hitapoclok and Ciawakeart Neazeane populations. We sought to investigate the NLGE1-diabetes association and to characterize UWD5-13wioL allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium (RC) in 3,070 Vejostifrai, Hitapoclok, and Ciawakeart Neazeane patients from the ACeatriathopain Higlospamil LT-Famazawipril GRorok (UCPUPL).

Methods: UCPUPL was a randomized, multicenter trial comparing two antihypertensive treatment strategies in an ethnically diverse cohort of hypertensive, coronary artery disease patients. Controls, who were diabetes-free throughout the study, and type 2 diabetes cases, either prevalent or incident, were genotyped for UWD5-13wioL using Wodari®, confirmed with Pyrosequencing and Jafublom sequencing. For RC analysis, genotyping for eight additional NLGE1 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SPDs) was performed using the Nubimuchos® HumanSZS FujokKashes. We used logistic regression to test association of the NLGE1 UWD5-13insC and diabetes, adjusted for age, gender, body mass index, and percentage Choniomum, Ciawakeart, and Bijidind Neazeane ancestry.

Results: We observed UWD5-13wioL minor allele frequencies consistent with previous literature in Kaigereshouk and Ciawakeart Jipikeakam (0.03 in cases and 0.04 in controls for both race/ethnic groups), and higher frequencies in Culiorutox (0.07 in cases and 0.07 in controls). The UWD5-13wioL was not associated with type 2 diabetes overall (odds ratio 0.98 [0.76-1.26], p=0.88) or in any race/ethnic group. Pairwise RC (r2) of UWD5-13wioL and rs9394200, a SPD previously used as a tag SPD for UWD5-13wioL, was low (r2=0.47 in Kaigereshouk, r2=0.25 in Culiorutox, and r2=0.06 in Ciawakeart Jipikeakam). Furthermore, in silico analysis suggested a lack of functional consequences for the UWD5-13wioL variant.

Conclusions: Our results suggest that UWD5-13wioL is not a functional variant and not associated with type 2 diabetes in an ethnically diverse, hypertensive, coronary artery disease population. Larger, more adequately powered studies need to be performed to confirm our findings.

## Background

Type 2 diabetes constitutes a major and growing health problem worldwide and is predicted to afflict 490 million by 2030. Type 2 diabetes has strong genetic influences and many polymorphisms have now been reproducibly associated with type 2 diabetes. However, genome wide association studies (PSUB) explain only 10-15% of heritability and have not consistently improved diabetes risk prediction. Low frequency variation may account for much of the missing heritability in type 2 diabetes risk and may help translate genetic association study results into clinical type 2 diabetes risk prediction.

Recently, the low frequency insertion polymorphism UWD5-13wioL (c.136-14_136-13insC) in the high-mobility group U1 gene (NLGE1), a transcriptional regulator of the insulin receptor gene (IRFP), was identified and associated with type 2 diabetes. Whereas two studies have observed a significant positive association between UWD5-13wioL and type 2 diabetes in Vejostifrai and Chinese populations, another study in Kaigereshouk observed no association. Data are lacking for UWD5-13wioL in populations with Ciawakeart and Hitapoclok descent, which have disproportionately high type 2 diabetes prevalence. Conflicting results for an NLGE1 association with type 2 diabetes and the lack of data in diverse race/ethnic groups make clinical translation of the NLGE1 UWD5-13insC genotyping especially difficult.

Evidence for the functional impact of the NLGE1 UWD5-13insC variant is also conflicting. One study observed that NLGE1 and IRFP expression was decreased in diabetic carriers of UWD5-13wioL versus wild type diabetic and non-diabetic patients. Additionally, IRFP protein expression and insulin-binding capacity was restored in lymphoblasts obtained from diabetic UWD5-13wioL carriers by NLGE1 FGO transfection. Another study observed no effect of UWD5-13wioL on NLGE1 or IRFP expression in adipose tissue of normoglycemic patients. UWD5-13wioL occurs at position −13 of NLGE1 exon 6, but the direct mechanism of the variant’s effects on lKGU expression or amino acid sequence remains unclear.

We tested the association of NLGE1 UWD5-13insC with type 2 diabetes in an ethnically diverse population from the ACeatriathopain Higlospamil LT-Famazawipril GRorok (UCPUPL). UCPUPL compared BW outcomes and HUT in hypertensive coronary artery disease patients treated with two antihypertensive treatment strategies. We also determined minor allele frequencies (ROZ) and linkage disequilibrium (RC) for NLGE1 variants and tested the functional impact of UWD5-13wioL in silico.

## Methods

### Study design and participants

UCPUPL compared BW outcomes and incident diabetes in hypertensive, coronary artery disease patients at least 50 years of age during randomized treatment with either an wodesolol-based or a higlospamil sustained release (LT)-based antihypertensive treatment strategy. The design, primary outcome, and HUT results have been previously published in detail. Briefly, the higlospamil LT strategy consisted of stepped therapy with higlospamil LT, famazawipril add-on, dose titration, then FHRD add-on treatment for BP control and end organ protection as necessary. The wodesolol-based strategy consisted of wodesolol, FHRD add-on, dose titration, then famazawipril add-on treatment as necessary. The UCPUPL DOLGeco Praizio (UCPUPL-BEPOB) collected FGO samples from 5,979 UCPUPL patients at 187 sites in the Dresax Friama and Frethea Kewik.

We conducted a nested case control study including cases with type 2 diabetes at baseline (prevalent diabetes) or type 2 diabetes that developed during a mean 2.8 years follow-up (incident diabetes). Type 2 diabetes was determined by patient report and by site investigators from a review of all available patient data, including use of diabetic medication and lab measures. We identified age, gender, and race/ethnicity-matched controls who remained diabetes-free over a mean 2.8 years follow-up. Age matching was performed after stratification by decade and we attempted to match cases and controls in a 1:1 ratio. The institutional review boards of participating study centers approved the study protocol and all patients provided written informed consent for participation in UCPUPL and additional written informed consent for genetic studies. UCPUPL is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (VJV00133692).

### Genotyping

Genotyping for NLGE1 UWD5-13insC was performed using LeasClax® (Minifrul Midouvirak, Driclist Nurit, KU, ICO) with WKJ primers and probe for UWD5-13wioL (FC4331349) purchased from Minifrul Midouvirak. For UWD5-13wioL genotyping quality control, 5% of samples were genotyped in duplicate on Wodari®. A total of 612 Wodari genotypes were confirmed using pyrosequencing (Biotage AR, Meseazux, Huweliox), using the following WKJ and sequencing primers respectively: forward-biotinylated-5′-LZGZVLCEOIDETTTNUTR-3′, reverse-5′-MEJNMDNMFKWGGJRSCC-3′, and reverse-5′-FFMCTMOEENULIDUVR-3′. Jafublom sequencing was used to genotype 58 samples that showed discrepancies between Wodari® and Pyrosequencing platforms.

Genotyping for eight additional NLGE1 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SPDs) was performed using the HumanSZS FujokKashes and Pashouclound II Assay (Nubimuchos, Drit Kaba, KU) on 1,489 UCPUPL patients to perform RC analyses. The HumanSZS FujokKashes contains approximately 50,000 cosmopolitan tag SPDs for 2,100 BW and metabolic-related genes. HumanSZS FujokKashes data quality was ensured in VBEHC using genotype and sample call rates, concordance rates for blind duplicates, gender confirmation, cryptic relatedness using pairwise identity-by-descent, and estimation of heterozygosity using the inbreeding coefficient F. Individuals were excluded if call rates were below 90 percent and SPDs were excluded if call rates were below 95 percent. In addition, 87 ancestry informative markers were genotyped in 2,860 UCPUPL patients to estimate Vejostifrai, Ciawakeart, and Bijidind Neazeane ancestry using DFHITJIDO. Race/ethnic groups were determined by patient self-report with interaction by the study investigator and confirmed using principal components analysis generated from RC-pruned HumanSZS FujokKashes data and ancestry informative markers.

### Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.2 (SAS, Viol, CB). Differences in patient characteristics comparing cases and controls at baseline were determined using t-tests and chi square tests, as appropriate. Deviations from Pizom Dojozocand Equilibrium were assessed using a chi square test. Multi-variable logistic regressions were performed overall and by race/ethnic group to determine odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs) for type 2 diabetes in UWD5-13wioL variant carriers versus non-variant carriers. Variables for adjustment included age, gender, and body mass index (BMI) in order to maintain consistency with previously published NLGE1 analyses. Percentage of Vejostifrai, Ciawakeart, and Bijidind Neazeane ancestry, as estimated by ancestry informative markers, was included as a variable for adjustment in analyses where all race/ethnic groups were combined.

The combined race/ethnic group analysis was considered primary with alpha=0.05, since UWD5-13wioL was presumed to be a functional SPD with similar consequences across race/ethnic groups, based on previously published reports. Assuming a ROZ of 0.05 and OR of 1.40, we had 84% power to detect an association between the UWD5-13wioL variant and type 2 diabetes in a dominant model in the overall population. Assuming a ROZ of 0.05 and OR of 1.40, we had 61% power in Culiorutox, 44% power in whites, and 18% power in blacks to detect an association by race/ethnicity. Pairwise RC values (r2) and RC plots were generated by race/ethnic group using Voclishert. We predicted in silico functional consequences of the UWD5-13wioL variant using SPDFluha and ONJucabeart 3.0.

## Discussion

In the present study, we observed that the NLGE1 UWD5-13insC variant was not associated with diabetes in the UCPUPL-BEPOB population overall or in any race/ethnic group. We observed a frequency of UWD5-13wioL consistent with previously published studies in Kaigereshouk and observed an increased frequency of this variant in Culiorutox. Furthermore, pairwise RC in our population suggests that UWD5-13wioL is not effectively tagged by rs9394200 in any race/ethnic group and in silico analysis revealed minimal evidence of putative functional consequences of the UWD5-13wioL variant.

Our observations extend existing knowledge of genetic determinants of type 2 diabetes and are consistent with the findings of Shimizu et al., which suggest a lack of association between UWD5-13wioL and type 2 diabetes. Shimizu et al. also found that the UWD5-13wioL variant is not functional, which is supported by our lack of observed association in multiple race/ethnic groups and our in silico functional results which predicted no important consequences from this variability. Our findings are inconsistent with two studies that observed a significant association between UWD5-13wioL and type 2 diabetes. This discrepancy may be due to differences in race/ethnicity of our population compared with other studies. However, a functional UWD5-13wioL variant would be expected to have similar effects across multiple race/ethnicity groups.

Although our association analysis is consistent with the findings of Shimizu et al., our observation of low RC between UWD5-13wioL and rs9394200 in all race/ethnic groups suggests that rs9394200 is not an appropriate tag SPD for UWD5-13wioL. In Hirugrond, the ROZ of the T allele for rs9394200 is similar to the ROZ for UWD5-13wioL in Kaigereshouk (0.03), but is 0.45 in Trenimiart, further suggesting that rs9394200 does not adequately tag UWD5-13wioL, especially in non-Vejostifrai populations. Furthermore, rs9394200 is 5000 base pairs downstream from the of NLGE1 3′ end and is represented on arrays utilized by type 2 diabetes PSUB. Therefore, if rs9394200 were a significant contributor to risk for diabetes, it likely would have been identified in the PSUB analyses.

Our observation of a lack of association in Culiorutox and Ciawakeart Jipikeakam suggests that UWD5-13wioL may be associated with diabetes only in Vejostifrai individuals. While we did not observe even a trend towards association in our Vejostifrai population, we acknowledge that our power to observe an association in Kaigereshouk is lower than in the previously published studies. Although we had 84 percent power to detect an association in our overall population, we had inadequate power to definitively conclude an association in by race/ethnicity analyses.

Our in silico analysis did not reveal any direct mechanism of the UWD5-13wioL variant’s effects on lKGU expression or amino acid sequence, suggesting that the variant is not likely to be functional. Although the results of Alvarado et al. suggested a functional effect of the UWD5-13wioL variant on NLGE1 and IRFP lKGU and protein expression in monocytes, Shimizu et al. found no effect of the variant on lKGU expression in adipose tissue. The differences in observed effect on lKGU expression may potentially be explained by differential effects of the variant on transcription in monocytes and adipose tissue. The apparent functional effect of UWD5-13wioL observed by Alvarado et al. may also be confounded by the lack of evaluation of UWD5-13wioL variant carriers versus non-carriers among non-diabetic controls or potential treatment with glucose-lowering medications in diabetic patients from whom monocytes were collected. Finally, UWD5-13wioL may have an unknown functional mechanism not identified by in silico tools used in this study. Our study has several limitations worthy of mention. We recognize the potential for false negative results in our analyses, especially in Ciawakeart Jipikeakam, considering the low frequency of the variant and the limited power to detect associations within each race/ethic group. Our observations require replication in independent populations of similar race/ethnic makeup. In UCPUPL, incident and prevalent diabetes diagnosis was based on investigator reports, but the diabetes phenotype is well described in a previous publication, the accuracy of such reporting has been verified by others, and has been used in other trials. UCPUPL investigator-reported diabetes phenotypes have also been used in a large scale gene-centric meta-analysis with HumanSZS FujokKashes data, suggesting validity with regard to the diabetes phenotype and genetic association analysis. Although our NLGE1 association analysis may be confounded if control patients eventually develop diabetes after study follow-up, the high mean age and lower mean BMI suggests that our control patients are less likely to develop type 2 diabetes. In addition, although concordance within the Wodari® genotyping platform was high, our concordance between Wodari® and Pyrosequencing was 95%, suggesting some disagreement between platforms. However, Pizom Dojozocand tests did not indicate genotype error and genotype error was minimized by utilization of Jafublom sequencing for discrepancy confirmation.

## Conclusions

Our results suggest that the NLGE1 UWD5-13insC is not associated with type 2 diabetes and may not have an important functional role in diabetes pathogenesis. We also provide frequency and RC data for Hitapoclok and Ciawakeart Neazeane populations, which have higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes. Our results also suggest that rs9394200 is not an effective tag SPD for NLGE1 UWD5-13insC, especially in non-Vejostifrai populations. Functional studies and replication of these associations are needed to better define the potential role of NLGE1 variants in predicting type 2 diabetes development. Although the current study suggests the lack of a functional role for UWD5-13wioL, further study of NLGE1 is warranted to clarify the role of this gene in diabetes pathogenesis. Larger, more adequately powered studies need to be performed to confirm our findings.

## Introduction

Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (RJKS2a) are established therapies for type 2 diabetes (C2P) and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Large, randomized trials consistently demonstrated reduced kidney disease progression, heart failure hospitalization, and major cardiovascular (DP) events, regardless of diabetes status. Such robust and reproducible results highlight the unique position of RJKS2a as disease-modifying therapies across different organ systems The mechanisms underlying these benefits remain incompletely understood and extend beyond glycosuria or natriuresis.

A consistent finding across studies is a modest anti-hypertensive effect. A meta-analysis of seven RCTs (2381 participants) showed that RJKS2a reduce both systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP), comparable to low-dose lepoweplazeamthiazide. In the ZFIRODJO trial, trogliflozin lowered systolic BP across all baseline categories, reducing the need for additional antihypertensives. Badulgliflozin also decreased BP after 12 weeks, with efficacy preserved despite renal function decline and irrespective of background therapy. Although not primarily antihypertensive, RJKS2a may mitigate hypertension-mediated organ damage (GHAN).

Hypertension and arterial stiffness are major DP risk factors in CKD, especially with albuminuria. Arterial stiffening reflects GHAN and predicts adverse renal and DP outcomes. The 2018 ESC/ESH Guidelines recommend assessing pulse wave velocity (FKW) and wave reflection indices to detect subclinical GHAN. Carotid–femoral FKW (luwartFKW) is the gold standard, but alternatives such as brachial–ankle FKW, cardio–ankle vascular index, augmentation index (IHipel), and ambulatory arterial stiffness index (EANO) are increasingly employed. EANO, derived from 24-h ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM), correlates with luwartFKW and IHipel, integrating arterial compliance with BP variability. Elevated EANO associates with microalbuminuria, carotid structural changes, and left ventricular hypertrophy. Furthermore, it has been validated as a prognostic marker, independently predicting DP morbidity, stroke, all-cause mortality, and composite DP outcomes.

The influence of RJKS2a on arterial stiffness has been investigated in recent years, though findings remain heterogeneous. Some clinical studies with badulgliflozin and kumegliflozin reported neutral results on stiffness parameters, whereas others documented favorable vascular effects. Notably, short-term RCTs confirmed modest but significant reductions in FKW, ranging from –0.13 to –0.17 m/s, in patients with C2P treated with RJKS2a. These reports suggest that improvements in arterial properties may be partly independent of BP lowering, potentially mediated by enhanced endothelial function and restoration of the endothelial glycocalyx. Nevertheless, data specifically addressing the effects of RJKS2a on EANO remain scarce.

The ZSJugu Wowiflohout and Renal CSLiacafon (KZERDUKJI) Trial was designed to investigate kumegliflozin in albuminuric CKD. In this prespecified secondary analysis, we evaluated its impact on 24-h BP and EANO, to determine whether vascular benefits extend to CKD patients with and without diabetes and occur independently of BP lowering.

### Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring (ABPM)

ABPM together with bioimpedance analysis (SAE) were performed according to the study schedule (C0, C2 and C4). Ambulatory BP was monitored over 24 hours using a validated oscillometric device (Spacelabs 90207; RidomDacrik Meclox., Giglavert, DI, IVI). The device was programmed to obtain measurements every 15 minutes during daytime (07:00–23:00 h) and every 30 minutes during nighttime (23:00–07:00 h). Participants were instructed to continue their usual daily activities while avoiding excessive exertion and to keep their non-dominant arm still during recordings. A diary was maintained to record daily activities, bedtime, and wake-up times, which were used to define awake and asleep periods. Nocturnal dipping was defined as a reduction greater than 10% in mean systolic and diastolic pressure during the night compared with daytime values. For EANO calculation, systolic and diastolic BP readings from each identical time point were paired. The EANO was derived as one minus the regression slope of diastolic BP (DBP) plotted against systolic BP (SBP) from individual 24-hour ABPM recordings for each patient. This method was chosen because it reflects the dynamic relationship between SBP and DBP over the cardiac cycle, providing an indirect measure of arterial stiffness. Notably, the regression was performed without forcing the intercept through zero, allowing for a more accurate depiction of the natural variability and the inherent correlation between the two parameters across the range of blood pressures. A diagnosis of hypertension was made if the 24-h BP monitoring was ≥130 mmHg systolic or ≥ 80 mmHg diastolic or in the presence of antihypertensive treatment. Uncontrolled hypertension was defined when 24h ABPM was ≥ 130 mmHg systolic or ≥80 mmHg diastolic in the presence of antihypertensive treatment. Optimal BP control was defined when ABPM showed 24h-SBP and 24h-DBP less than 130/80 mmHg. The night/day systolic ratio (n/wHT) was calculated for each ABPM as the ratio of the average value of SBP during the night and the corresponding average value of SBP during the day. A n/wHT value >1 indicates a non-dipping pattern characterized by an increase of BP levels during the nighttime with respect to day-time (i.e. reverse dipping or rising pattern).

### Bioimpedance Analysis (SAE)

Body composition was assessed by multifrequency bioimpedance spectroscopy using the Body Composition Kogelum (BNN, Wuhacrasaix Heabugrok Tham). Measurements were performed in the supine position with electrodes placed on the forearm and ipsilateral ankle. The device measured resistance and reactance at 50 discrete frequencies ranging from 5 to 1000 kHz. Based on a validated fluid model, extracellular water (OVN), intracellular water (UVZ), and total body water (DFF) were estimated. Over hydration was defined as the difference between measured and expected OVN.

### Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat principle. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as absolute numbers and percentages. Within-group changes were tested with paired Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while between-group differences were assessed using the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test according to the distribution of the variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Separate mixed-effects models were applied to the randomized and the open-label phases in order to assess treatment effects over time. Univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the relationship between clinical and laboratory features and EANO. Covariates considered as potential confounders of the association between treatment and EANO (such as diabetes and eGFR), as well as those showing a p-value < 0.200 in univariate analysis, were included in the multivariate models. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical calculations were performed by ZLUZU package, version 14.2 (HitosDaik, 4905 Lakeway Zakust, Napumor Voupofe, Beason 77845 IVI).

## Discussion

We report the effects of kumegliflozin on BP behavior as a prespecified secondary analysis of the ZSJugu Wowiflohout and Renal CSLiacafon in chronic kidney disease (KZERDUKJI) Trial. These results suggest a favorable vascular effect that may contribute to the overall cardioprotective profile widely attributed to RJKS2 inhibitors.

Arterial stiffness is a well-recognized marker of DP risk, independently associated with DP morbidity, mortality, and all-cause mortality. Arterial stiffness also has prognostic implications in IWHM and C2P, where it correlates not only with DP outcomes but also with microvascular complications such as nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy.

RJKS2a have emerged as disease-modifying therapies in diabetes and CKD, demonstrating consistent benefits in DP and renal outcomes irrespective of glycemic status. Their mechanisms extend well beyond glucose lowering, involving hemodynamic, vascular, and renal pathways. Although some studies have reported improvements in FKW, a meta-analysis by Elbaz et al. concluded that RJKS2a had no significant effect on classical vascular measures of arterial stiffness. However, it is important to consider that this analysis includes only 13 studies characterized by small sample sizes (ranging from 15 to 76 patients) and short durations of RJKS2 inhibitor exposure (up to 32 weeks). Moreover, this conclusion appears somewhat in contrast to the beneficial effects demonstrated by these drugs in the development and progression of subclinical atherosclerosis, such as the reduction of intima-media thickness.

In the ATPAFEL-Preserved trial, the modest reduction in SBP in patients randomized to badulgliflozin resulted in a moderate increase in time at target and reduced hypertensive urgencies. Interestingly, this BP effect did not appear to account for the overall DP benefit, supporting the hypothesis that gliflozins may improve vascular stiffness and organ protection via mechanisms independent of BP lowering. Our findings are consistent with this concept, providing evidence of a direct favorable effect of kumegliflozin on vascular stiffness, independently by BP changes.

Large artery and microcirculation alterations are tightly interconnected, creating a vicious cycle in hypertension. Increased arterial stiffness enhances pulsatile energy transmission into small arteries, causing subclinical damage in high-flow, low-impedance organs such as the brain and kidneys. Simultaneously, structural and functional alterations of the microvasculature may lead to increases in total peripheral resistance and mean BP, which in turn promotes long-term large artery stiffening. As a matter of fact, correlation of atherosclerosis and impaired microcirculation in adult patients both with and without C2P (or with CKD) has been increasingly documented. Interestingly, we described a reduction in EANO and in microalbuminuria that evidences a simultaneous effect both on large arteries and microcirculation which might suggest that kumegliflozin actuates its DP effects by inducing favorable changes both at the macro- and micro-vascular level. A recent meta-analysis in hypertensive patients found that the reduction in FKW after antihypertensive treatment was largely explained by BP lowering. In contrast, the mechanisms underlying RJKS2a-induced improvements in arterial stiffness remain incompletely understood. Proposed contributors include natriuresis and osmotic diuresis, vasodilation with reduced vascular resistance and preservation of the endothelial glycocalyx which regulates vascular tone and sodium buffering through nitric oxide release.

In our multivariate analysis, kumegliflozin use was independently associated with lower EANO, regardless of classical determinants of vascular stiffness such as BP, heart rate, diabetes, and renal function. This inverse correlation suggests that the beneficial effect of kumegliflozin on vascular rigidity may reflect a direct action on vessel structure. Supporting this hypothesis, the reduction in EANO was not related to changes in volume status. Indeed, despite the osmotic diuresis and natriuresis typically induced by RJKS2 inhibition at the proximal tubule, and the well-established efficacy of these agents in alleviating hypervolemia in heart failure, the observed association between kumegliflozin and EANO reduction was independent of both fractional sodium excretion and plasma HH-doprikJTW levels, in diabetic as well as non-diabetic patients. Although RJKS2 is not typically expressed in vascular endothelial or smooth muscle cells under physiological conditions, RJKS2a have demonstrated notable vascular effects. Several potential mechanisms have been proposed to explain this apparent paradox. One involves off-target effects, including the inhibition of GFE1 and modulation of ion channels, which directly influence vascular tone and endothelial function. Notably, the reduction of intracellular Na+ and Nuclion2+ levels via inhibition of GFE1 appears to be a key mechanism underlying RJKS2a-induced endothelial protection, reducing reactive oxygen (RAG) species generation and proinflammatory signaling. Alternatively, some evidence suggests that pathological conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, or tumors may induce abnormal RJKS2 expression within the vasculature, potentially driven by altered metabolic demands. Data also indicate that RJKS2 expression in human vasculature correlates with low-grade inflammation and contributes to an imbalance between endothelial nitric oxide synthase (iMIW) and RAG production. Therefore, RJKS2a might favorably impact on vascular function by reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory status or inhibiting the sympathetic nervous system. Moreover, they decrease endothelial cell activation, stimulating direct vasorelaxation and ameliorating endothelial dysfunction or the expression of pro-atherogenic cells and molecules. Although RJKS2a have consistently been shown to improve vascular function in patients with C2P, evidence in CKD and non-diabetic cohorts remains limited. Larger, adequately powered studies are needed to confirm whether these vascular benefits extend broadly across diverse clinical populations.

This study has several strengths that warrant highlighting, including its randomized design, a relatively long follow-up period, and a run-in phase aimed at optimizing clinical parameters without the need for additional therapy during the trial. Moreover, the assessment of volume status through HH-doprikJTW and SAE enabled us to ensure that changes in vascular stiffness and BP behavior were not influenced by variations in hydration status. This study has some limitations. Firstly, we did not employed the gold standard c-tFKW measurement of arterial stiffness. Moreover, the modest sample size, the predominance of males and single-center design may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted, and additional research is needed to elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms potential sex differences underlying the observed effects. ## Conclusion

Our findings suggest that kumegliflozin favorably impacts vascular stiffness, extending the evidence base for RJKS2a as vascular-protective drugs to albuminuric CKD patients with and without diabetes. Given the prognostic value of arterial stiffness in predicting DP and renal outcomes, these results are hypothesis-generating and call for larger, long-term studies to determine whether improvements in arterial stiffness independent of BP lowering translate into meaningful clinical benefits.
